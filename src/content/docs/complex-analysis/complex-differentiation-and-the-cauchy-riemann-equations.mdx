## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll derive the Cauchy–Riemann equations from the requirement that the derivative be direction-independent, learn exactly when they are sufficient as well as necessary, and use them as a fast test for differentiability.

## The Concept

### The derivative

> $f$ is **differentiable at $z_0$** if
>
> $$
> f'(z_0) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(z_0+h)-f(z_0)}{h}
> $$
>
> exists, with $h$ complex and the limit independent of how $h\to0$.

The differentiation rules you know all hold with the same proofs: sum, product, quotient, chain, and $\frac{d}{dz}z^n = nz^{n-1}$. Differentiability implies continuity, again with the same argument.

What is new is how few functions qualify.

### Deriving the equations

Write $f = u+iv$ and let $h$ approach 0 in two particular ways.

**Along the real axis**, $h = t$ with $t$ real:

$$
f'(z_0) = \lim_{t\to0}\frac{u(x_0+t,y_0)-u(x_0,y_0) + i\big[v(x_0+t,y_0)-v(x_0,y_0)\big]}{t} = u_x + iv_x
$$

**Along the imaginary axis**, $h = it$:

$$
f'(z_0) = \lim_{t\to0}\frac{u(x_0,y_0+t)-u(x_0,y_0)+i\big[v(x_0,y_0+t)-v(x_0,y_0)\big]}{it} = \frac{u_y+iv_y}{i} = v_y - iu_y
$$

If $f$ is differentiable these two must be equal. Matching real and imaginary parts gives the **Cauchy–Riemann equations**:

$$
\boxed{\;u_x = v_y \qquad\text{and}\qquad u_y = -v_x\;}
$$

And the derivative itself has two equivalent formulas:

$$
f'(z) = u_x + iv_x = v_y - iu_y
$$

Note that we used only **two** directions. Every other direction imposes no new condition - that is a small miracle and the reason the theory is workable at all.

### Necessary, and when sufficient

The derivation shows the equations are **necessary**. On their own they are not quite sufficient, and the standard counterexample is unpleasant enough that it is worth knowing the correct statement instead:

> **Sufficiency.** If $u, v$ have continuous first partial derivatives on a neighbourhood of $z_0$ and satisfy the Cauchy–Riemann equations there, then $f$ is differentiable at $z_0$.

In practice this is what you use. Both directions are needed for different purposes: necessity lets you rule functions out with a one-line check, sufficiency lets you rule them in.

### What the equations mean

Three readings, all worth carrying.

**Rigidity.** The equations couple $u$ and $v$ tightly: you cannot choose the real and imaginary parts independently. Given $u$, the function $v$ is determined up to a constant. Half of an analytic function determines the other half.

**No conjugates allowed.** Introduce the operator

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar z} = \frac12\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + i\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)
$$

The Cauchy–Riemann equations are exactly $\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar z} = 0$, which says $f$ depends on $z$ but **not** on $\bar z$. That is the sharpest way to state the whole condition, and it instantly explains why $\bar z$, $|z|^2 = z\bar z$, $\operatorname{Re}z = \frac{z+\bar z}{2}$, and $\operatorname{Im}z$ all fail.

**Geometric.** As a real map $\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$, the derivative of $f$ has Jacobian

$$
\begin{pmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a\end{pmatrix}
$$

after substituting the equations. Matrices of that shape are exactly the rotation-and-scaling matrices. So **complex differentiability means the map is locally a rotation and a uniform scaling**, which is why analytic maps preserve angles. Conjugation's Jacobian is a reflection, which is why it fails.

Also worth noting: the Jacobian determinant is $a^2+b^2 = |f'(z)|^2$, so $|f'|^2$ is the local area magnification factor.

### The harmonic consequence

Differentiate the first equation with respect to $x$ and the second with respect to $y$, then add. Assuming enough smoothness, which we will later prove is automatic,

$$
u_{xx}+u_{yy} = 0
$$

and the same for $v$. So the real and imaginary parts of an analytic function are **harmonic**: they satisfy Laplace's equation. This is the bridge to physics, since Laplace's equation governs steady-state heat, electrostatic potential, and incompressible irrotational flow. It gets a lesson of its own later.

### Polar form of the equations

For functions naturally written in polar coordinates, with $f = u(r,\theta)+iv(r,\theta)$:

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial r} = \frac1r\frac{\partial v}{\partial\theta}, \qquad \frac{\partial v}{\partial r} = -\frac1r\frac{\partial u}{\partial\theta}
$$

These are what you use for $\log z$ and for power functions with non-integer exponents.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Verify $f(z)=z^2$ is differentiable and find $f'$.**

**Solution.** Here $u = x^2-y^2$ and $v = 2xy$, so

$$
u_x = 2x, \quad v_y = 2x, \quad u_y = -2y, \quad v_x = 2y
$$

Both equations hold: $u_x = v_y$ ✓ and $u_y = -v_x$ ✓. The partials are polynomials, hence continuous, so $f$ is differentiable everywhere.

$$
f' = u_x+iv_x = 2x+2iy = 2z
$$

✓ as expected.

**Example 2: Show $f(z)=\bar z$ fails the test.**

**Solution.** $u = x$ and $v = -y$, giving

$$
u_x = 1, \quad v_y = -1, \quad u_y = 0, \quad v_x = 0
$$

The second equation holds, since $0 = -0$. The first does not: $1 \ne -1$, and not at any point.

**Answer:** nowhere differentiable. ∎

**One failed equation is enough**, and here it fails by a constant amount everywhere, so there is not even a single exceptional point.

**Example 3: Where is $f(z) = |z|^2$ differentiable?**

**Solution.** $|z|^2 = x^2+y^2$, so $u = x^2+y^2$ and $v = 0$.

$$
u_x = 2x, \quad v_y = 0, \quad u_y = 2y, \quad v_x = 0
$$

The equations require $2x = 0$ and $2y = 0$, so both hold **only at the origin**. The partials are continuous, so $f$ is differentiable at $z=0$ and nowhere else, with $f'(0) = 0$.

**Differentiable at a single point is useless**, because every strong theorem in this subject needs differentiability on an open set. This is exactly why the word _analytic_ is defined the way it is in the next lesson.

**Example 4: Verify $f(z)=e^z$ is entire.**

**Solution.** $e^z = e^x\cos y + ie^x\sin y$, so $u = e^x\cos y$ and $v = e^x\sin y$. Then

$$
u_x = e^x\cos y, \quad v_y = e^x\cos y, \quad u_y = -e^x\sin y, \quad v_x = e^x\sin y
$$

Both equations hold everywhere, and all partials are continuous. So $e^z$ is differentiable on all of $\mathbb{C}$, and

$$
f' = u_x+iv_x = e^x\cos y + ie^x\sin y = e^z
$$

**The exponential is its own derivative in $\mathbb{C}$ too.** ∎

**Example 5: Find a harmonic conjugate.**

Given $u(x,y) = x^2-y^2+3x$, find $v$ so that $u+iv$ is analytic.

**Solution.** From the first equation, $v_y = u_x = 2x+3$, so integrating in $y$,

$$
v = 2xy+3y+g(x)
$$

for some function $g$ of $x$ alone. From the second equation, $v_x = -u_y = 2y$. But differentiating the expression above gives $v_x = 2y+g'(x)$, so $g'(x)=0$ and $g$ is a constant $C$.

$$
v = 2xy+3y+C
$$

Checking, $f = z^2+3z + iC$. ✓

**The constant is the only freedom**, which is the rigidity from earlier made concrete: $u$ determines $v$ up to an additive constant.

**Example 6: A function satisfying the equations at a point yet not differentiable there.**

**Solution.** Let $f(z) = \frac{\bar z^2}{z}$ for $z \ne 0$ and $f(0)=0$. A computation shows $u$ and $v$ satisfy the Cauchy–Riemann equations at the origin, because all four partials there are 0.

But along the ray $\arg z = \theta$,

$$
\frac{f(z)-f(0)}{z} = \frac{\bar z^2}{z^2} = e^{-4i\theta}
$$

which depends on $\theta$, so the limit does not exist and $f$ is not differentiable at 0.

**This is why the sufficiency statement requires the partials to be continuous in a neighbourhood**, not merely to satisfy the equations at the one point. The partials here are not continuous at 0.

**Example 7: Use the polar equations on $\log z$.**

**Solution.** Take the principal branch $\log z = \ln r + i\theta$, so $u = \ln r$ and $v = \theta$. Then

$$
u_r = \frac1r, \qquad \frac1r v_\theta = \frac1r \cdot 1 = \frac1r \quad ✓
$$

$$
v_r = 0, \qquad -\frac1r u_\theta = 0 \quad ✓
$$

Both hold for $r>0$, so $\log z$ is differentiable away from the origin and away from whatever ray we cut to make $\theta$ single-valued. And

$$
\frac{d}{dz}\log z = \frac1z
$$

∎

## Real-World Applications

**Fluid flow.** For a two-dimensional incompressible irrotational flow, the velocity potential and stream function are precisely a pair $u, v$ satisfying the Cauchy–Riemann equations. The complex function $u+iv$ is the complex potential, and its derivative gives the velocity field directly.

**Electrostatics.** Potential and field lines form the same orthogonal pair. Field-line diagrams in textbooks are level curves of $u$ and $v$ for an analytic function, which is why they always cross at right angles.

**Conformal meshing.** Grid generation for finite-element simulation often uses analytic maps because the Jacobian being a rotation-and-scaling means cells stay well shaped, which keeps the numerics stable.

**Image processing.** The Wirtinger derivative $\partial/\partial\bar z$ is used in complex-valued signal and image processing, where a filter's dependence on $\bar z$ measures how far it is from being analytic.

**Elasticity.** The Kolosov–Muskhelishvili approach solves plane elasticity problems using two analytic functions, turning a system of PDEs into complex function theory. It is standard in fracture mechanics.

**Aerodynamics.** Circulation and lift are computed from a complex potential whose real and imaginary parts satisfy these equations, which is how the Kutta–Joukowski theorem is derived.

:::note[You've Got This]
The formula worth memorizing is short: $u_x = v_y$ and $u_y = -v_x$. But the reading that will serve you better is $\partial f/\partial\bar z = 0$ - an analytic function depends on $z$ and not on $\bar z$. With that in mind you can classify most functions at a glance: anything you can write in terms of $z$ alone is a candidate, and anything needing $\bar z$, $|z|$, $\operatorname{Re}$, or $\operatorname{Im}$ is not. That one heuristic will save you a lot of partial derivatives.
:::
