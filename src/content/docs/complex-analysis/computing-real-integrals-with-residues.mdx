## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn the four standard contour techniques for real integrals: the semicircle for rational functions, the semicircle with Jordan's lemma for oscillatory integrands, the unit circle for trigonometric integrals over a full period, and the keyhole for branch cuts.

## The Concept

### The strategy

Every method in this lesson follows the same four steps.

1. **Choose a contour** that includes the real integral you want as one of its pieces.
2. **Apply the residue theorem** to get the total around the closed contour.
3. **Show the unwanted pieces vanish**, usually by the ML inequality.
4. **Solve** for the piece you wanted.

The art is entirely in step 1. Steps 2 to 4 are mechanical.

### Type 1: rational functions over $\mathbb{R}$

For $\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}dx$ with $q$ having no real zeros and $\deg q \ge \deg p + 2$, use the semicircular contour in the upper half plane:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{p}{q}\,dx = 2\pi i\sum_{\text{poles in UHP}}\operatorname{Res}\frac{p}{q}
$$

<SemicircleContour />

The degree condition is what makes the arc vanish. On $|z|=R$ the integrand is $O(R^{-2})$ while the arc length is $\pi R$, so the ML bound is $O(R^{-1})\to0$. With only one degree of decay the bound would be $O(1)$ and the method fails.

The figure carries out the whole argument for $\int\frac{dx}{1+x^4}$, computing the residues, the numerical value of the real integral, and the ML bound on the arc at two radii. All three agree on $\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}$.

**You could close in the lower half plane instead**, picking up those residues with a sign flip from the reversed orientation. The answer is the same, which is a good consistency check.

### Type 2: oscillatory integrands, with Jordan's lemma

For $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\cos(ax)\,dx$ or with $\sin$, replace the trigonometric factor by $e^{iaz}$ and take the appropriate part at the end. The tool that makes the arc vanish is stronger than ML alone:

> **Jordan's lemma.** If $|f(z)|\to0$ uniformly as $|z|\to\infty$ in the upper half plane, and $a>0$, then
>
> $$
> \int_{\Gamma_R}f(z)e^{iaz}\,dz \to 0
> $$
>
> on the semicircular arc $\Gamma_R$ in the upper half plane.

The reason is that $|e^{iaz}| = e^{-a\operatorname{Im}z}$ is exponentially small over most of the upper arc. This buys you a full degree: only $f\to0$ is needed, rather than $O(R^{-2})$ decay.

**The sign of $a$ decides the half plane.** For $a>0$ close upward, for $a<0$ close downward, because that is where $e^{iaz}$ decays.

### Type 3: trigonometric integrals over a period

For $\int_0^{2\pi}R(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,d\theta$, substitute $z=e^{i\theta}$ so the interval becomes the unit circle:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{z-z^{-1}}{2i}, \qquad d\theta = \frac{dz}{iz}
$$

The integral becomes a contour integral of a rational function around $|z|=1$, evaluated by residues at whichever poles land inside. **A real trigonometric integral becomes rational algebra**, which is usually a large simplification.

### Type 4: branch cuts and the keyhole

Integrals like $\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{a-1}}{1+x}dx$ involve a non-integer power, so the integrand has a branch point at 0. The standard contour is the **keyhole**: out along one side of the positive real axis, around a large circle, back along the other side, and around a small circle at the origin.

Because the two straight pieces sit on opposite sides of the cut, the multi-valued factor differs between them by $e^{2\pi ia}$, and they do **not** cancel. Instead they combine to give a multiple of the integral you want. That deliberate use of the branch jump is the whole trick.

### Principal values and poles on the contour

If the integrand has a pole **on** the real axis, the integral only exists as a Cauchy principal value, and the contour is indented with a small semicircle around the pole. A small semicircle over a simple pole contributes exactly $\pi i$ times the residue — half of the full $2\pi i$, as the half-turn suggests. This is how $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}dx = \frac{\pi}{2}$ is evaluated, and the $\frac12$ in the answer comes from precisely that half-loop.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{1+x^2}$.**

**Solution.** Poles at $\pm i$; only $i$ is in the upper half plane. With $q = 1+z^2$ and $q'=2z$,

$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=i} = \frac{1}{2i}
$$

$$
\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2} = 2\pi i\cdot\frac{1}{2i} = \pi
$$

✓ Consistent with $\arctan x$ evaluated from $-\infty$ to $\infty$.

The degree condition holds since $\deg q = 2 = \deg p + 2$.

**Example 2: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{1+x^4}$.**

**Solution.** The poles are the fourth roots of $-1$, namely $e^{i\pi/4}$, $e^{3i\pi/4}$, $e^{5i\pi/4}$, $e^{7i\pi/4}$. The first two are in the upper half plane.

Since $q = 1+z^4$ has $q'=4z^3$, and $z^4=-1$ at each pole,

$$
\operatorname{Res} = \frac{1}{4z^3} = \frac{z}{4z^4} = \frac{z}{-4} = -\frac{z}{4}
$$

Summing over the two upper poles:

$$
-\frac14\left(e^{i\pi/4}+e^{3i\pi/4}\right) = -\frac14\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}i-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}i\right) = -\frac{i\sqrt2}{4}
$$

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{1+x^4} = 2\pi i\left(-\frac{i\sqrt2}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi\sqrt2}{2} = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}\approx2.2214
$$

∎

**Try this with real methods.** Partial fractions over $\mathbb{R}$ requires factoring $1+x^4$ into two irreducible quadratics with irrational coefficients, and the resulting integrals are unpleasant. The contour method does not care about the degree.

**Example 3: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}dx$.**

**Solution.** Consider $\frac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2}$ on the upper semicircle. Since $a = 1 > 0$, Jordan's lemma applies and the arc vanishes.

The only pole in the upper half plane is $z=i$, with

$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=i}\frac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2} = \frac{e^{i\cdot i}}{2i} = \frac{e^{-1}}{2i}
$$

So

$$
\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{e^{ix}}{1+x^2}dx = 2\pi i\cdot\frac{e^{-1}}{2i} = \frac{\pi}{e}
$$

The result is real, so taking real parts,

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}dx = \frac{\pi}{e}\approx1.1557
$$

∎

And as a bonus, the imaginary part gives $\int\frac{\sin x}{1+x^2}dx = 0$, which is also obvious by oddness. **Two integrals for the price of one** is typical of this method.

**Example 4: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2+\cos\theta}$.**

**Solution.** Substitute $z=e^{i\theta}$, so $\cos\theta = \frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}$ and $d\theta = \frac{dz}{iz}$:

$$
\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{1}{2+\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2}}\cdot\frac{dz}{iz} = \oint\frac{2}{4z+z^2+1}\cdot\frac{dz}{i}
$$

The denominator $z^2+4z+1$ has roots $z=-2\pm\sqrt3$. Only $-2+\sqrt3\approx-0.268$ is inside the unit circle.

$$
\operatorname{Res} = \frac{2}{i}\cdot\frac{1}{2z+4}\bigg|_{z=-2+\sqrt3} = \frac{2}{i}\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt3} = \frac{1}{i\sqrt3}
$$

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2+\cos\theta} = 2\pi i\cdot\frac{1}{i\sqrt3} = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt3}\approx3.6276
$$

∎

**Checking which root is inside is the crucial step**, and it is where most errors in this type occur.

**Example 5: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}dx$.**

**Solution.** Consider $\frac{e^{iz}}{z}$ on a contour indented by a small semicircle above the pole at 0. There are no poles enclosed, so the total is 0.

The large arc vanishes by Jordan's lemma. The small semicircle above a simple pole, traversed clockwise, contributes $-\pi i\operatorname{Res} = -\pi i$.

So the two straight pieces sum to $\pi i$, giving

$$
\text{P.V.}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}}{x}dx = \pi i
$$

Taking imaginary parts, $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}dx = \pi$, and by evenness of $\frac{\sin x}{x}$,

$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}dx = \frac{\pi}{2}
$$

∎

**The $\frac12$ comes from the half-loop around the pole.** This integral, the Dirichlet integral, has no elementary antiderivative at all.

**Example 6: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^3}$.**

**Solution.** The integrand is not even, so a full-line contour will not work directly. Instead use a **sector** contour of angle $\frac{2\pi}{3}$, since $e^{2\pi i/3}$ maps the positive real axis to the other ray and multiplies the integrand in a controlled way.

On the returning ray, substituting $z = e^{2\pi i/3}t$ gives $z^3=t^3$, so the integrand is unchanged while $dz$ picks up a factor $e^{2\pi i/3}$. The sector encloses the single pole $z=e^{i\pi/3}$, with residue $\frac{1}{3z^2}$ evaluated there.

Carrying this out gives

$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^3} = \frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt3}\approx1.2092
$$

∎

**Match the contour to the symmetry of the integrand.** A cube in the denominator suggests a $120°$ sector, just as a fourth power suggests $90°$.

**Example 7: A keyhole integral.**

Set up $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{-1/2}}{1+x}dx$.

**Solution.** The integrand has a branch point at 0 and a pole at $-1$. Use a keyhole around the positive real axis with the branch cut there.

On the lower edge of the cut, $z^{-1/2}$ has picked up a factor $e^{-i\pi}= -1$ relative to the upper edge, so the two straight contributions **add** rather than cancel, giving $2I$ where $I$ is the wanted integral. The residue at $z=-1$, with the branch chosen so $\arg z\in(0,2\pi)$, is $e^{-i\pi/2} = -i$.

$$
2I = 2\pi i(-i) = 2\pi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I = \pi
$$

Verification by real methods: substituting $x=u^2$ turns it into $2\int_0^\infty\frac{du}{1+u^4}$... which is Example 2's cousin, and indeed gives $\pi$. ✓

## Real-World Applications

**Fourier transforms.** Most transforms in a table were computed by contour integration, and the transform of $\frac{1}{1+x^2}$ being $\pi e^{-|k|}$ is Example 3 with a parameter. Every filter design that uses a closed-form transform relies on this.

**Inverse Laplace transforms.** The Bromwich contour integral is evaluated by residues, with each pole contributing an exponential mode. This is the mathematics behind step and impulse responses in control theory.

**Diffraction and scattering.** The Sommerfeld theory of diffraction, and optical propagation integrals, are evaluated by steepest-descent and residue methods along contours in the complex plane.

**Dispersion relations.** The Kramers–Kronig relations connect the real and imaginary parts of a material's response, and they are derived by contour integration with a principal value exactly like Example 5. They are used to check the consistency of measured optical data.

**Statistical mechanics.** Partition functions and their asymptotics are extracted by contour integration around singularities, which is how phase transitions show up as singularity locations.

**Signal processing.** The $\operatorname{sinc}$ function's integral, Example 5, is the reason ideal low-pass filters have the impulse response they do, and its slow decay is why real filters must be windowed.

:::note[You've Got This]
This lesson is where complex analysis pays its way. The workflow never changes: pick a contour containing your integral, apply the residue theorem, kill the extra pieces, solve. Learning it is mostly learning which contour goes with which integrand — semicircle for rational, semicircle plus Jordan for oscillatory, unit circle for a full trig period, sector for symmetry, keyhole for branch points. Once that mapping is in place you can evaluate integrals that have no antiderivative at all, and that is a genuinely new ability rather than a faster old one.
:::
