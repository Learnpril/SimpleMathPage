## The Concept

### The definition

A **contour** is a piecewise smooth curve $\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb{C}$ with an orientation. The integral of $f$ along it is defined by pulling back to a real parameter:

$$
\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz = \int_a^b f\big(\gamma(t)\big)\,\gamma'(t)\,dt
$$

The right-hand side is an ordinary integral of a complex-valued function of a real variable, which you handle by integrating real and imaginary parts separately. So the definition is not exotic — it is a line integral, exactly as in Calculus 3, with complex multiplication in place of a dot product.

The mechanical recipe is short and worth internalizing:

1. Parametrise the contour as $z = \gamma(t)$.
2. Compute $dz = \gamma'(t)\,dt$.
3. Substitute into $f$ and multiply.
4. Integrate over $t$ from $a$ to $b$.

Basic properties, all immediate from the definition:

- **Linearity.** $\int_\gamma (\alpha f+\beta g) = \alpha\int_\gamma f + \beta\int_\gamma g$
- **Reversal.** $\int_{-\gamma}f = -\int_\gamma f$
- **Concatenation.** Splitting a contour splits the integral.
- **Reparametrisation invariance.** Only the curve and its orientation matter, not the choice of parameter.

### Path dependence, and when it goes away

Here is the central question of the lesson: does $\int_\gamma f\,dz$ depend on the path, or only on the endpoints?

<ContourPathCompare />

For a general continuous $f$ it depends on the path. But:

> **Fundamental theorem for contour integrals.** If $F$ is analytic on a domain containing $\gamma$ and $F' = f$, then
>
> $$
> \int_\gamma f(z)\,dz = F\big(\gamma(b)\big) - F\big(\gamma(a)\big)
> $$

So if the integrand has an antiderivative, **only the endpoints matter** and the integral around any closed contour is 0.

That explains both halves of the figure. The integrand $z$ has antiderivative $z^2/2$, so both routes to $1+i$ give the same answer $\frac{(1+i)^2}{2} = i$. The integrand $\bar z$ is analytic nowhere, has no antiderivative, and duly gives different answers on the two routes.

Cauchy's theorem, next lesson, is the statement that analytic functions on a simply connected domain always have this property. This lesson is the groundwork for it.

### The integral that everything rests on

Compute $\oint_C \frac{dz}{z}$ where $C$ is the unit circle traversed counterclockwise. Parametrise $z = e^{it}$ for $t\in[0,2\pi]$, so $dz = ie^{it}dt$:

$$
\oint_C\frac{dz}{z} = \int_0^{2\pi}\frac{ie^{it}}{e^{it}}\,dt = \int_0^{2\pi} i\,dt = 2\pi i
$$

$$
\boxed{\;\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{dz}{z} = 2\pi i\;}
$$

**Not zero.** This is the most important computation in the subject, and the reason is instructive: $1/z$ is analytic on the punctured plane, but the punctured plane is not simply connected, and $1/z$ has no single-valued antiderivative there. Its natural antiderivative is $\log z$, which gains $2\pi i$ each time you loop the origin — exactly the branch-cut behaviour from last lesson.

Every $2\pi i$ you will meet for the rest of the section traces back to this one integral.

By contrast $\oint_C z^n\,dz = 0$ for every integer $n \ne -1$, because $z^{n+1}/(n+1)$ is then a genuine single-valued antiderivative on a neighbourhood of the circle. **The exponent $-1$ is the unique exception**, which is why residues are about the coefficient of $\frac{1}{z-z_0}$ and nothing else.

### The ML inequality

Often you need to show an integral is small without evaluating it. The tool is:

> If $|f(z)|\le M$ for all $z$ on $\gamma$, and $\gamma$ has length $L$, then
>
> $$
> \left|\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz\right| \le ML
> $$

Sometimes called the estimation lemma. It is the workhorse for showing that a large semicircular arc contributes nothing in the limit, which is the step that makes real integrals computable by contour methods later.

For a circle of radius $R$ the length is $2\pi R$, so the bound is $2\pi R M$. If $|f|$ decays faster than $1/R$, the contribution vanishes as $R\to\infty$.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Evaluate $\int_\gamma z\,dz$ along the straight line from 0 to $1+i$.**

**Solution.** Parametrise $\gamma(t) = t(1+i)$ for $t\in[0,1]$, so $\gamma'(t) = 1+i$:

$$
\int_0^1 t(1+i)\cdot(1+i)\,dt = (1+i)^2\int_0^1 t\,dt = \frac{(1+i)^2}{2} = \frac{2i}{2} = i
$$

∎

Or immediately: $z$ has antiderivative $z^2/2$, so the answer is $\frac{(1+i)^2}{2} - 0 = i$. **When an antiderivative exists, use it.**

**Example 2: Same integrand, the L-shaped path.**

**Solution.** First leg along the real axis, $\gamma_1(t)=t$ for $t\in[0,1]$:

$$
\int_0^1 t\,dt = \frac12
$$

Second leg upward, $\gamma_2(t) = 1+it$ for $t\in[0,1]$, with $dz = i\,dt$:

$$
\int_0^1 (1+it)\,i\,dt = i\int_0^1(1+it)\,dt = i\left(1+\frac i2\right) = i - \frac12
$$

Total: $\frac12 + i - \frac12 = i$. ✓ **Same as Example 1**, as the antiderivative guaranteed.

**Example 3: $\int_\gamma \bar z\,dz$ on both paths.**

**Solution.** Straight line, $\gamma(t)=t(1+i)$, $\bar\gamma(t) = t(1-i)$:

$$
\int_0^1 t(1-i)(1+i)\,dt = (1-i)(1+i)\cdot\frac12 = \frac{2}{2} = 1
$$

L-shaped path. First leg: $\int_0^1 t\,dt = \frac12$. Second leg, $z = 1+it$ so $\bar z = 1-it$:

$$
\int_0^1(1-it)i\,dt = i\left(1-\frac i2\right) = i+\frac12
$$

Total: $\frac12 + i + \frac12 = 1+i$.

$$
1 \ne 1+i
$$

**Path dependent.** ∎ And necessarily so: $\bar z$ is nowhere analytic, so it has no antiderivative.

**Example 4: Evaluate $\oint \frac{dz}{z}$ on the circle $|z|=3$.**

**Solution.** Parametrise $z = 3e^{it}$, $dz = 3ie^{it}dt$:

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{3ie^{it}}{3e^{it}}\,dt = \int_0^{2\pi}i\,dt = 2\pi i
$$

∎

**The radius cancelled.** Any counterclockwise circle around the origin gives $2\pi i$, and later we will see any counterclockwise closed contour enclosing the origin once does too.

**Example 5: Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=1} z^2\,dz$.**

**Solution.** $z^2$ is entire with antiderivative $z^3/3$, so around a closed contour the integral is 0.

Checking directly with $z=e^{it}$:

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}e^{2it}\cdot ie^{it}\,dt = i\int_0^{2\pi}e^{3it}\,dt = i\left[\frac{e^{3it}}{3i}\right]_0^{2\pi} = 0
$$

✓

**Example 6: Use the ML inequality.**

Show that $\displaystyle\left|\int_{C_R}\frac{dz}{z^2+1}\right| \to 0$ as $R\to\infty$, where $C_R$ is the semicircle $|z|=R$ in the upper half plane.

**Solution.** On the contour, $|z| = R$, so by the reverse triangle inequality

$$
|z^2+1| \ge |z|^2-1 = R^2-1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left|\frac{1}{z^2+1}\right| \le \frac{1}{R^2-1} = M
$$

The semicircle has length $L = \pi R$. So

$$
\left|\int_{C_R}\frac{dz}{z^2+1}\right| \le \frac{\pi R}{R^2-1} \to 0
$$

∎

**This is the standard closing argument.** It is exactly the step that lets a real integral over $(-\infty,\infty)$ be replaced by a closed contour, which is how residues evaluate real integrals.

**Example 7: An integral that is not zero despite an analytic integrand.**

Explain why $\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{dz}{z} = 2\pi i$ does not contradict the fundamental theorem.

**Solution.** The integrand is analytic on $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$, so it is analytic at every point of the contour. But the theorem needs an antiderivative on a domain containing the contour, and $1/z$ has none on any annulus around 0: its antiderivative $\log z$ is multi-valued there, gaining $2\pi i$ per loop.

$$
\text{no single-valued antiderivative} \implies \text{no vanishing}
$$

∎

**The obstruction is topological, not analytic.** The punctured plane has a hole, and that hole is the entire source of the residue calculus.

## Real-World Applications

**Circulation and flux.** For a fluid flow with complex potential $\Phi$, the contour integral $\oint \Phi'(z)\,dz$ has real part the circulation and imaginary part the flux through the curve. The Kutta–Joukowski lift formula is a contour integral of exactly this kind.

**Ampère's law.** Computing the magnetic field around a wire is a closed line integral, and the two-dimensional case is literally $\oint \frac{dz}{z}$ with the current at the origin. The nonzero answer is the physical statement that the field circulates.

**Inverse Laplace transforms.** Recovering a time-domain signal from its transform is a contour integral, the Bromwich integral, evaluated by residues. Every step response computed symbolically goes through this.

**Winding numbers in robotics and vision.** Counting how many times a path loops a point is $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{dz}{z-z_0}$, and it is used for point-in-polygon tests and for topological path planning.

**Numerical differentiation.** Cauchy's integral formula turns derivatives into contour integrals, and evaluating those integrals with the trapezoid rule on a circle gives derivative estimates that are far more accurate than finite differences.

**Phase unwrapping.** In interferometry and synthetic-aperture radar, accumulated phase around a loop is a contour integral whose nonzero value signals a defect or a residue in the data. The terminology is borrowed directly from this subject.

:::note[You've Got This]
One computation from this lesson is worth memorizing outright: $\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{dz}{z} = 2\pi i$, and the reason it is not zero. Everything else here is bookkeeping — parametrise, multiply by $\gamma'$, integrate — but that single result is the seed of Cauchy's integral formula, Laurent series, and the residue theorem. And keep the ML inequality nearby; it looks like a throwaway lemma and it turns out to be the tool that makes half the applications work.
:::
