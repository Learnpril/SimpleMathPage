## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn what makes a map conformal, work with Möbius transformations and their circle-preserving property, meet the Riemann mapping theorem, and see how a change of shape solves boundary-value problems.

## The Concept

### Conformal means angle-preserving

> A map is **conformal** at $z_0$ if it preserves angles between curves through $z_0$, including their sense of rotation.

> An analytic function is conformal exactly where $f'(z_0)\ne0$.

The reason is the local picture from lesson 5. Near $z_0$,

$$
f(z) \approx f(z_0) + f'(z_0)(z-z_0)
$$

so to first order the map is multiplication by the fixed complex number $f'(z_0)$: a **rotation** by $\arg f'(z_0)$ and a **scaling** by $|f'(z_0)|$. Both operations leave angles alone.

Where $f'(z_0)=0$ conformality fails. For $f(z)=z^2$ at the origin, angles are doubled; in general, a zero of order $m$ in $f'$ multiplies angles by $m+1$. Such points are called **critical points**, and a conformal map must avoid them.

Note the scaling factor $|f'|$ varies from point to point, so shapes are preserved only **infinitesimally**. A large square does not stay square; a tiny one does.

### Möbius transformations

The most useful family:

$$
w = \frac{az+b}{cz+d}, \qquad ad-bc\ne0
$$

Their properties are worth knowing in full because they do most of the work in applications.

- **Bijections of $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$.** Every Möbius map is invertible on the Riemann sphere, with inverse $z = \frac{dw-b}{-cw+a}$.
- **Group under composition**, isomorphic to $PGL_2(\mathbb{C})$. Composing two gives another.
- **Built from four elementary pieces**: translation, rotation, scaling, and inversion. Every Möbius map factors into those.
- **Conformal everywhere** on $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$, since $w' = \frac{ad-bc}{(cz+d)^2}$ never vanishes.
- **Circles go to circles**, where a line counts as a circle through $\infty$. This is the single most useful property.
- **Three points determine the map.** Given three distinct source points and three distinct targets, exactly one Möbius transformation does the job.

That last fact is the practical construction tool: to map one region to another, pick three boundary points and their images, and the map is forced.

### The Riemann mapping theorem

> **Riemann mapping theorem.** Any simply connected domain in $\mathbb{C}$ that is not all of $\mathbb{C}$ can be mapped conformally and bijectively onto the unit disc.

This is one of the most remarkable existence theorems in mathematics. **Any** simply connected region — a square, a half plane, the interior of a fractal-looking curve, the complement of a slit — is conformally the same as a disc.

Two caveats keep it honest. It is an **existence** theorem: it gives no formula, and finding an explicit map for a given region can be very hard or practically impossible. And the excluded case $\mathbb{C}$ itself is genuinely excluded, since a bijection from $\mathbb{C}$ onto the disc would be a bounded entire function, hence constant by Liouville.

Multiply connected regions are not covered, and cannot be: an annulus is conformally equivalent only to annuli with the same ratio of radii, which is a genuine invariant.

### The standard maps

Worth having memorized, since most problems reduce to composing these:

| Map                         | Effect                                                  |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| $w = az+b$                  | translate, rotate, scale                                |
| $w = 1/z$                   | invert; exchanges inside and outside of the unit circle |
| $w = \frac{z-i}{z+i}$       | upper half plane $\to$ unit disc                        |
| $w = z^2$                   | opens a quarter plane to a half plane                   |
| $w = e^z$                   | horizontal strip of height $\pi$ $\to$ upper half plane |
| $w = \operatorname{Log}z$   | upper half plane $\to$ horizontal strip                 |
| $w = z+\frac1z$             | circle $\to$ segment; the Joukowski map                 |
| $w = \frac{z-a}{1-\bar az}$ | disc $\to$ disc, moving $a$ to the centre               |

The strategy for a hard region is to compose: get to a half plane, then to a disc.

### Why it solves physics problems

Here is the payoff. If $u$ is harmonic — satisfying $\nabla^2u=0$ — and $f$ is conformal, then $u\circ f$ is harmonic too. **Laplace's equation is preserved by conformal maps.**

So to solve a boundary-value problem on an awkward region $D$:

1. Find a conformal map $f$ from $D$ to the unit disc.
2. Transport the boundary data along $f$.
3. Solve on the disc, where the answer is a standard formula.
4. Pull the solution back through $f^{-1}$.

Since Laplace's equation governs steady-state temperature, electrostatic potential, and incompressible irrotational flow, this single technique addresses all three. Before numerical methods it was the _only_ way to handle non-trivial geometry, and it is still how the theory is organized.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Where is $f(z)=z^2+z$ conformal?**

**Solution.** $f'(z) = 2z+1$, which vanishes at $z=-\frac12$.

$$
\text{conformal on } \mathbb{C}\setminus\{-\tfrac12\}
$$

At $z=-\frac12$ the derivative has a simple zero, so angles there are doubled. ∎

**Example 2: Map the upper half plane to the unit disc.**

**Solution.** Use $w = \frac{z-i}{z+i}$. Verify with the three-point method:

$$
i\mapsto\frac{0}{2i}=0, \qquad 0\mapsto\frac{-i}{i}=-1, \qquad \infty\mapsto1
$$

The real axis passes through 0 and $\infty$, so its image is a circle through $-1$ and 1. Testing $z=1$ gives $w = \frac{1-i}{1+i} = -i$, which is on the unit circle, so the image circle is $|w|=1$.

Since $i$, an interior point, maps to the centre, the interior goes to the interior. ✓

**Example 3: Map the first quadrant to the upper half plane.**

**Solution.** The first quadrant is the sector $0<\arg z<\frac\pi2$. Squaring doubles arguments:

$$
w = z^2
$$

gives $0<\arg w<\pi$, the upper half plane. ∎

Composing with Example 2 sends the first quadrant to the disc via $w = \frac{z^2-i}{z^2+i}$. **Composition is how every non-elementary region gets handled.**

**Example 4: Map the strip $0<\operatorname{Im}z<\pi$ to the unit disc.**

**Solution.** Two steps. First $\zeta = e^z$ takes the strip to the upper half plane, from the mapping lesson. Then $w = \frac{\zeta-i}{\zeta+i}$ takes that to the disc.

$$
w = \frac{e^z-i}{e^z+i}
$$

∎

**Example 5: Build the Möbius map sending $1\mapsto0$, $i\mapsto1$, $-1\mapsto\infty$.**

**Solution.** Reading off the requirements: a zero at $z=1$ means a factor $(z-1)$ on top; a pole at $z=-1$ means $(z+1)$ on the bottom. So

$$
w = k\cdot\frac{z-1}{z+1}
$$

Fix $k$ using $i\mapsto1$:

$$
1 = k\cdot\frac{i-1}{i+1}
$$

Now $\frac{i-1}{i+1} = \frac{(i-1)(1-i)}{(1+i)(1-i)} = \frac{i-i^2-1+i}{2} = \frac{2i}{2}=i$, so $k\cdot i = 1$ and $k = -i$.

$$
w = -i\cdot\frac{z-1}{z+1}
$$

∎

**Zeros and poles are read straight off the requirements**, which makes these constructions quick once you see the pattern.

**Example 6: Verify that a conformal map preserves harmonicity.**

**Solution.** Let $u$ be harmonic on $D'$ and $f:D\to D'$ analytic. Locally $u = \operatorname{Re}F$ for some analytic $F$ on $D'$, since a harmonic function has a harmonic conjugate on a simply connected region.

Then $F\circ f$ is analytic on $D$ as a composition of analytic functions, and

$$
u\circ f = \operatorname{Re}(F\circ f)
$$

is the real part of an analytic function, hence harmonic. ∎

**The proof needs no computation with second derivatives.** Harmonic means "real part of analytic," and analyticity is obviously preserved by composition.

**Example 7: The Joukowski map.**

Describe what $w = z+\frac1z$ does to the unit circle.

**Solution.** On $|z|=1$ write $z=e^{i\theta}$:

$$
w = e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta} = 2\cos\theta
$$

which is **real** and ranges over $[-2,2]$. So the unit circle collapses onto the segment $[-2,2]$, traversed twice.

The map has critical points where $w' = 1-\frac{1}{z^2} = 0$, that is $z=\pm1$, and those map to $w=\pm2$, the endpoints of the segment. **The corners of the image are exactly the images of the critical points.**

Applying the same map to a circle slightly offset from the unit circle produces a smooth curve with one sharp corner: an aerofoil. That is how the map earns its place in aerodynamics.

## Real-World Applications

**Aerofoil theory.** The Joukowski map turns the solvable flow around a cylinder into the flow around a wing, and the resulting Kutta–Joukowski theorem gives lift in closed form. It is the historical foundation of aerodynamics.

**Electrostatics and capacitance.** Field configurations for awkward electrode shapes are computed by mapping to a parallel-plate or coaxial geometry. Handbook formulas for capacitance of odd-shaped conductors were obtained this way.

**Heat sink and cooling design.** Steady temperature distributions in fins and irregular cross-sections were computed by conformal mapping long before finite-element analysis, and the mapped solutions remain useful as benchmarks.

**Mesh generation.** Structured grids for computational fluid dynamics are often built by conformal maps, because the rotation-and-scaling property keeps cells well shaped and the numerics stable.

**Map projections.** The Mercator projection is conformal, which is why straight lines on it are constant-bearing courses. Stereographic projection is conformal too, and is used in crystallography for pole figures.

**Medical imaging and brain mapping.** Conformal flattening of curved cortical surfaces to a disc lets anatomical data from different subjects be compared in a common coordinate system, and the angle preservation is what keeps local structure recognizable.

:::note[You've Got This]
Two things carry the weight here. First, conformal means locally rotate-and-scale, which is why $f'\ne0$ is the condition — this is the geometry of complex differentiability from lesson 5 finally cashed out. Second, Möbius transformations plus a handful of standard maps, composed, handle most concrete regions you will meet, and the Riemann mapping theorem promises that a map always exists even when you cannot write it down. If a problem's region looks hopeless, the move is always the same: get to a half plane, then to a disc.
:::
