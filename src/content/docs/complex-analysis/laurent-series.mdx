## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll extend power series to allow negative exponents, learn that the region of validity is an annulus rather than a disc, see that one function can have several different Laurent series, and meet the coefficient $c_{-1}$ that the rest of the section revolves around.

## The Concept

### The idea

Taylor series need the function to be analytic on a whole disc. That rules out any expansion around a singularity, which is exactly where the interesting behaviour is. Laurent's fix is to allow negative powers and to shrink the region to an annulus that excludes the bad point.

> **Laurent's theorem.** If $f$ is analytic on the annulus $r < |z-z_0| < R$, then
>
> $$
> f(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n(z-z_0)^n
> $$
>
> converging on that annulus, with
>
> $$
> c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}\,dz
> $$
>
> for any circle $C$ in the annulus. The expansion is unique.

Notice the coefficient formula is the same one as in Cauchy's integral formula for derivatives, now allowed to run over negative $n$ as well. For $n \ge 0$ and $f$ analytic on the full disc, $c_n = f^{(n)}(z_0)/n!$ and the negative coefficients all vanish, recovering the Taylor series. **Laurent series generalize Taylor series rather than replacing them.**

The two halves have names:

- $\sum_{n\ge0}c_n(z-z_0)^n$ - the **analytic part**, converging for $|z-z_0|<R$
- $\sum_{n<0}c_n(z-z_0)^n$ - the **principal part**, converging for $|z-z_0|>r$

Both conditions together give the annulus. The principal part is the new object and it is what encodes the singularity.

### One function, several series

Because the region is an annulus and a function can have several, the expansion is not unique to the function - only to the function **together with the annulus**.

<LaurentAnnulus />

Take $f(z) = \frac{1}{(z-1)(z-2)}$ expanded about 0. The poles at 1 and 2 cut the plane into three regions, and each gets its own series:

- $|z|<1$: analytic here, so an ordinary Taylor series with only non-negative powers.
- $1<|z|<2$: negative powers from the pole at 1, positive powers from the pole at 2.
- $|z|>2$: both poles are "inside," so only negative powers appear.

The figure computes the middle annulus's coefficients directly from the integral formula on the circle $|z|=1.5$. Using partial fractions, that series should be

$$
f(z) = -\sum_{k\ge1}z^{-k} - \sum_{k\ge0}\frac{z^k}{2^{k+1}}
$$

so $c_{-1}=c_{-2}=\cdots=-1$ and $c_k = -1/2^{k+1}$ for $k \ge 0$, giving $c_0=-0.5$ and $c_1=-0.25$. Those are the numbers the figure measures.

**Asking "what is the Laurent series of $f$?" is incomplete.** You must say where.

### How to actually compute one

Almost never from the integral. The three usable techniques:

**Partial fractions plus geometric series.** Split into terms $\frac{A}{z-a}$, then expand each in the form appropriate to the region. Inside, write $\frac{1}{z-a} = -\frac1a\cdot\frac{1}{1-z/a}$ and expand in $z/a$. Outside, write $\frac{1}{z-a} = \frac1z\cdot\frac{1}{1-a/z}$ and expand in $a/z$. **Which factor you pull out is the whole decision**, and it is decided by which quantity is less than 1 in your region.

**Multiply a known series by a power.** For $\frac{\sin z}{z^4}$, take the series for $\sin z$ and divide term by term.

**Substitute into a known series.** For $e^{1/z}$, put $1/z$ into the exponential series to get $\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^{-n}}{n!}$, an infinite principal part.

### Why $c_{-1}$ is special

Integrate the series term by term around a circle in the annulus. From lesson 8, $\oint (z-z_0)^n dz = 0$ for every integer $n$ except $n=-1$, where it is $2\pi i$. So every term dies except one:

$$
\oint_C f(z)\,dz = 2\pi i\,c_{-1}
$$

**That is the residue theorem in one line.** The number $c_{-1}$ gets the name **residue** of $f$ at $z_0$, and computing contour integrals reduces entirely to finding it. Everything in the next three lessons is about extracting $c_{-1}$ efficiently.

### Classifying singularities by the principal part

The principal part also tells you what kind of singularity you have, which is the subject of the next lesson:

- **No principal part** - removable singularity.
- **Finitely many terms**, lowest power $(z-z_0)^{-m}$ - pole of order $m$.
- **Infinitely many terms** - essential singularity.

This is the cleanest of the several equivalent definitions, and it is why Laurent series come before the classification.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Find the Laurent series of $\frac{e^z}{z^3}$ about 0.**

**Solution.** Divide the exponential series by $z^3$:

$$
\frac{1}{z^3}\left(1+z+\frac{z^2}{2}+\frac{z^3}{6}+\frac{z^4}{24}+\cdots\right)
$$

$$
= \frac{1}{z^3}+\frac{1}{z^2}+\frac{1}{2z}+\frac16+\frac{z}{24}+\cdots
$$

Valid on $0<|z|<\infty$. The principal part has three terms, so this is a **pole of order 3**, and

$$
\operatorname{Res} = c_{-1} = \frac12
$$

∎

**Example 2: Find the Laurent series of $e^{1/z}$ about 0.**

**Solution.** Substitute $1/z$ into the exponential series:

$$
e^{1/z} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!z^n} = 1+\frac1z+\frac{1}{2z^2}+\frac{1}{6z^3}+\cdots
$$

Infinitely many negative powers, so this is an **essential singularity**, and $c_{-1}=1$. ∎

**Example 3: Expand $\frac{1}{(z-1)(z-2)}$ on $|z|<1$.**

**Solution.** Partial fractions:

$$
\frac{1}{(z-1)(z-2)} = \frac{-1}{z-1}+\frac{1}{z-2}
$$

Here $|z|<1<2$, so expand both in powers of $z$:

$$
\frac{-1}{z-1} = \frac{1}{1-z} = \sum_{n\ge0}z^n
$$

$$
\frac{1}{z-2} = \frac{-1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{1-z/2} = -\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}
$$

$$
f(z) = \sum_{n\ge0}\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\right)z^n = \frac12 + \frac34z+\frac78z^2+\cdots
$$

No negative powers, as expected on a disc where $f$ is analytic. ∎

**Example 4: Same function on $1<|z|<2$.**

**Solution.** Now $|1/z|<1$ and $|z/2|<1$, so pull out different factors:

$$
\frac{-1}{z-1} = \frac{-1}{z}\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/z} = -\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{1}{z^{n+1}}
$$

$$
\frac{1}{z-2} = -\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}} \quad\text{(unchanged)}
$$

$$
f(z) = -\sum_{n\ge1}z^{-n} - \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}
$$

So $c_{-1}=-1$, $c_0=-\frac12$, $c_1=-\frac14$, matching the figure's measured values. ✓

**Same function, same centre, completely different series.**

**Example 5: Same function on $|z|>2$.**

**Solution.** Now both $|1/z|<1$ and $|2/z|<1$:

$$
\frac{-1}{z-1} = -\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{1}{z^{n+1}}, \qquad \frac{1}{z-2} = \frac1z\cdot\frac{1}{1-2/z} = \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{2^n}{z^{n+1}}
$$

$$
f(z) = \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{2^n-1}{z^{n+1}} = \frac{0}{z}+\frac{1}{z^2}+\frac{3}{z^3}+\cdots
$$

Note $c_{-1}=0$ here, which is consistent: a large circle encloses both poles, whose residues $-1$ and $+1$ cancel, exactly as computed in the Cauchy's theorem lesson. ✓

**Example 6: Find the residue of $\frac{\sin z}{z^4}$ at 0.**

**Solution.**

$$
\frac{1}{z^4}\left(z-\frac{z^3}{6}+\frac{z^5}{120}-\cdots\right) = \frac{1}{z^3}-\frac{1}{6z}+\frac{z}{120}-\cdots
$$

The coefficient of $\frac1z$ is $-\frac16$, so

$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}\frac{\sin z}{z^4} = -\frac16
$$

∎

Note the pole has order 3, not 4, because $\sin z$ contributes a zero of order 1 at the origin.

**Example 7: Evaluate a contour integral from the series.**

Compute $\displaystyle\oint_{|z|=1}e^{1/z}\,dz$.

**Solution.** From Example 2, $c_{-1}=1$. Integrating the series term by term, only that coefficient survives:

$$
\oint_{|z|=1}e^{1/z}\,dz = 2\pi i\cdot1 = 2\pi i
$$

∎

**An essential singularity is no obstacle to integration.** You do not need to understand the singularity, only to read off one coefficient - which is the practical power of Laurent series.

## Real-World Applications

**System response near a resonance.** Expanding a transfer function in a Laurent series about a pole separates the resonant behaviour, which is the principal part, from the smooth background. Engineers use the leading coefficient to characterize a resonance's strength.

**Partial fraction expansion in circuit analysis.** Decomposing a transfer function into pole terms is the first step in inverting a Laplace transform, and each term is a one-term Laurent series about its pole.

**Asymptotic expansions.** Behaviour of a function for large argument is a Laurent series in $1/z$ about infinity, which is how the growth of special functions and the tails of distributions are described.

**Multipole expansions.** In electrostatics and gravitation, the field far from a charge distribution is expanded in inverse powers of distance, with monopole, dipole and quadrupole terms. It is a Laurent series with physical names for the coefficients.

**Digital filter design.** The $z$-transform of a causal filter is a Laurent series, and the region of convergence - an annulus - determines whether the filter is stable and causal. Signal processing texts state stability directly in terms of that annulus.

**Perturbation near a singular limit.** Problems with a small parameter appearing in a denominator are handled by Laurent expansion in that parameter, standard in fluid dynamics and in quantum field theory regularization.

:::note[You've Got This]
Two things to take from this lesson. First, a Laurent series belongs to a function **and an annulus**, so always say which region you are in; the three-region example is worth working through by hand once. Second, and more important: $\oint f\,dz = 2\pi i\,c_{-1}$. Every contour integral in the rest of the section is an exercise in finding one coefficient. That is a remarkable reduction, and it came from the fact that $z^{-1}$ is the only power whose loop integral survives.
:::
