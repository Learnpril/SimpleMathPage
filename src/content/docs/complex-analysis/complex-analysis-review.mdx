## The Complex Plane

Arithmetic in polar form is the working skill:

$$
z_1z_2 = r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}, \qquad z^n = r^ne^{in\theta}
$$

**Multiply the moduli, add the arguments.** Multiplication by $i$ is a quarter turn.

- $|z_1z_2| = |z_1||z_2|$, but $|z_1+z_2|$ has no formula, only the triangle inequality.
- $z\bar z = |z|^2$, which is how division is done.
- $z^n = w$ has exactly $n$ solutions, forming a regular $n$-gon.
- The $n$th roots of unity sum to 0 and form a cyclic group of order $n$.

Set descriptions: $|z-a|=r$ is a circle, $|z-a|<r$ a disc, $r_1<|z-a|<r_2$ an annulus, $\alpha<\operatorname{Arg}z<\beta$ a sector.

## Analytic Functions

### The condition

Complex differentiability requires the difference quotient's limit to be the same from **every** direction. That forces the Cauchy–Riemann equations, and geometrically it forces the map to be locally a rotation and a uniform scaling.

Fails everywhere: $\bar z$, $|z|$, $\operatorname{Re}z$, $\operatorname{Im}z$. Fails except at one point, hence analytic nowhere: $|z|^2$.

**Analytic** means differentiable on an **open set**, not at a point. Everything below needs the open set.

### What analyticity gives

- Infinite differentiability, and each derivative analytic
- Equality with the Taylor series on any disc in the domain
- The identity theorem: agreement on a set with a limit point forces global agreement
- The maximum modulus principle: no interior maximum of $|f|$
- Liouville: bounded plus entire implies constant
- Open mapping, and conformality where $f'\ne0$
- $u$ and $v$ harmonic, with orthogonal level curves

### Elementary functions

$$
e^z = e^x(\cos y+i\sin y), \qquad e^{z+2\pi i}=e^z, \qquad e^z \ne 0
$$

Periodicity is the source of everything awkward. It makes $\log$ multi-valued:

$$
\log z = \ln|z|+i(\operatorname{Arg}z+2\pi k), \qquad \operatorname{Log}(-1)=i\pi
$$

which needs a **branch cut**, conventionally the negative real axis. $\operatorname{Log}(z_1z_2) = \operatorname{Log}z_1+\operatorname{Log}z_2$ can fail by $2\pi i$.

$\sin z$ and $\cos z$ are entire and **unbounded**, since $\sin(iy)=i\sinh y$; Liouville requires it. Their zeros stay real. Powers $z^a = e^{a\log z}$ give one value for integer $a$, $q$ values for $a=p/q$, and infinitely many otherwise; $i^i = e^{-\pi/2-2\pi k}$ is always real.

## Integration

$$
\int_\gamma f\,dz = \int_a^b f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)\,dt
$$

The one computation to memorize:

$$
\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{dz}{z} = 2\pi i, \qquad \oint_{|z|=1}z^n\,dz = 0 \ (n\ne-1)
$$

The exponent $-1$ being the sole exception is why residues exist.

**ML inequality:** $\left|\int_\gamma f\right|\le ML$. Used to kill large arcs.

### The Cauchy theorems

| Theorem             | Statement                                                             |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cauchy's theorem    | $f$ analytic on a simply connected domain $\Rightarrow \oint_C f = 0$ |
| Deformation         | Reshaping a contour changes nothing unless it crosses a singularity   |
| Multiply connected  | An outer contour equals the sum of contours around the holes          |
| Integral formula    | $f(z_0) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{z-z_0}dz$                |
| Derivative formula  | $f^{(n)}(z_0) = \frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}dz$ |
| Mean value property | $f(z_0)$ is the average of $f$ over any circle about it               |
| Morera              | Vanishing closed integrals plus continuity implies analytic           |

Cauchy's theorem is the Cauchy–Riemann equations integrated, via Green's theorem. The integral formula is where infinite differentiability comes from, and it gives Liouville, then the fundamental theorem of algebra, in a few lines each.

## Series

**Taylor.** Analytic on a disc means equal to the Taylor series there. Over $\mathbb{C}$, _analytic_ and _locally a power series_ coincide, unlike over $\mathbb{R}$.

$$
R = \text{distance from the centre to the nearest singularity}
$$

**Laurent.** On an annulus $r<|z-z_0|<R$,

$$
f(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n(z-z_0)^n
$$

unique for that annulus. One function can have several Laurent series about the same centre, one per annulus of analyticity. Compute them with partial fractions plus geometric series, deciding which factor to pull out by which ratio has modulus below 1.

The consequence that matters:

$$
\oint_C f\,dz = 2\pi i\,c_{-1}
$$

## Singularities and Residues

| Type              | Limit behaviour                        | Principal part |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| Removable         | $\lim f$ exists (boundedness suffices) | empty          |
| Pole of order $m$ | $\lvert f\rvert\to\infty$              | $m$ terms      |
| Essential         | no limit at all                        | infinite       |

For $f=p/q$ with zero orders $a$ and $b$: removable if $a\ge b$, pole of order $b-a$ if $a<b$. **Cancel before classifying.**

Near an essential singularity, **Casorati–Weierstrass** gives a dense image and **Picard** gives every value infinitely often with at most one exception.

Residue formulas:

$$
\text{simple: } \lim_{z\to z_0}(z-z_0)f(z), \qquad \frac{p(z_0)}{q'(z_0)}
$$

$$
\text{order } m: \ \frac{1}{(m-1)!}\lim_{z\to z_0}\frac{d^{m-1}}{dz^{m-1}}\big[(z-z_0)^mf(z)\big]
$$

Essential singularities have no formula; expand the series.

**Residue theorem:** $\oint_C f = 2\pi i\sum\operatorname{Res}$ over enclosed singularities only.

## Contour Recipes for Real Integrals

| Integral                                                 | Contour                      | Extra tool                                |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| $\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{p}{q}$, $\deg q\ge\deg p+2$ | upper semicircle             | ML on the arc                             |
| $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)e^{iax}dx$, $a>0$             | upper semicircle             | Jordan's lemma                            |
| $\int_0^{2\pi}R(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)d\theta$           | unit circle, $z=e^{i\theta}$ | $d\theta = dz/(iz)$                       |
| $\int_0^\infty$ with $x^n$ symmetry                      | sector of angle $2\pi/n$     | match the symmetry                        |
| $\int_0^\infty x^{a-1}f(x)dx$                            | keyhole                      | the branch jump                           |
| pole on the real axis                                    | indented contour             | half-loop gives $\pi i\operatorname{Res}$ |

Reference values: $\int\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\pi$, $\int\frac{dx}{1+x^4}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}$, $\int\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}dx=\frac{\pi}{e}$, $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}dx=\frac{\pi}{2}$.

## Mapping and Counting

**Conformal** means angle-preserving, and an analytic $f$ is conformal exactly where $f'\ne0$. At a zero of order $m$ in $f'$, angles are multiplied by $m+1$.

**Möbius maps** $\frac{az+b}{cz+d}$ with $ad-bc\ne0$ are bijections of $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$, form a group, send circles and lines to circles and lines, and are determined by three points.

| Map               | Effect                                 |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| $az+b$            | similarity                             |
| $1/z$             | inversion                              |
| $\frac{z-i}{z+i}$ | upper half plane $\to$ disc            |
| $z^2$             | quarter plane $\to$ half plane         |
| $e^z$             | strip of height $\pi$ $\to$ half plane |
| $z+\frac1z$       | circle $\to$ segment (Joukowski)       |

**Riemann mapping theorem:** every simply connected domain other than $\mathbb{C}$ maps conformally onto the disc. Existence only, no formula.

**Argument principle:** $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f'}{f} = Z-P$, equal to the winding number of the image around 0.

**Rouché:** $|g|<|f|$ on $C$ implies $f$ and $f+g$ have the same zero count inside. Gives the fundamental theorem of algebra with the exact count.

## Harmonic Functions

$\nabla^2u=0$. The real and imaginary parts of an analytic function are harmonic, and on a simply connected domain every harmonic function has a harmonic conjugate, unique up to a constant. On an annulus $\ln|z|$ shows the conjugate can fail to exist globally.

Inherited properties: mean value, maximum principle, uniqueness from boundary data, infinite smoothness, Liouville.

The **Dirichlet problem** is solved on the disc by the Poisson integral formula and elsewhere by conformal mapping. Level curves of $u$ and $v$ are the equipotentials and field lines, or isotherms and heat flow, or velocity potential and streamlines.

## The Named Theorems

| Theorem                        | Statement                                                     |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cauchy–Riemann                 | $u_x=v_y$ and $u_y=-v_x$ characterize differentiability       |
| Cauchy's theorem               | $\oint_C f = 0$ on a simply connected domain                  |
| Cauchy's integral formula      | Boundary values determine interior values                     |
| Morera                         | Converse of Cauchy's theorem                                  |
| Liouville                      | Bounded plus entire implies constant                          |
| Fundamental theorem of algebra | Every non-constant polynomial has a root                      |
| Maximum modulus                | No interior maximum of $\lvert f\rvert$                       |
| Identity theorem               | Agreement on a set with a limit point forces global agreement |
| Taylor                         | Analytic on a disc means equal to the power series            |
| Laurent                        | Analytic on an annulus means a two-sided series               |
| Riemann (removable)            | Bounded near a singularity implies removable                  |
| Casorati–Weierstrass           | Dense image near an essential singularity                     |
| Picard                         | Every value, infinitely often, at most one exception          |
| Residue theorem                | $\oint_C f = 2\pi i\sum\operatorname{Res}$                    |
| Argument principle             | $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f'}{f}=Z-P$                       |
| Rouché                         | $\lvert g\rvert<\lvert f\rvert$ preserves the zero count      |
| Riemann mapping                | Simply connected, not $\mathbb{C}$, maps onto the disc        |
| Jordan's lemma                 | Oscillatory arcs vanish with only $f\to0$                     |

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting simple connectivity** in Cauchy's theorem. $\oint\frac{dz}{z}=2\pi i$ is the standing counterexample.
- **Confusing $\arg$ with $\operatorname{Arg}$**, or ignoring the quadrant when computing an argument.
- **Applying $\operatorname{Log}(z_1z_2)=\operatorname{Log}z_1+\operatorname{Log}z_2$** to principal values.
- **Assuming $\lvert\sin z\rvert\le1$.** True on $\mathbb{R}$ only.
- **Saying "differentiable at a point" when analyticity is needed.** $\lvert z\rvert^2$ is the reminder.
- **Not cancelling before classifying a singularity.** $\frac{\sin z}{z^3}$ has a pole of order 2, not 3.
- **Assuming residue and order are linked.** A high-order pole can have residue 0.
- **Including residues of poles outside the contour.**
- **Reading a Laurent series as belonging to a function alone.** It belongs to a function and an annulus.
- **Forgetting to check the arc vanishes** before equating a real integral with $2\pi i\sum\operatorname{Res}$.
- **Using a conformal map through a critical point**, where $f'=0$ and angles are not preserved.
- **Expecting a harmonic conjugate on a region with a hole.**

:::note[You've Got This]
Look at what this section actually rested on: one condition, that the derivative exists as a complex limit. From it came infinite differentiability, power series, rigidity, a working integral calculus, and a method for real integrals that no real-variable technique reaches. Almost every question in the section is answered by locating singularities and asking which the contour encloses, so if you have that reflex you have the subject. And you now know why $\frac{1}{1+x^2}$ has a Taylor radius of 1, why sine is bounded only on the real line, and why the primes need contour integration — three things that looked like unrelated curiosities and turned out to be the same idea.
:::
