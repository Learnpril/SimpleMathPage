## What You'll Learn

The difference between a point and a vector and why storing both as `(x, y, z)` hides
a real distinction. What basis vectors are. Which way is up and which way is forward in
Godot, Unity and Blender, and why that single table prevents most sign errors. Degrees
versus radians, and why every engine function wants radians.

## Points and Vectors Are Not the Same Thing

Both are three numbers. They mean different things.

A **point** is a location. `(3, 2)` as a point is a specific spot, and it only means
anything once you have agreed where the origin is.

A **vector** is a displacement - a direction with a length, and no location at all.
`(3, 2)` as a vector means _three right and two up_, starting from wherever you happen
to be.

<PointVsVector />

All three arrows in that figure are the same vector. They are drawn in different places
because a vector does not have a place. Their tips are three different points.

This is not pedantry, and here is the practical test:

- **Point minus point is a vector.** Enemy position minus player position gives the
  displacement from you to the enemy. Subtracting two places gives a journey.
- **Point plus vector is a point.** Your position plus your velocity times delta time
  gives your new position. Applying a journey to a place gives a place.
- **Point plus point is nothing.** There is no meaningful sum of two locations. If you
  find yourself adding positions, something is wrong - except when you are averaging
  them, which is really a weighted sum of displacements from the origin.

Most engines use one type for both. Godot has `Vector3` and nothing else; you decide
per variable whether it holds a place or a displacement. The compiler will not help
you, which is exactly why the distinction is worth holding in your head.

:::note[In Godot]
`Vector3` serves as both. The naming convention that saves you is to name
displacements after what they do - `velocity`, `to_target`, `offset` - and points after
what they are - `position`, `spawn_point`, `hit_point`.

```gdscript
var to_target: Vector3 = target.position - self.position   # point - point = vector
var next_pos:  Vector3 = self.position + velocity * delta  # point + vector = point
```

Later, in the transforms module, the distinction becomes mechanical rather than a
matter of discipline: points carry a fourth component of 1 and directions carry 0.
:::

## Basis Vectors: What the Numbers Are Counting

Writing a vector as `(3, 2)` is shorthand. In full it means:

$$
(3, 2) = 3\,\hat{\imath} + 2\,\hat{\jmath}
$$

where $\hat{\imath}$ and $\hat{\jmath}$ are the **basis vectors** - one unit along x
and one unit along y. The numbers are counts of basis vectors, not the vector itself.

That sounds like a technicality until you realise the basis can be anything. A
character's own right, up and forward directions form a basis, and the same physical
arrow has different numbers in that basis than in the world's. "Move me one metre to my
right" and "move me one metre along world x" are different instructions that happen to
coincide only when the character faces down the world axis. Everything the transforms
module does is bookkeeping for changing which basis the numbers refer to.

## The Conventions Table

Here is where engines genuinely disagree, and where a wrong assumption produces a model
lying on its side or a character walking backwards.

<HandednessTriads3D />

Two independent choices are being made. **Which axis points up**, and **whether the
coordinate system is left- or right-handed**.

|                   | Handedness   | Up axis | Forward axis | Vector convention          |
| :---------------- | :----------- | :------ | :----------- | :------------------------- |
| **Godot 4**       | right-handed | $+Y$    | $-Z$         | column vectors, $M\vec{v}$ |
| **Unity**         | left-handed  | $+Y$    | $+Z$         | column vectors, $M\vec{v}$ |
| **Unreal**        | left-handed  | $+Z$    | $+X$         | row vectors, $\vec{v}M$    |
| **Blender**       | right-handed | $+Z$    | $-Y$         | column vectors, $M\vec{v}$ |
| **OpenGL / glTF** | right-handed | $+Y$    | $-Z$         | column vectors, $M\vec{v}$ |

Bookmark this. Six later lessons refer back to it.

### Handedness, and why it flips your cross products

Point the fingers of your **right** hand along $+X$, curl them toward $+Y$, and your
thumb points along $+Z$. If that matches the engine, it is right-handed. Do the same
with your left hand and you have the left-handed convention.

The consequence is concrete: **the cross product changes sign**. In a right-handed
system $\hat{x} \times \hat{y} = +\hat{z}$; in a left-handed one the same formula
applied to the same numbers gives a vector pointing the other way. So a "which side of
this wall am I on" test written for Unity gives the opposite answer in Godot unless you
account for it. This is dealt with properly in the cross product lesson; for now, just
know the two are not interchangeable.

### Up axis, and why exported models lie down

Godot and Unity treat $+Y$ as up. Blender treats $+Z$ as up. So a model that stands
correctly in Blender arrives in Godot rotated 90 degrees about the x axis, face-planted
on the floor.

Both tools know this and will convert on export. The reason to understand it anyway is
that the conversion sometimes fails silently, and when a rig arrives rotated it is much
faster to recognise a known axis swap than to hunt for a broken bone.

### Forward, which is the arbitrary one

Handedness and up-axis are mathematical facts about the coordinate system. Forward is
pure convention - somebody decided which way a camera looks by default.

Godot and OpenGL use $-Z$ as forward, which surprises people: a camera at the origin
looks toward _negative_ z, so things in front of it have negative z coordinates. Unity
picked $+Z$. Neither is wrong; they simply disagree.

:::note[In Godot]
Rather than writing axis constants by hand, ask the transform:

```gdscript
var forward: Vector3 = -global_transform.basis.z   # note the minus
var right:   Vector3 =  global_transform.basis.x
var up:      Vector3 =  global_transform.basis.y
```

The minus sign in the first line _is_ the $-Z$ forward convention. Godot also provides
`Vector3.FORWARD`, which equals `(0, 0, -1)`, so the same fact is baked into the
constant.
:::

:::note[In other engines]
In Unity the same three lines read `transform.forward`, `transform.right` and
`transform.up`, and `forward` is `+Z` with no minus sign. Porting code between them
means checking every place a forward vector is constructed from raw axis numbers.
Anywhere you see a literal `(0, 0, -1)` or `(0, 0, 1)`, expect to change it.
:::

## Degrees and Radians

Humans think in degrees. Every trigonometric function in every engine expects radians.

A **radian** is not an arbitrary unit. It is defined so that the angle equals the arc
length it sweeps on a circle of radius 1:

<AngleUnitsDial />

Sweep the slider and watch the bottom two rows. The radian measure and the arc length
are always the same number, because the radius is 1. That is the whole definition.

Since a full circle has circumference $2\pi r$, a full turn is $2\pi$ radians:

$$
360^\circ = 2\pi \text{ rad}, \qquad
1 \text{ rad} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57.296^\circ
$$

The conversions are worth memorising in this direction, because this is the direction
you need when a designer hands you a number in degrees:

$$
\text{radians} = \text{degrees} \times \frac{\pi}{180}
$$

The values worth knowing on sight: $\pi/6 = 30^\circ$, $\pi/4 = 45^\circ$,
$\pi/3 = 60^\circ$, $\pi/2 = 90^\circ$, $\pi = 180^\circ$.

:::note[In Godot]
`sin`, `cos`, `atan2` and the rotation properties all use radians.
`rotation_degrees` is the exception, and it exists because the inspector shows degrees
to designers.

```gdscript
var r := deg_to_rad(45.0)   # 0.7853981633974483
var d := rad_to_deg(PI)     # 180.0
```

The bug this causes is passing a degree value to `sin` and getting a plausible-looking
wrong answer. `sin(90)` is not 1; it is about 0.894, because 90 radians is roughly
fourteen full turns plus a bit.
:::

## Try It Yourself

Three short exercises, each under twenty lines.

**Print your own axes.** Attach a script to a node, rotate it in the editor, and print
`-global_transform.basis.z` each frame. Watch the numbers as you spin it. This makes
"forward is $-Z$" a thing you have seen rather than read.

**Break the units on purpose.** Write `print(sin(90), sin(deg_to_rad(90)))` and look at
the two numbers. Getting this wrong once deliberately is cheaper than getting it wrong
accidentally later.

**Subtract two positions.** Make two nodes, print `a.position - b.position`, then move
them and watch it change. Confirm that the length of that vector matches
`a.position.distance_to(b.position)` - which is the next lesson.

## Where This Shows Up

- **Every gameplay script**, whenever you compute a direction from one thing to another
  and have to decide whether you are holding a place or a displacement.
- **Asset pipelines**, where the up-axis mismatch between Blender and a game engine is
  the single most common import problem.
- **Porting and tutorials.** Most game-math material online is written for Unity. Being
  able to spot which parts are convention rather than mathematics is what makes it
  usable in Godot.
- **Networking**, where sending a position and sending a velocity have different
  precision and prediction requirements, and conflating them causes rubber-banding.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
When something is mirrored, upside down, or facing backwards, do not start flipping
signs until it looks right. Come back to the table above and work out _which_
convention you assumed wrongly. A sign error you understand is fixed once; a sign error
you guessed at comes back in the next system you write.
:::
