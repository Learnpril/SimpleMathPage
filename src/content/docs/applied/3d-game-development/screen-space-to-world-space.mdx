## What You'll Learn

How to run Section 5.1's pipeline **backwards**: from a pixel the player clicked to a ray in the
world, which is how clicking, picking and aiming all work. How to run it **forwards** for a single
point, to put a health bar above someone's head - and the one check that stops markers appearing
for things behind you. And **spherical coordinates**, the two angles that make an orbit camera
behave, which the capstone needs.

## A Pixel Is Not a Position

Click on the screen and you have selected a pixel. A pixel is two numbers; a position in the world
is three. The missing number is depth, and the screen has no opinion about it.

So a click does not name a point. It names a **ray** - every point in the world that would land on
that pixel, which is a line running away from the camera. Finding out what was clicked means
intersecting that ray with the scene.

Getting to the ray takes two steps.

### Pixels to NDC

Section 5.1's normalised device coordinates run $-1$ to $1$ in both directions. Pixels run 0 to
width and 0 to height. And they disagree about which way is up:

$$
\text{ndc}_x = \frac{p_x}{w} \cdot 2 - 1
\qquad
\text{ndc}_y = -\left(\frac{p_y}{h} \cdot 2 - 1\right)
$$

**Screen Y counts downward from the top; NDC Y counts upward from the middle.** That minus sign is
the single most common bug in this whole area, and it is invisible when you test by clicking near
the centre of the screen.

### NDC to a ray

Now use the frustum. Section 5.1 established that at distance $d$ the frustum is
$d \tan(\theta/2)$ tall and that times the aspect ratio wide. A cursor at NDC $(x, y)$ is that
fraction of the way across it:

$$
\text{point} = \big(x \cdot d\tan(\theta/2)\cdot a,\;\; y \cdot d\tan(\theta/2),\;\; -d\big)
$$

Set $d = 1$, normalise, and that is the ray direction. **No matrix inverse required** - the
geometry gives it directly.

That is worth checking rather than trusting, because it is a shortcut. `unprojectAt` derives the
point from the frustum and `ndcOf` puts it back through the projection matrix; the two share no
code. Across a grid of cursor positions at four different depths they agree to $10^{-15}$, so the
shortcut really is the matrix inverse.

:::note[Why engines invert the matrix anyway]
The shortcut assumes a symmetric frustum centred on the view direction. Engines support
projections where that fails - off-centre frusta for each eye in VR, oblique projections for
portals and mirrors, sheared frusta for cascaded shadow maps - so they invert the matrix and
handle every case with one code path.

The geometry above is what the inverse _is_ for the ordinary case, which is why it is the version
worth understanding.
:::

## Picking

Below, the orange dot is a camera, the purple rectangle is its screen, and the purple dot is a
cursor on it. The orange line is the ray that cursor becomes.

<DemoPanel name="picking" />

Move the cursor sliders and the ray sweeps the scene. When it meets a sphere, that sphere turns
teal, a white dot marks the entry point, and the ray stops there.

Two details that matter in real code. The ray stops at the **nearest** hit, not the first one
tested - which is why an intersection test returns a distance rather than a boolean. And the test
runs in the camera's own **view space**, where the camera sits at the origin looking down $-Z$, so
the ray needs no transforming at all.

The check pins the arithmetic: the centre ray meets a sphere of radius 1 at $z = -10$ at exactly
$t = 9$, its near face. A ray aimed into a corner misses it. A ray starting inside a sphere still
reports a hit.

Part 6 does intersection properly - planes, boxes, capsules, and the cases `raySphere` glosses
over. This Section only needs enough to show the ray doing its job.

## The Other Direction, and the Trap

Now forwards: given something's position in the world, which pixel do you draw its health bar at?

That is just Section 5.1's pipeline followed by the pixel mapping in reverse. And it contains a
trap that everyone hits once.

<DemoPanel name="screenmath" />

Look at the middle two rows. A point **5 metres in front** of the camera and a point **5 metres
behind** it both project to pixel $(400, 225)$ — dead centre of an $800 \times 450$ screen. The
same pixel. Nothing about the pixel coordinates distinguishes them.

The reason is Section 5.1's divide. Clip $w$ is the point's distance in front of the camera, so it
goes **negative** behind - and dividing by a negative number mirrors the result through the
origin. Points behind you land back on screen, mirrored, at plausible-looking coordinates.

$$
w > 0 \;\;\text{is not optional}
$$

<DemoPanel name="marker" />

Turn the camera and watch the markers. With the check on, markers appear only for objects actually
in view. Tick the checkbox to skip it and red markers show up for objects behind the camera -
drifting across the screen in the wrong direction as you turn, which is exactly how this bug looks
when you ship it.

The build check asserts the ugly part directly: that the in-front and behind points land on the
same pixel, so the prose above cannot quietly become false. It also sweeps the camera through a
full turn and confirms the careless path really does over-draw.

## Spherical Coordinates for an Orbit Camera

An orbit camera goes round a target. The naive way to store it is a position plus a look-at, and
then dragging means rotating that position, which drifts and tangles the controls together.

Store **two angles and a radius** instead:

| Control           | Parameter                              |
| :---------------- | :------------------------------------- |
| Drag horizontally | azimuth, the angle around $Y$          |
| Drag vertically   | elevation, the angle above the horizon |
| Scroll            | radius                                 |

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= r\sin(\text{el}) \\
x &= r\cos(\text{el})\sin(\text{az}) \\
z &= r\cos(\text{el})\cos(\text{az})
\end{aligned}
$$

Now each input drives exactly one number, nothing accumulates error, and clamping is trivial. The
round trip is exact: the check sweeps azimuth and elevation and recovers both to $10^{-9}$.

**Clamp the elevation short of $\pm 90°$.** At the poles $\cos(\text{el})$ is zero, so $x$ and $z$
vanish and the azimuth stops meaning anything - every value gives the same point. The value list
above shows it: at elevation 89 the azimuth comes back as 137 as set; at elevation 90 it comes back
as 0, because there is nothing to recover.

That is the same degeneracy as Section 3.1's gimbal lock, arriving from a different direction, and
it has the same fix for the same reason.

:::note[In an engine]
Three.js has `Raycaster` with `setFromCamera(ndcCoords, camera)` - and it wants **NDC**, so you do
the pixel conversion above yourself, Y flip included. `Vector3.project(camera)` and
`Vector3.unproject(camera)` are the two directions. `Spherical` and `Vector3.setFromSpherical()`
exist, and `OrbitControls` is spherical coordinates with `minPolarAngle` and `maxPolarAngle` as the
clamp.

Godot's `Camera3D` has `project_ray_origin()` and `project_ray_normal()` taking **pixels** rather
than NDC, plus `unproject_position()` for the forward direction. `is_position_behind()` is the
`inFront` check as a named method, which tells you how often it is needed.

Unity's `Camera.ScreenPointToRay` takes pixels with the origin at the **bottom left**, not the top
left - so its Y is already flipped relative to most mouse APIs. `WorldToScreenPoint` returns a
`Vector3` whose `z` is the distance in front of the camera, and checking `z > 0` is exactly the
trap above.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Clicking on anything** in a strategy game, an editor, or an inventory in 3D.
- **Aiming**, where a crosshair at the centre of the screen becomes a ray down $-Z$ and the shot
  is whatever it meets first.
- **Health bars, name plates, objective markers and damage numbers**, all of which are a world
  position projected to a pixel, and all of which need the depth check.
- **Off-screen indicators**, which use the projected position _and_ the fact that it is off screen
  to point an arrow toward it.
- **Drag and drop in 3D**, which unprojects at a fixed depth each frame rather than taking a ray.
- **Orbit cameras** in editors, model viewers and strategy games - and the capstone's third-person
  camera, which is this plus Section 4.1's damping.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
A click is a ray, not a point, because the screen never had the depth. Pixels to NDC needs a Y
flip, and NDC to a ray is the frustum's own geometry rather than a matrix inverse. Going the other
way, a point behind the camera projects to a perfectly plausible pixel - the same one as a point in
front - so check that clip $w$ is positive before drawing anything. And store an orbit camera as
azimuth, elevation and radius, clamped short of the poles where the azimuth stops existing.
:::
