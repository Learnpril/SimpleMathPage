## What You'll Learn

How to get a smooth curve **through** a set of placed points rather than one steered by handles
beside them - which is what a path through waypoints actually needs. Where the tangents come from
when nobody authors them. And then the loose end Section 4.3 left: **equal steps in $t$ are not
equal distances along a curve**, which is why a camera moving at "constant speed" along a spline
speeds up and slows down until you fix it.

## The Problem With Handles

A cubic Bezier touches its first and last control point and only pulls towards the middle two. For
drawing a shape that is exactly right. For a path through waypoints it is backwards: you want the
curve to **visit** the points, and you do not want to author handles for each one.

**Hermite** curves reframe the same cubic. Instead of four points, they take two points and two
**tangents** - a position and a velocity at each end:

$$
H(t) = h_{00}(t)\,P_0 + h_{10}(t)\,\vec{m}_0 + h_{01}(t)\,P_1 + h_{11}(t)\,\vec{m}_1
$$

The four weights are chosen so that the curve starts at $P_0$ with velocity $\vec{m}_0$ and ends
at $P_1$ with velocity $\vec{m}_1$ - all four exactly. The build check confirms it: the endpoints
come back bit-identical, and the tangents to $10^{-12}$.

It is the same family of curve as before, just parameterised differently. A Hermite segment
converted to a Bezier puts the handles **one third** of the way along each tangent:

$$
B_1 = P_0 + \tfrac{1}{3}\vec{m}_0 \qquad B_2 = P_1 - \tfrac{1}{3}\vec{m}_1
$$

That is the same factor of three that showed up in Section 4.3's derivative, seen from the other
side. The check runs 401 values of $t$ through both forms and asserts they agree.

## Catmull-Rom: Guessing the Tangents

Hermite still needs tangents from somewhere. **Catmull-Rom** supplies them with one rule:

$$
\vec{m}_i = \frac{P_{i+1} - P_{i-1}}{2}
$$

The direction a waypoint is "heading" is the direction from the point before it to the point after
it. That is the obvious guess, and it turns out to be a good one - it is the entire algorithm.

The result passes through every point, is smooth at every joint, and needs **nothing** authored
beyond the points themselves.

<DemoPanel name="spline" />

Drag a waypoint and watch two things. The path always goes through every orange dot - the build
check asserts that to $10^{-12}$ at all five. And the purple segment at each waypoint is the
tangent Catmull-Rom chose, drawn at a third of its length so it sits where the equivalent Bezier
handle would.

Notice that moving one waypoint only disturbs the curve near it. That is **local control**, and it
is why this is the format for a designer-authored path: nudging one point cannot ripple down the
whole spline.

The tension slider scales those tangents. At 0 every tangent is zero, so the path collapses to
straight lines with a stop at each corner. The standard $0.5$ is what makes $\vec{m}_i$ come out
as _half_ the neighbour gap - which is where the division by two in the formula comes from.

| Continuity | Catmull-Rom | Why                                              |
| :--------- | :---------- | :----------------------------------------------- |
| **C0**     | yes         | consecutive segments share a waypoint            |
| **C1**     | yes         | both segments use the same tangent at that point |
| **C2**     | no          | curvature can jump at a joint                    |

C1 is what Section 4.3 said a camera needs, and Catmull-Rom gives it for free. C2 needs B-splines,
which buy it by giving up passing through the points at all - a trade worth knowing about and
rarely worth making for a waypoint path.

:::caution[Uneven spacing can make it loop]
The tangent at a waypoint is half the gap between its neighbours, so if one neighbour is far away
that tangent gets long - long enough that the curve can overshoot, and with badly uneven spacing it
can form a small loop or a cusp near a tight corner.

The fix is **centripetal** Catmull-Rom, which spaces the parameter by the square root of the
distance between points rather than uniformly. Three.js exposes it as
`curveType: "centripetal"`, and it is the default there for exactly this reason. The uniform
version above is the one worth understanding first; reach for centripetal when a path with clustered
points starts misbehaving.
:::

## Equal Steps in t Are Not Equal Distances

Now the loose end, and it catches almost everybody once.

A path is a function of $t$. Nothing in that function promises that a given change in $t$ covers a
given amount of ground. Segments spanning far-apart waypoints get the same slice of $t$ as segments
spanning close-together ones, so a dot advancing $t$ at a steady rate **races through the long
stretches and crawls through the short ones**.

Below, two dots on one path. Orange steps $t$ evenly. Teal steps **distance** evenly. The small
dots are ticks at even steps of each dot's own input.

<DemoPanel name="arclength" />

Look at the orange ticks: bunched where the waypoints are close together, spread out where they are
far apart. The teal ticks are evenly spread along the whole path, which is what "constant speed"
should mean. Drag the slider and the two dots pull apart by a large fraction of the path.

Here is the size of it:

<DemoPanel name="arcmath" />

**Nearly eight times.** Stepping $t$ evenly, the longest hop is 7.8 times the shortest, so a camera
on this path would visibly surge and stall. After reparametrizing, that ratio is 1.08.

:::note[Why not exactly 1.00]
The residual is not the method falling short, and it is worth being precise about. The measurement
walks the path in steps and compares **straight-line** hops. A fixed amount of arc spans a slightly
shorter chord where the curve bends harder, so even a perfect arc-length parametrization shows a
little variation under that ruler. Increasing the table from 256 samples to 4096 moves the ratio
from 1.076 to 1.070 and no further - it converges to the geometry, not to 1.
:::

### The lookup table

There is no closed form for the arc length of a cubic worth using, so the honest approach is the
one every engine takes:

1. **Walk the curve** in many small steps, summing straight-line distances.
2. **Store** the running total against each $t$.
3. To move a distance $s$, **binary search** the table for $s$ and interpolate between the two
   straddling entries.

That is `buildArcTable` and `tAtDistance`. Build it once when the path is created, not per frame.

The table has one bias worth knowing: chords cut corners, so it always **underestimates** the true
length, and the estimate rises as you add samples. Measured on this path: 8.074 at 32 samples,
8.096 at 128, 8.0974 at 1024. The check asserts that ordering, and that it has all but converged by
128 - which is why a few hundred samples is the usual choice.

With the table in place, moving at a genuine speed becomes what you would want to write:

```ts
travelled += speed * dt; // Section 4.1's dt, doing its job
const t = tAtDistance(table, travelled);
object.position = pathAt(t);
```

And that is Part 4 assembled: a frame-rate-independent step, a distance, a table, and a curve.

:::note[In an engine]
Three.js has `CatmullRomCurve3` with `curveType` of `"centripetal"`, `"chordal"` or `"uniform"`,
plus `tension`. Its `getPoint(t)` is parameter space and **`getPointAt(u)` is arc length** - two
methods one character apart that mean different things, which is worth reading twice.
`getSpacedPoints()` and `getLength()` build the same table internally.

Godot's `Curve3D` has `sample()` for parameter space and `sample_baked()` for arc length, with
`bake_interval` controlling the table resolution. `PathFollow3D.progress` is measured in distance,
so it is already doing the right thing.

Unity has no built-in spline in the old API; the Splines package added `SplineUtility.GetPointAtLinearDistance`
and `SplineUtility.EvaluatePosition`, again the arc-length and parameter versions of the same idea.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Camera rails in cutscenes**, which need both C1 continuity and arc-length parametrization or the
  shot lurches at every waypoint.
- **Patrol routes and rail shooters**, where a guard should walk at a steady pace regardless of how
  the level designer spaced the nodes.
- **Racing lines and track splines**, where distance along the path is the primary coordinate - lap
  position, checkpoints, opponent placement.
- **Placing objects evenly along a curve** - fence posts, road markings - which is the same lookup
  table used at authoring time rather than at runtime.
- **Animation retiming**, where an easing curve from Section 4.2 gets applied to the _distance_
  rather than to $t$, so the easing means what it says.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
Hermite takes two points and two tangents, so the curve passes through both ends with the velocity
you asked for. Catmull-Rom picks those tangents as half the gap between each point's neighbours,
giving a C1 path through every waypoint with nothing to author and local control retained. And a
curve's $t$ is not its distance: stepping $t$ evenly on this path varies the speed by almost eight
times, so anything travelling at a real speed needs an arc-length table built once and searched per
frame.
:::
