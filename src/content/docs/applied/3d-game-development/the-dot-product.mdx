## What You'll Learn

The dot product as three tools wearing one hat: a facing test, a projection, and an
angle. Which of the three you actually want most of the time (the first). And the
floating-point guard that stops the third from producing `NaN`.

## The Formula, and What It Means

Multiply matching components, add the results:

$$
\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = a_x b_x + a_y b_y + a_z b_z
$$

The output is a single number, not a vector. That is worth saying out loud, because it
is the difference between the dot product and the cross product and the source of a lot
of confusion.

The reason it is useful is the second identity:

$$
\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}|\,|\vec{b}| \cos\theta
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between them. Two very different-looking expressions for the
same number: one you can compute with three multiplies, one that tells you what it
means.

## Use One: Is It In Front Of Me?

This is the one you will reach for constantly, and it does not need the angle at all -
only the **sign**.

Since $|\vec{a}|$ and $|\vec{b}|$ are always positive, the sign of the dot product is
the sign of $\cos\theta$:

| Dot product | Angle                | Meaning                       |
| :---------- | :------------------- | :---------------------------- |
| positive    | less than $90^\circ$ | pointing broadly the same way |
| zero        | exactly $90^\circ$   | perpendicular                 |
| negative    | more than $90^\circ$ | pointing broadly opposite     |

So to ask "is the player in front of this guard", take the guard's forward vector, take
the vector from guard to player, and check the sign. No angle, no trigonometry, no
square roots.

That is `isInFront` in the panel above. In Three.js:

```js
const forward = new THREE.Vector3();
guard.getWorldDirection(forward); // -Z forward, from section 1

const toPlayer = new THREE.Vector3().subVectors(
  player.position,
  guard.position,
);

if (forward.dot(toPlayer) > 0) {
  console.log("player is somewhere ahead");
}
```

Neither vector needs normalizing for a **sign** test, because normalizing only divides by
a positive length and cannot change a sign. Skip it and save two square roots per check.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot: `(-global_transform.basis.z).dot(to_player) > 0.0`.
Unity: `Vector3.Dot(transform.forward, toPlayer) > 0f`, with no minus sign because Unity's
forward is +Z.
:::

### A field of view test

Narrowing "ahead" to "within a 60 degree cone" needs one more step. A 60 degree cone
means 30 degrees either side of forward, so compare the dot product against
$\cos 30^\circ \approx 0.866$:

$$
\hat{f} \cdot \hat{d} > \cos\left(\tfrac{\text{fov}}{2}\right)
$$

Here both vectors **must** be normalized first - meaning you divide each one by its own
length so it becomes a unit vector of length 1 (see the previous section). If you skip
this, the dot product's value depends on how far away the target is, not just the angle,
and the comparison against the threshold becomes meaningless. Normalizing strips out the
distance and leaves only the direction, which is all the cone test cares about.

Note that a wider cone means a _smaller_ threshold, which reads backwards at first:
$\cos$ decreases as the angle grows. A 60 degree cone uses $\cos 30° \approx 0.87$ as
its threshold, while a 140 degree cone uses $\cos 70° \approx 0.34$. The wider the
opening, the lower the bar the dot product has to clear.

That is `isInCone` in the panel above. Here it is running - drag the sliders and watch
the comparison happen:

The guard is the grey dot, looking up the screen. The blue wedge is its field of view.
Move the **target bearing** and the dot turns green the moment `forward.dot(toPlayer)`
climbs above the threshold, and red when it drops below. Widen the **field of view** and
the threshold falls - which is the $\cos$ decreasing as the angle grows.

In Three.js the same thing is four lines:

```js
const FOV_DEG = 60;
const threshold = Math.cos(THREE.MathUtils.degToRad(FOV_DEG * 0.5)); // 0.866, once

const toPlayer = new THREE.Vector3()
  .subVectors(player.position, guard.position)
  .normalize();

if (forward.dot(toPlayer) > threshold) {
  console.log("player is in the guard's cone of vision");
}
```

Compare the cosine, not the angle. Calling `Math.acos` per enemy per frame just to compare
against 30 degrees does the same job and throws away the arccosine's cost for nothing.

:::note[In an engine]
Identical in Godot and Unity, with `cos(deg_to_rad(...))` and `Mathf.Cos(... * Mathf.Deg2Rad)`
respectively. Unity also has `Vector3.Angle`, which returns degrees and is convenient but
computes the arccosine you were trying to avoid.
:::

## Use Two: How Much Of This Lies Along That?

The **projection** of $\vec{b}$ onto $\vec{a}$ is the part of $\vec{b}$ that points
along $\vec{a}$:

$$
\text{proj}_{\vec{a}}\vec{b} = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{|\vec{a}|^2}\,\vec{a}
$$

If $\vec{a}$ is already normalized this collapses to the pleasant form
$(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})\,\vec{a}$, which is the teal bar in the figure above.

Two immediate uses. **Speed in a direction**: the dot product of velocity with forward
tells you how fast you are going forwards, ignoring any sideways drift. And
**decomposition**: any vector splits into a part along a direction and a part
perpendicular to it, and subtracting the projection leaves the perpendicular part:

$$
\vec{b}_{\perp} = \vec{b} - (\hat{a} \cdot \vec{b})\,\hat{a}
$$

Hold on to that last equation. It is exactly how sliding along a wall works, and it
comes back in the collision response section: remove the component of velocity going
into the wall, keep the rest, and the character slides instead of sticking.

Those are `project` and `slide` in the panel above. Three.js gives you the first and not
the second:

```js
// The part going into the wall.
const along = velocity.clone().projectOnVector(wallNormal);

// Everything except that part, which is the sliding motion.
const sliding = velocity.clone().sub(along);
```

Three.js has `projectOnVector` and `projectOnPlane`; `projectOnPlane` is the slide, so
`velocity.clone().projectOnPlane(wallNormal)` does it in one call. Both expect the normal
to be normalized.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot has both directly: `velocity.project(n)` and `velocity.slide(n)`. Unity has
`Vector3.Project` and `Vector3.ProjectOnPlane`. All three are this one formula with
different names, and `move_and_slide` in the collision section is built on it.
:::

## Use Three: The Actual Angle

Rearranging the second identity gives the angle:

$$
\theta = \arccos\left(\frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{|\vec{a}|\,|\vec{b}|}\right)
$$

Here $\arccos$ - spelled `acos` in most languages - is the **inverse** of cosine: where
$\cos$ turns an angle into a ratio, $\arccos$ turns a ratio back into an angle. It reads
as "the angle whose cosine is this". Its input must lie between $-1$ and $1$, and its
output is always between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$. The next section leans on that output
range, because an always-positive angle cannot tell you which way to turn.

Use this when you genuinely need a number of degrees - to display it, or to feed a
rotation speed. Prefer the sign test or the cosine comparison when you do not.

### The clamp that prevents NaN

`arccos` is only defined on $[-1, 1]$. Mathematically the fraction above can never
leave that range. In floating point it absolutely can, because a normalized vector's
length is not exactly 1 and the division can land on `1.0000001`.

`acos(1.0000001)` is `NaN`, and once `NaN` is in a rotation it stays there.

So clamp, always:

$$
\theta = \arccos\big(\text{clamp}(x, -1, 1)\big)
$$

That is `angleBetween` in the panel above, and the clamp is the reason it exists as a
function rather than a one-liner:

```js
const c = a.dot(b) / (a.length() * b.length());
const angle = Math.acos(THREE.MathUtils.clamp(c, -1, 1)); // the clamp is not optional
```

Three.js's `a.angleTo(b)` already clamps internally, so prefer it when you can:

```js
const angle = a.angleTo(b); // safe, and shorter
```

The clamp matters when you write the arithmetic yourself, which you will as soon as you
need a signed angle - the subject of section 5, since `angleTo` always returns a positive
value and cannot tell you which way to turn.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot's `Vector3.angle_to()` and Unity's `Vector3.Angle()` also clamp internally. Every
library does, because every library has been bitten by it.
:::

## Worked Example

A guard faces $\hat{f} = (0, 0, -1)$, Godot's forward. The player is at
$(3, 0, -4)$ relative to the guard. Is the player within a 90 degree cone?

**Distance and direction.** $|\vec{d}| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$, so
$\hat{d} = (0.6, 0, -0.8)$.

**Dot product.** $\hat{f} \cdot \hat{d} = (0)(0.6) + (0)(0) + (-1)(-0.8) = 0.8$.

**Threshold.** A 90 degree cone is 45 degrees each side, and
$\cos 45^\circ \approx 0.707$.

**Verdict.** $0.8 > 0.707$, so yes, just inside. The actual angle is
$\arccos(0.8) \approx 36.9^\circ$ from forward, comfortably under 45.

Notice we answered the question with a comparison and only computed the angle afterwards
to check the work.

## See It Work

### The NaN hiding inside `acos`

Nothing to look at here - the whole event is one number going a single bit too far.

<DemoPanel name="acos-clamp" />

What is the angle between a vector and itself? Zero, obviously. But a normalized vector dotted
with itself comes out as `1.00000000000000022` rather than `1`, and `Math.acos` of anything
above 1 is `NaN`.

The clamp fixes it in one line and costs nothing when it is not needed - the 90-degree case
still answers 90. That is why `angleBetween` above applies it unconditionally instead of
checking whether it looks necessary.

:::note[In an engine]
Three.js does this for you: `a.angleTo(b)` clamps internally, as do Godot's
`Vector3.angle_to()` and Unity's `Vector3.Angle()`. The clamp becomes yours to write when you
compute the cosine yourself - which you will, because the cosine is the cheap part and the
arccosine is the expensive one you usually want to skip.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Enemy perception** - facing tests and vision cones, exactly as above.
- **Lighting.** Diffuse shading is $\max(0, \hat{n} \cdot \hat{l})$: how much a surface
  faces the light. The `max` is a facing test, throwing away the surfaces pointing away.
- **Backface culling.** A triangle is skipped when its normal points away from the
  camera, which is one dot product per triangle.
- **Wall sliding and surface friction**, decomposing velocity into "into the surface"
  and "along the surface".
- **Audio panning**, using the dot product of the listener's right vector with the
  direction to a sound source to decide left versus right.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
Reach for the **sign** first. Most questions that sound like they need an angle - in
front, behind, facing away, within a cone - are answered by a dot product's sign or by
comparing it to a precomputed cosine. Computing the actual angle is the slow, fragile
option, and it needs a clamp.
:::
