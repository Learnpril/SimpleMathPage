## What You'll Learn

Two lines of arithmetic that turn a point about the origin, the third step needed to turn it about
anything else, and the reason that third step is the most commonly forgotten line in 2D game code.
Then turning **toward** a heading rather than snapping to it, which is where Section 2.2's wrap earns
its place.

## The Rotation Formula

$$
x' = x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta \qquad y' = x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta
$$

Counter-clockwise, about the origin, in world coordinates with Y up.

Worth reading rather than memorising, because it is not arbitrary. Ask where the two axes go:

$$
(1, 0) \;\longmapsto\; (\cos\theta,\; \sin\theta) \qquad (0, 1) \;\longmapsto\; (-\sin\theta,\; \cos\theta)
$$

The first is the unit circle from Section 2.2. The second is that direction given a quarter turn, which
Section 2.1 showed costs nothing: swap and negate. Now read the formula again — it is $x$ lots of the
first plus $y$ lots of the second. **A rotation moves the axes and lets the point ride along.**

That observation is the whole of Section 3.1, where those two destinations become the two columns of a
matrix. Nothing new will be needed there; it is this, written down differently.

The bug to know about is a sign. Writing $x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta$ for the first component rotates the
other way, and on screen a rotation going the wrong way looks like a rotation right up until something
has to line up with something else. The build checks that rotating and then unrotating returns the
original, and that a $90°$ rotation is exactly the free quarter turn from Section 2.1.

Two properties fall out that are worth relying on. **Length is preserved** — checked at every degree
around the circle, drift under $10^{-12}$. And **rotations add**: turning by $30°$ then $40°$ is turning
by $70°$, which is why a spinning object can accumulate an angle rather than a matrix.

## Rotating About a Pivot

The formula only turns things about the origin. For anything else, three steps:

1. **Subtract** the pivot, so the pivot is at the origin.
2. **Rotate.**
3. **Add** the pivot back.

The dashed circle is the arrow's tip keeping its distance from the pivot, which is what rotating about a
point means. The pivot itself never moves.

## The Forgotten Translate Is Not a Crash

Nothing errors. Nothing is `NaN`. The sprite rotates **perfectly correctly** and lands somewhere else,
displaced by exactly the pivot — which the build asserts over **5,766** pivot-and-angle combinations
rather than eyeballing on one. The faint outline is where it should have been.

Watch what happens to the dashed circle when you uncheck the box. **It keeps its radius and changes its
centre**, from the pivot to the origin. That is the most precise statement of the bug available: the
shape is still turning, still rigid, still sweeping the same circle — around the wrong point. Both
halves of that are asserted, because a circle drawn in the wrong place is exactly the kind of claim a
picture makes silently.

Then click **pivot at the origin** with the box still unchecked, and watch the bug vanish.

That is the whole reason this is worth a section rather than a footnote. **With the pivot at the origin
there is nothing to add back, so the broken version is exactly right.** Which is where things sit while
you are writing the code, and while you are testing it, and right up until the first time an artist
moves a sprite off the origin or you parent something to something else. The failure arrives later than
the mistake, and by then the rotation code looks like the part that already works.

A near relative of this shows up in Section 3.1 as transform order, and again in Section 3.2 where a
turret on a tank has to rotate about its own mount rather than about the world's origin.

:::tip[Compute the sine and cosine once]
For a whole sprite, `Math.cos` and `Math.sin` belong outside the loop over corners. Forty corners is
forty identical trigonometric calls otherwise. This is not a micro-optimisation worth distorting code
for — the natural shape of the function is already the fast one, which is `rotateAll` below.
:::

## The Angle Difference, and Why It Needs Wrapping

Facing $170°$, aiming at $-170°$. How far do you have to turn?

Subtracting says $-170 - 170 = -340°$. The honest answer is $20°$, the other way. **Both describe the
same final heading**, which is exactly why this bug is so quiet: nothing is wrong with $-340°$ except
that it is the long way round.

$$
\Delta = \operatorname{wrap}(\theta_{\text{target}} - \theta_{\text{current}})
$$

One subtraction and one wrap, and the wrap is the entire function. The sign that comes out says which
way to turn: positive is counter-clockwise. That sign is the same information Section 2.1's cross
product carries, arriving by a different route.

## Turning at a Limited Rate

Snapping an angle to its target reads as teleportation. Real machinery has a turn rate: move toward the
target by at most so much per step, and stop when you arrive.

<DemoPanel name="2d-turn" />

The turret starts at $170°$ and the target defaults to $-170°$, $20°$ away across the seam. Push the
steps slider up and count.

Then uncheck the wrap and push it again. At the default $4°$ per step:

| Version                 | Difference it computes | Steps to arrive |
| :---------------------- | ---------------------: | --------------: |
| wrapped, the short way  |                  $20°$ |               5 |
| unwrapped, the long way |                $-340°$ |              85 |

Seventeen times the work to reach a heading that was $20°$ away, and both numbers are checked. **It
still arrives**, which is the point worth dwelling on. Nothing hangs, nothing oscillates, no value
becomes `NaN`. The turret simply takes the scenic route whenever the short way happens to cross the
$\pm 180°$ seam — which means it looks like a bug in the AI's decision-making rather than a missing
call to `wrap`, and that is where people go looking.

The correct version is checked for three things beyond the step count: it never overshoots, it settles
exactly on the target instead of oscillating around it, and its first step goes the direction the
wrapped difference says. Sweeping six targets against five turn rates, the unwrapped version is never
faster and is slower in most of them.

:::caution[The same seam, in a follower]
`lerp` between two headings has the identical problem and a more alarming symptom. Halfway from $170°$
to $-170°$ should be $180°$, straight behind. Lerping the raw numbers gives $0°$ — pointing exactly the
opposite way, through a heading nothing ever passed through.

Both values are asserted at build time. The fix is the same as above: interpolate the **wrapped
difference**, not the endpoints. Section 4.2 does the general version of this, and Section 4.1 explains
why the interpolation factor itself needs care once frames stop being evenly spaced.
:::

:::note[In an engine]
Godot: `Vector2.rotated()` for the formula, and `Node2D` has both `rotation` in radians and
`rotation_degrees`, so pick one and stay with it. `Node2D` also has a `pivot_offset`-style concept
through its transform origin, which is the three-step dance done for you. `lerp_angle()` is the
wrapped interpolation and `rotate_toward()` the limited-rate turn.

Unity: `Quaternion.Euler(0, 0, degrees)` for a 2D rotation, or `Transform.RotateAround()` for the pivot
version. `Mathf.DeltaAngle()` is the wrapped difference and `Mathf.LerpAngle()` the wrapped lerp.
`Mathf.MoveTowardsAngle()` is the limited-rate turn.

The naming is the thing to carry across: any engine method with `Angle` in its name is very likely doing
the wrap for you, and any that does not have it probably is not.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Anything that faces a direction**: turrets, vehicles, characters, a sprite's `rotation` field.
- **Rotating a sprite about a point that is not its origin** — a wheel, a hinge, a swinging platform.
- **Turning smoothly toward a target**, which is what makes a movement feel mechanical rather than
  instant.
- **Matrices in Section 3.1**, which package exactly this alongside translation and scale.
- **Local space in Section 3.2**, where a child's rotation composes with its parent's.
- **Angle interpolation in Section 4.2**, which is the wrapped difference with an easing curve on it.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
Rotating about the origin is $x' = x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta$ and $y' = x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta$,
which is the point's own components used as weights on the two rotated axes. About any other point it is
subtract the pivot, rotate, add it back — and forgetting that last step is exactly correct while the
pivot sits at the origin, which is why it ships. To turn toward a heading, wrap the difference before
you use it: unwrapped, a $20°$ turn across the $\pm 180°$ seam becomes a $340°$ one that still arrives,
so it reads as bad AI rather than bad arithmetic. Interpolating between two headings has the same seam
and needs the same wrap.
:::
