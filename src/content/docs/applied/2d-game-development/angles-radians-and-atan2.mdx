## What You'll Learn

How to get from an angle to a direction, from a direction back to an angle, and why the obvious way to
do the second one is wrong for exactly half of the plane. Then the wrap that makes two angles
comparable, without which a turret turns almost all the way round to face something beside it.

## Radians Are Not a Preference

An angle in radians **is** the length of the arc it cuts on a unit circle. That is the definition, not
a conversion factor, and it is why every trigonometric function in every language takes radians.

| Turn        | Radians | Degrees |
| :---------- | :------ | ------: |
| none        | $0$     |    $0°$ |
| an eighth   | $\pi/4$ |   $45°$ |
| a quarter   | $\pi/2$ |   $90°$ |
| a half      | $\pi$   |  $180°$ |
| a full turn | $2\pi$  |  $360°$ |

Degrees are for people: designers, inspector fields, dialogue with your team. So convert at the edges
and keep radians everywhere in between. A codebase that converts in the middle is a codebase where
somebody eventually calls `Math.cos` on a number of degrees, gets a plausible-looking wrong answer,
and loses an afternoon.

## From an Angle to a Direction

$$
\hat{d} = (\cos\theta,\; \sin\theta)
$$

That is the unit circle, and it is already length 1, so nothing needs normalizing. Section 1.1
established the convention this assumes: angles counter-clockwise from the $+X$ axis, measured in
**world** coordinates with Y up. On the canvas the same angle appears to turn the other way, and that
is a drawing concern, fixed with one minus sign at the point of drawing.

## From a Direction to an Angle

The other way needs `atan2`:

$$
\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(v_y,\; v_x)
$$

**Y first.** Every language spells it in that order and it still catches people, because it reads
backwards from the $(x, y)$ you are holding.

The readout also shows the number that decides whether this Section is telling the truth: the barrel's
own direction dotted back against the direction to the target. That has to be $1$. It is worth
explaining why that particular number is on screen. **The scene draws the barrel from the angle it
computed**, so a barrel pointing exactly the wrong way still looks like a barrel — you get a picture
with nothing visibly wrong in it. Aiming and then measuring where you actually ended up pointing turns
"it looks right" into something a build can check, and the check sweeps **6,560** target positions.

Now uncheck the box.

## Plain `atan` Cannot Work, and Looks Like It Does

The tempting version is the one from trigonometry class:

$$
\theta = \arctan\!\left(\frac{v_y}{v_x}\right)
$$

**The division is where the information goes.** $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$ are opposite directions, and
both give a ratio of $1$. `atan` receives one number where there were two, and no amount of care
afterwards can recover what the division discarded.

<DemoPanel name="2d-atan" />

The four rows are one target per quadrant. In the two where $x$ is positive the two functions agree
exactly. In the two where $x$ is negative they are half a turn apart — and nothing in the code says
which case you are in.

The row underneath is the one that matters: aim a barrel with each answer, then dot it back against the
target. `atan2` scores $1$ four times. `atan` scores $1, -1, -1, 1$. **A score of $-1$ is a barrel
pointing exactly away from what it was aiming at.**

Over the swept grid the damage is precisely the left half-plane: `atan` is exactly backwards at all
**3,240** sampled targets with negative $x$, and exactly correct at all **3,240** with positive $x$.
The 80 samples sitting on the vertical axis come out right, by luck — the division gives `Infinity`
and `Math.atan(Infinity)` happens to be $\pi/2$ — so straight up and straight down work, which just
concentrates the failure in the half you are least likely to test first.

At the origin it is worse than wrong: $0/0$ is `NaN`, `Math.atan(NaN)` is `NaN`, and Section 1.4
covered what a `NaN` does to every comparison downstream. `atan2(0, 0)` returns $0$ instead. Not
meaningful, but finite.

:::tip[There is one honest use for `atan`]
If you already know the direction is in the right half-plane — a slope you built yourself, a
guaranteed-positive $x$ — then `atan` is correct. That is a narrow enough case that reaching for
`atan2` unconditionally costs nothing and removes a whole class of bug.
:::

## The Signed Angle, Which Closes Section 1.4

Section 1.4 ended on a limitation: `acos` returns an angle from $0°$ to $180°$ and can never tell you
which way to turn. Section 2.1 supplied the missing sign. Put both together:

$$
\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(a \times b,\; a \cdot b)
$$

Both readings, in one expression. The cross product supplies $|a||b|\sin\theta$, the dot product
supplies $|a||b|\cos\theta$, and `atan2` divides out the lengths, leaving the angle **with its sign**.

Three things this buys over `acos`:

- **It is signed**, so it answers "which way should I turn" rather than only "how far off am I".
- **It needs no clamp.** `atan2` accepts any two numbers, so the `NaN` that rounding forces out of
  `acos` about 30% of the time cannot happen here.
- **It is better conditioned near $0°$ and $180°$**, where `acos` loses precision because its slope
  goes vertical.

The build checks it against `angleBetween` from Section 1.4 across the full circle: same magnitude
everywhere, sign supplied by the cross product. If you find yourself reaching for `acos`, this is
almost always the function you actually wanted.

## Wrapping

Angles live on a circle, not on a line. $370°$ and $10°$ are the same heading, and $-190°$ is that
heading too. Arithmetic does not know this, so you have to tell it:

$$
\operatorname{wrap}(\theta) = \left((\theta + 180°) \bmod 360°\right) - 180°
$$

with one wrinkle: **`%` in JavaScript keeps the sign of its left operand**, so a single modulo leaves
negative inputs negative and outside the range you asked for. Adding a full turn and taking the modulo
again fixes it, and the double modulo is checked over $\pm 900°$ rather than trusted.

Two facts worth knowing before you write a test against this:

- $370°$ wraps to $10°$, as expected.
- **Exactly $180°$ comes back as $-180°$.** Same direction, other spelling. The range is
  $[-180°, 180°)$, half-open at the top.

Wrapping never changes the direction an angle describes, which is checked by comparing sines and
cosines rather than the numbers themselves — the honest form of the claim.

Section 2.3 is where this stops being housekeeping. Every "turn toward" and every "how far off am I"
is a subtraction of two angles, and a subtraction of two angles is wrong without this.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot has `Vector2.angle()` for the angle of a vector and `Vector2.angle_to_point()` for aiming, both
`atan2` underneath. `Vector2.angle_to()` is the **signed** angle between two vectors — the
`atan2(cross, dot)` above, already written for you. `Vector2.from_angle()` goes the other way.

Unity has `Mathf.Atan2(y, x)`, same argument order. `Vector2.SignedAngle()` is the signed version and
`Vector2.Angle()` the unsigned one, both in degrees, which is a units boundary worth being deliberate
about. `Mathf.DeltaAngle()` is the wrapped difference.

Both engines return radians from the `atan2` family and degrees from the `Angle` helpers. That
inconsistency is the source of a genuinely common bug, so check the return unit rather than assuming
it.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Aiming**: turrets, homing projectiles, an enemy facing a player, a sprite's rotation.
- **Turning toward a heading at a limited rate**, which is Section 2.3 and needs the wrap.
- **Directions from input**: converting a stick's $(x, y)$ into a heading to compare against.
- **Sorting things by bearing**, for a radial menu or a minimap arrow.
- **Bezier tangents**, where a curve's derivative becomes a facing direction. Section 4.3.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
Radians are arc length, so keep them internally and convert to degrees only at the edges. Going from an
angle to a direction is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$; going back is `atan2(y, x)`, with Y first. Never
`atan(y / x)` — the division discards the signs and returns an answer exactly half a turn wrong for
every direction with negative $x$, which is half the plane, while being perfectly correct in the other
half. For the angle between two vectors, `atan2(cross, dot)` gives it signed, needs no clamp, and is
what `acos` should have been. And wrap any angle difference into $[-180°, 180°)$ before comparing,
remembering that $180°$ itself comes back as $-180°$.
:::
