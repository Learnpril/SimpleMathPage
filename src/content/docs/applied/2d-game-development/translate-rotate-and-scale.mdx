## What You'll Learn

Nothing new, at first. You can already translate a point, rotate one, and scale one. This Section puts
those three in a single box so they can be **combined** — which is what makes a sprite on a turret on a
tank possible, and what makes "why is my sprite in the wrong place" a question with one answer instead
of five.

Then the two things that go wrong: the order you apply them in, and forgetting that a matrix should not
move a direction the way it moves a place.

## Three Things You Have Already Done

| Operation     | What it is                        | Where it came from |
| :------------ | :-------------------------------- | :----------------- |
| **translate** | point plus displacement           | Section 1.2        |
| **rotate**    | $x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta$, etc. | Section 2.3        |
| **scale**     | multiply each component           | new, and trivial   |

Applied one at a time, in order, these are three lines of code and no matrix is needed. So the matrix
has to earn its place, and it earns it in exactly two ways.

**One transform instead of three.** Compose once, then apply the result to every corner of a sprite, or
every tile in a chunk, or every particle. A composition costs a handful of multiplications and pays for
itself immediately.

**Composition becomes multiplication.** A turret's transform relative to its tank, times the tank's
transform relative to the world, is the turret's transform relative to the world. That single sentence
is the whole of Section 3.2, and it is only available because the three operations share a shape.

## Why $3 \times 3$ and Not $2 \times 2$

Rotation and scale are both multiplications, so a $2 \times 2$ matrix holds them comfortably.
Translation is an **addition**, and no $2 \times 2$ matrix can add a constant — put $(0,0)$ in and any
matrix gives $(0,0)$ back.

The fix is to add a coordinate. Write a point as $(x, y, 1)$, and the extra $1$ has something to
multiply against:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} a & b & t_x \\ c & d & t_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} ax + by + t_x \\ cx + dy + t_y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

That is all **homogeneous coordinates** are at this stage: a third coordinate carried along so that
translation fits in the same box as the other two. It is worth meeting here, where the matrix is small
enough to read every entry, rather than for the first time in a $4 \times 4$ where the same idea is
buried in sixteen numbers.

The bottom row stays $0\;0\;1$ for everything in this Section, and the build checks that it does for
every matrix built here. Section 3.3 is where it finally does something.

## Conventions, Stated Once

Half of all matrix confusion is two sources disagreeing without saying so. This module makes these
choices and keeps them:

| Question                 | This module                    | The other convention |
| :----------------------- | :----------------------------- | :------------------- |
| points are               | **columns**                    | rows                 |
| a point goes on the      | **right**: $p' = Mp$           | left: $p' = pM$      |
| translation lives in the | **right-hand column**          | bottom row           |
| a product reads          | **right to left**              | left to right        |
| storage                  | **row-major**, a row at a time | column-major         |

The consequences are worth spelling out, because they are what you actually trip over. In $T R S$ the
**scale happens first** and the translation last, because $S$ is nearest the point. Reading a product
left to right is the opposite of the order things happen in, which never stops feeling backwards and is
forced by putting the point on the right.

If you use the row-vector convention instead, every product in this module reverses and every matrix
transposes. Neither is more correct; mixing them silently is what hurts. Sections 3.2 and 3.3 refer
back to this table rather than repeating it.

## The Columns Are Where the Axes Land

This is the one idea that turns a matrix from nine arbitrary numbers into something you can read.

$$
(1, 0) \longmapsto \text{column 1} \qquad (0, 1) \longmapsto \text{column 2} \qquad
\text{column 3} = \text{the translation}
$$

Which is Section 2.3's observation again: a transform moves the axes and lets the point ride along, with
the point's own components as the weights. A rotation matrix is not a formula to memorise — it is
$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and its quarter turn, written side by side.

That the matrix and Section 2.3's `rotate` agree is checked rather than claimed, at every degree from
$-360°$ to $360°$ across four different vectors.

## The Determinant Is an Area, and a Warning

The top-left block has a determinant, $ad - bc$, and Section 2.1 already told you what that number
means: it is the **cross product of the two columns**, so it is the area of the parallelogram the
transformed axes span.

| Transform             | Determinant | Meaning                      |
| :-------------------- | ----------: | :--------------------------- |
| rotation              |         $1$ | turning does not change area |
| pure translation      |         $1$ | nor does moving              |
| scale by $2$ and $3$  |         $6$ | six times the area           |
| scale by $-1$ and $1$ |        $-1$ | **mirrored**, same area      |

The negative case is the useful one. A sprite that comes out backwards, a polygon whose normals point
inward, a shape that suddenly fails a winding test — check the determinant's sign before anything else.

There is an exact identity behind this, and it is checked across eight different matrices: the
**signed** polygon area from Section 2.1 is multiplied by the determinant, sign included. So a negative
determinant is not merely associated with a flipped winding; it _is_ one.

## The Third Coordinate Is the Type Tag Section 1.2 Wanted

Section 1.2 made a distinction and then admitted the type checker could not enforce it: a place and a
displacement are both two numbers, so nothing stops you passing one where the other belongs.

Homogeneous coordinates enforce it. A place is $(x, y, 1)$ and a displacement is $(x, y, 0)$, and that
third number decides whether translation reaches the thing:

<DemoPanel name="2d-homogeneous" />

Rotation and scale act on both, because turning the world really does turn your directions. Translation
acts only on places, because moving the world leaves "four metres east" four metres east.

The fourth row of that panel is the reason this is a fact rather than a rule to remember. Transforming a
displacement has to equal transforming two places and subtracting them — that is what a displacement
_is_ — and the $w = 0$ arithmetic is exactly what makes those agree. The build sweeps that identity over
six matrices, three base points and three displacements.

:::caution[The bug this prevents]
Translating a normal, a velocity or a surface direction along with its owner. Nothing errors. At the
origin it is even correct, so it survives being tested — and the further your object is from the origin,
the more wrong it gets. Section 2.3's forgotten translate had the same shape, and so will Section 3.2's
local-to-world conversion. The pattern is worth naming: **a bug that is exactly right at the origin is
a bug that ships.**
:::

## Order Matters, and Only Sometimes

$T R S$ and $S R T$ are different transforms. Not subtly — the sprite lands somewhere else.

<DemoPanel name="2d-orders" />

The left panel is always $T \cdot R \cdot S$, the order nearly every engine uses. Click through the six
buttons and compare. At the panel's default settings all six land somewhere different, and the build
asserts that none of the other five matches the standard.

Now set the two scale factors equal and click through again. Because a **uniform** scale commutes with a
rotation, several of the six collapse together:

| Settings                  | Distinct results | What collapses                         |
| :------------------------ | ---------------: | :------------------------------------- |
| all three doing something |            **6** | nothing                                |
| uniform scale             |            **4** | $TRS = TSR$ and $RST = SRT$            |
| no translation            |            **2** | only "which of $R$ and $S$ went first" |
| no rotation               |            **2** | only "was the translation scaled"      |
| translation alone         |            **1** | nothing is left to order               |

Every one of those counts is asserted, and so are the specific groupings — including that a uniform
scale does _not_ collapse $R T S$ into $S T R$, because those two are the orders where a scale gets
applied to a translation.

**That middle row is why transform-order bugs reach players.** Uniform scale is the common case, and
with it two of the wrong orders are indistinguishable from the right one. The code looks fine for as
long as nobody sets a non-uniform scale. Then an artist squashes one sprite and a bug appears in code
that has not been touched in months.

:::note[In an engine]
Unity's helper is called `Matrix4x4.TRS(position, rotation, scale)` — the name states the order, and it
is the order above. A `Transform`'s `localScale`, `localRotation` and `localPosition` compose the same
way.

Godot's `Transform2D` stores exactly the interesting part of the matrix above: two basis columns, `x`
and `y`, plus an `origin` for the translation. The bottom row is not stored at all, because it is always
$0\;0\;1$ — which is a practical confirmation that ignoring it until Section 3.3 costs nothing.
`Transform2D` also multiplies with the same right-to-left meaning, so `parent * child` is a child
transform expressed in the parent's space.

Both engines expose the determinant's consequence rather than the determinant: Godot's
`Transform2D.determinant()` is there explicitly, and a negative scale on one axis in either engine is
the mirroring case above.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Every sprite that is drawn**, whether or not you build the matrix yourself.
- **Parent and child hierarchies**, where composition is multiplication. Section 3.2.
- **Cameras**, which are this matrix inverted. Section 3.3.
- **Normals and velocities**, which need the $w = 0$ rule to survive being transformed.
- **Mirrored sprites and inverted polygons**, diagnosed by the determinant's sign.
- **Section 5.3's separating axis test**, which needs a polygon's transformed axes — the columns above.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
A $3 \times 3$ matrix holds translation, rotation and scale in one object, and it needs the third row and
column because translation is an addition rather than a multiplication. Read its **columns**: the first
two are where the axes land, the third is the translation. Its determinant is the area factor, and a
negative one means the shape was mirrored. A place carries a third coordinate of $1$ so translation
reaches it, a displacement carries $0$ so it does not — which is Section 1.2's distinction finally
enforced by arithmetic. And with points as columns on the right, a product applies right to left, so
$TRS$ scales first; get that order wrong and it will look correct for as long as every scale stays
uniform.
:::
