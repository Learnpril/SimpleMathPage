## What You'll Learn

A Bezier curve is a **weighted blend of a few control points**, where the weights depend on one
parameter. Two points give a straight line, three give an arc, four give the cubic that every vector
drawing tool and every animation editor is built on.

Then there is the thing that catches everybody, and it is the reason this Section exists rather than
being a footnote: **the parameter is not distance.** It runs from 0 to 1 and it looks like "how far
along". Walk it at a constant rate and your sprite speeds up and slows down depending on where the
handles happen to sit. On one of the curves below, $t = 0.5$ has covered only $32.94\%$ of the ground.

## The Blend

$$
B(t) = (1-t)^2 P_0 + 2(1-t)t\,P_1 + t^2 P_2
$$

$$
B(t) = (1-t)^3 P_0 + 3(1-t)^2t\,P_1 + 3(1-t)t^2 P_2 + t^3 P_3
$$

Quadratic and cubic. The coefficients are $1{:}2{:}1$ and $1{:}3{:}3{:}1$ — Pascal's triangle, which is
where the pattern for any degree comes from.

The property worth holding on to is that **the weights always sum to 1**, at every $t$, and the build
sweeps a thousand values of $t$ to confirm it. That single fact explains most of a Bezier's behaviour:
the curve can never leave the box its control points sit in, it cannot loop off somewhere surprising,
and moving one control point moves the curve in a way you can predict.

It also explains the part beginners trip on. At $t = 0.5$ a quadratic's weights are $0.25$, $0.5$,
$0.25$ — so the middle point gets half the vote and the two ends split the other half. The curve
therefore lands **exactly halfway between the handle and the midpoint of the chord**, which on the
demo's quadratic is $2.6$ units short of the handle itself. A handle is something you steer with, not a
waypoint you route through.

## De Casteljau: The Same Answer, With Only `lerp`

There is a second way to evaluate a Bezier, and it needs no formula at all. Take each neighbouring pair
of control points and find the point $t$ of the way between them. You now have one fewer point. Repeat
until one is left. That is the point on the curve.

Nothing but `lerp`, all the way down — the function from Section 4.2, applied three times, then twice,
then once. It is worth knowing even though the polynomial is faster, for three reasons: it works at any
degree without a new formula, it is better behaved numerically, and it hands you two other operations
for free (below).

<DemoPanel name="2d-path" />

The two are genuinely different arithmetic — a loop of lerps against a weighted sum of four terms — so
the build sweeping ten thousand values of $t$ and finding them agree to $10^{-13}$ means both are right,
rather than that one was copied from the other.

:::tip[Look at the last orange segment]
The final pair of intermediate points, the one just before the answer, lies **along the curve's
tangent**. That is not a coincidence and it is not decoration: it means the direction to face while
travelling the curve is already sitting in the construction. The build checks it as a direction, at
several values of $t$, on both a quadratic and a cubic.
:::

## The Tangent, And Facing Along It

The derivative of a degree-$n$ Bezier is $n$ times a degree-$(n-1)$ Bezier built on the **differences
between neighbouring control points**. So the derivative of a cubic is a quadratic on three vectors,
which is a pleasant thing rather than a chore. The build checks it against a finite difference of the
curve itself at two hundred points on five different curves, so it is the derivative rather than a
plausible rearrangement.

One `atan2` on that gives the facing angle, and a car follows a road instead of sliding along it
sideways. Two consequences worth knowing:

**The tangent at an endpoint points straight at the neighbouring handle.** That is what makes handles
feel steerable — you are aiming the departure, not just bending the middle.

**The tangent's length is the speed**, in parameter terms, and it is not constant. Which brings us to
the problem.

## `t` Is Not Distance

<DemoPanel name="2d-travel" />

Eleven marks at equal steps of $t$. They bunch where the curve is slow and spread where it is fast, so
the picture is the speed profile rather than a claim about one. Then tick the box, and the same eleven
marks are placed at equal steps of **distance**.

On the "slow start, fast finish" curve:

| Parameter | Distance covered |
| --------: | ---------------: |
|  $t=0.25$ |        $16.49\%$ |
|   $t=0.5$ |        $32.94\%$ |
|  $t=0.75$ |        $58.12\%$ |

Half the length is not reached until $t = 0.6829$. The fastest point on that curve moves **3.409 times**
as fast as the slowest, and the widest gap between marks is $3.118$ times the narrowest. Step by
distance instead and that ratio drops to $1.009$.

:::caution[A symmetric curve hides this completely]
The first preset is in the list as a control case. It is symmetric, so $t = 0.5$ lands on exactly
$50.00\%$ of its length, and anyone testing on a curve like that would conclude there was nothing to
fix. My first draft of this Section used three symmetric curves and every one reported a perfect half —
which would have made the central claim look like a rounding error. The build now asserts **both**: that
the symmetric curve does land on 50%, and that the lopsided one is nowhere near it.

Note also that the symmetric curve's speed still varies by a factor of $1.485$. Symmetric is not
uniform; it is only symmetric about the middle.
:::

### The Fix: An Arc-Length Table

There is no closed form for the length of a cubic Bezier. This is not a gap in the library — it is a
fact about the integral, and every engine samples instead. Walk the curve in small straight steps,
accumulate the distances, and keep the running total against each $t$. Then to travel at a constant
speed, ask for `distance = speed * elapsed` and binary-search the table for the $t$ that sits there.

Sampling is a choice with a price, so the price is measured rather than waved at:

| Samples | Length error, in pixels |
| ------: | ----------------------: |
|       8 |                $1.1554$ |
|      16 |                $0.2887$ |
|      64 |                $0.0180$ |
|     256 |                $0.0011$ |

Quartering with every doubling, which is what a second-order method should do. The default of $256$ is
accurate to a thousandth of a pixel at this scale — built once when the path changes, not per frame.

The table is read in both directions, and the build checks that the round trip is exact to $10^{-12}$
across all four curves. That is a real check rather than an identity: one direction is a binary search
and the other a forward interpolation, so they could disagree.

## Two Things The Construction Gives You Free

**Splitting.** The first point of every level of de Casteljau's construction is the left half's control
points; the last point of every level, reversed, is the right half's. Two curves of the same degree that
together retrace the original exactly — checked at three split points on five curves, to $10^{-12}$.
Useful for trimming a path, for drawing only the part already travelled, and for subdividing until a
curve is flat enough to draw as line segments.

**Degree elevation.** Add a control point without changing the shape at all:

$$
Q_i = \frac{i}{n+1}P_{i-1} + \left(1 - \frac{i}{n+1}\right)P_i
$$

So **every quadratic is also a cubic**. The demo's quadratic becomes a cubic whose handles sit exactly a
third of the way along each side. That is practically useful rather than trivia: a tool that only speaks
cubics can accept a quadratic, and a chain of mixed degrees can be made uniform before anything else
touches it.

## Chaining: A Shared Endpoint Is Not A Smooth Join

One cubic is rarely enough for a route. Chaining them is where a subtle bug lives, because the obvious
check passes.

<DemoPanel name="2d-joins" />

Those two curves share an endpoint exactly. `meetsAt` returns `true`. And the seam still turns through
**a right angle** — the first curve arrives at $-45°$ and the second leaves at $+45°$, so a sprite
crossing it swings its facing $90°$ in a single frame.

Sharing a point is $C^0$ continuity. Smooth means the **tangents** on either side point the same way,
and the fix is one line:

$$
P_1' = 2S - P_{n-1}
$$

Reflect the previous curve's last handle through the shared point. That is the whole trick behind every
smooth path editor — dragging a handle on one side of a node and watching its twin swing round on the
other side is this arithmetic, and nothing else. It moves exactly one point, leaves the shared point and
the rest of the second curve untouched, and running it on an already-smooth seam changes nothing.

:::note[Direction, or direction and speed?]
Matching tangent **directions** is $G^1$, and it is what a path usually needs — no visible kink, no
facing snap. Matching direction **and length** is $C^1$, which additionally means the parameter speed
does not jump at the seam. If you are travelling by arc length you have already stopped caring about
parameter speed, so $G^1$ is normally the right target and $C^1$ is a constraint you are paying for
without a reason.
:::

## The Facing That Has No Answer

Put a handle exactly on its own endpoint — which is what happens when somebody wants a "straight" start
— and the tangent there is the **zero vector**. The build confirms it is exactly zero, not merely small.

`atan2(0, 0)` returns $0$. No error, no `NaN`, no warning. So the sprite snaps to facing east for one
sample and then jumps back to its real heading of about $33°$. That is why `facingAt` returns `null`
rather than an angle:

- It is a hole of **exactly one point**. By $t = 10^{-5}$ the facing is defined and perfectly ordinary.
- Which is precisely what makes it hard to find: a one-frame flick at the start of a movement, with
  nothing in the console.
- The build also confirms every well-formed curve has a facing at all $501$ sampled values of $t$, so
  the `null` is a real case rather than the normal one.

Handle it by carrying the previous frame's facing forward, or by reading the tangent slightly ahead. Do
not let `atan2` answer a question that has no answer.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot's `Curve2D` is the direct equivalent and it already does the arc-length work: `sample_baked()`
takes a **distance**, not a parameter, which is the right default. `get_baked_length()` gives the total
and `bake_interval` is the sampling resolution — the table above, exposed as a setting.
`Path2D` plus `PathFollow2D` wraps the lot, and `PathFollow2D.rotates` is the tangent-facing trick.
`Curve2D.sample()` is the raw parameter version, so reach for `sample_baked()` unless you specifically
want uneven speed.

Unity has no built-in Bezier for gameplay. `AnimationCurve` is a different thing — a scalar over time
with tangents, not a path through space. `Vector3.Lerp` three times is de Casteljau, and it is common
to write the four-term polynomial directly. `LineRenderer` draws the result. If you want the arc-length
behaviour you will be building the table yourself, which is the second half of the file above.

Both are affine-invariant, which is worth knowing: transforming the control points and then evaluating
gives the same curve as evaluating and then transforming, so a Bezier drawn in local space survives
Section 3.2's hierarchy untouched.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Patrol routes and scripted camera moves**, which is where the arc-length table earns its keep.
- **Section 4.2's easing, composed with this**: ease the _distance_ along the path, not the parameter,
  or you get two speed curves multiplied together and neither one is what you asked for.
- **Section 2.2's `atan2`**, doing the facing, with the degenerate case above as its edge.
- **Section 3.2's local space**, since a path defined in a parent's frame moves with the parent.
- **The capstone**, where a moving platform follows a path and has to arrive on time.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
A Bezier is a weighted blend of its control points whose weights always sum to 1, which is why it stays
inside them and why a handle pulls the curve without the curve ever reaching it. De Casteljau evaluates
it with nothing but `lerp`, and throws in the tangent, the split and the subdivision for free. The
parameter $t$ is **not** distance: on an asymmetric curve $t = 0.5$ can be a third of the way along, and
the fastest stretch can be over three times the speed of the slowest. If you want constant speed, build
an arc-length table once and ask it for distances. Chain curves by reflecting the previous handle
through the shared point, $P_1' = 2S - P_{n-1}$, because sharing an endpoint alone leaves a corner. And
guard the facing: a handle sitting on its endpoint gives a zero tangent, and `atan2(0, 0)` will answer
$0$ without telling you it made that up.
:::
