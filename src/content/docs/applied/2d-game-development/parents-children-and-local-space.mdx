## What You'll Learn

Why a turret bolted to a tank stays bolted to it without anybody writing code to keep it there. That
is the payoff Section 3.1 was building toward: composition is multiplication, so a hierarchy costs one
matrix product per level and nothing else.

Then the three ways it goes wrong — the product written backwards, the conversion done in the wrong
direction, and one failure that belongs to hierarchies alone: a parent's uneven scale **shearing** a
rotated child, with nothing in the child reporting it.

## A Child Is Written In Its Parent's Coordinates

That single sentence is the whole idea. The turret is placed at $(0.25, 0)$ facing forward — **in the
hull's coordinates**, not the world's — and then never touched again.

$$
M_{\text{turret,world}} = M_{\text{hull,world}} \; M_{\text{turret,local}}
$$

Because points are columns on the right, as fixed in
[3.1's conventions](/applied/2d-game-development/translate-rotate-and-scale/#conventions-stated-once),
the rightmost matrix acts first. So a point starts in the deepest local space and is carried outward,
one parent at a time, until it lands in the world. A three-level chain is the same rule applied twice,
and the build checks that grouping the product either way gives the same answer.

**Parent times child. Not child times parent.** That ordering follows from the convention rather than
being a separate thing to remember, which is worth knowing because it is the mistake this Section
exists to prevent.

<DemoPanel name="2d-tank" />

Drive the tank with the first two sliders and the turret follows — but look at what is actually moving.
The turret's own numbers never change. The readout says so: mounted at $(0.25, 0)$, at whatever angle
you set relative to the hull. Everything it does on screen comes from its parent.

The two little arrow pairs are each frame's own axes, drawn straight out of its matrix columns. That is
what makes "the child's coordinates are the parent's" something you can point at rather than a sentence
to accept.

## The Product Backwards

Tick the last box and the turret is composed as `turret × hull` instead.

It is not subtly wrong. It reads the hull's transform as though it were written in the turret's
coordinates, which is a sentence with no meaning, and the turret lands somewhere unrelated. The values
panel below pins one case: the mount belongs at $(3.00, 1.25)$ and the reversed product puts it at
$(3.25, 1.00)$.

**And at the identity the two orders agree exactly.** Set every slider to zero and there is nothing to
tell them apart. That is the shape of bug this module keeps running into — Section 2.3's forgotten
translate, correct at the origin; Section 3.1's transform order, correct under uniform scale; and now
this, correct until something moves.

## Both Directions

Going outward is multiplication. Coming back is the inverse:

$$
p_{\text{local}} = M_{\text{world}}^{-1} \; p_{\text{world}}
$$

Which is what a mouse click needs. The pointer arrives in world coordinates and the question is where
that lands **on the tank**, so the chain has to be undone.

<DemoPanel name="2d-localworld" />

The first three rows are the round trip: the barrel tip is $(1.15, 0)$ in the turret's own coordinates,
which with the hull at $(3, 1)$ turned a quarter turn puts it at $(3.00, 2.40)$ in the world, and back
to exactly $(1.15, 0)$ again. That round trip is swept over a grid of hull positions and angles at
build time, because an inverse that is slightly wrong still returns a perfectly plausible point.

:::caution[The inverse is not a negated translation]
Inverting a transform means inverting the linear part **and** carrying the translation through it: if
$p' = Lp + t$ then $p = L^{-1}p' - L^{-1}t$. Negating the translation and stopping there is only
correct when there is no rotation or scale, so it works in exactly the test case you are most likely to
write first. The build asserts that the two genuinely differ across the sweep.

`inverse` also returns `null` rather than a matrix full of infinities when the determinant is zero. A
zero scale on either axis collapses the plane onto a line and destroys information — every point on
that line came from somewhere different, and nothing can recover which. Same reasoning as Section 1.3's
`normalize`: hand the decision back to the caller.
:::

## Shear: The Thing Only A Hierarchy Can Do

Give the hull an uneven scale and watch the turret.

A parent scaled unevenly does **not** scale a rotated child unevenly. It **shears** it. The child's
axes are no longer lined up with the parent's, so each one picks up a different share of the stretch,
and a square child comes out a parallelogram.

Here is a $2 \times 1$ parent against a child at various angles, measured rather than described:

| Child's rotation | Angle between its axes | Its axis lengths | Still square? |
| ---------------: | ---------------------: | :--------------- | :------------ |
|             $0°$ |                  $90°$ | 2.000, 1.000     | yes           |
|            $15°$ |               $110.6°$ | 1.949, 1.096     | no            |
|            $30°$ |               $123.0°$ | 1.803, 1.323     | no            |
|            $45°$ |               $126.9°$ | 1.581, 1.581     | **no**        |
|            $60°$ |               $123.0°$ | 1.323, 1.803     | no            |
|            $90°$ |                  $90°$ | 1.000, 2.000     | yes           |

Three things worth reading off that table. **The axis-aligned cases survive** — at $0°$ and $90°$ the
child is scaled, not sheared, because its axes still coincide with its parent's and only swap roles.
**$45°$ is the worst case**, and there the two axis lengths come out _equal_: the frame is a rhombus,
which is as far from a rectangle as it gets. And **none of those lengths are numbers anybody typed.**
The child's scale is $(1, 1)$ throughout.

That last point is why this needs measuring instead of describing. Nothing in the child's own placement
changed, so nothing in the child can report the problem — `shearOf` has to ask the composed frame
whether its axes are still perpendicular, using Section 1.4's dot product. The build sweeps every
$5°$ from $5°$ to $85°$ and asserts each one is sheared, plus that a **uniform** parent scale never
shears anything at any angle.

:::note[In an engine]
This is the reason Unity warns about non-uniform scale on a rotated parent, and why its `lossyScale`
on a `Transform` is called lossy: once a chain shears, no single $(x, y, z)$ scale describes the
result, so what you are handed back is an approximation. Godot's `Transform2D` has
`orthonormalized()` for forcing the frame square again, which is the fix when you would rather lose the
scale than keep the skew.

The practical rule in both: **scale the leaf, not the parent.** If a hull needs to be longer, scale the
hull's sprite, not the node the turret is parented to.
:::

## Reparenting Without Anything Jumping

Picking an item up, dropping a passenger out of a vehicle, attaching a projectile to whatever it hit —
all the same operation. The thing must not move on screen, so hold its world transform fixed and solve
for the local one:

$$
M_{\text{local}} = M_{\text{newParent}}^{-1} \; M_{\text{world}}
$$

Undo the new parent, then apply the world transform. That shape is the general rule
$(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ read in a useful direction. The last row of the panel above confirms it, and
the build sweeps it over a range of new parents, checking both that the recomposed matrix equals the
original and that a specific point on the child does not budge.

## Where This Shows Up

- **Anything mounted on anything**: turrets, wheels, held items, a health bar over a head.
- **Mouse picking**, which is a world point converted into a local space. Section 3.3 does the first
  half of that conversion, from screen to world.
- **Cameras**, which are this idea inverted: the whole world becomes a child of the camera. Section 3.3.
- **Sprite flipping**, where a negative parent scale mirrors every child — Section 3.1's determinant.
- **Collision shapes**, which must be transformed into a shared space before they can be compared.
  Part 5 assumes you can do this.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
A child's transform is written in its parent's coordinates, so its world transform is
$M_{\text{parent}} M_{\text{child}}$ — parent on the left, because the rightmost matrix acts first.
Get that order backwards and the child lands somewhere unrelated, while agreeing perfectly with the
correct version until something moves off the identity. Coming back the other way needs the inverse,
which inverts the linear part and carries the translation through it rather than merely negating it,
and which has to refuse when a zero scale has collapsed the space. And a parent with an uneven scale
shears a rotated child rather than scaling it: the child's own numbers stay $(1, 1)$, so the only way
to detect it is to ask the composed frame whether its axes are still perpendicular.
:::
