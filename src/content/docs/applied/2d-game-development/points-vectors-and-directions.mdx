## What You'll Learn

Two different things that are both written as two numbers: a **place** and a **displacement**. Which
combinations of them mean something, which one is the most used line of code in any game, and the
test that tells them apart when the naming doesn't.

## A Place and a Displacement

"The player is at $(3, 2)$." That is a **point** — a place, measured from the origin.

"Move 4 right and 1 up." That is a **vector** — a displacement. How far and which way, with no
opinion about where you started.

In code both are `{ x, y }`. TypeScript sees no difference between them and neither does any engine.
**The compiler will not catch you mixing them up**, which is exactly why it is worth being deliberate
about which one you are holding.

## Four Sentences, One of Them Nonsense

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{place} - \text{place} &= \text{displacement} \\
\text{place} + \text{displacement} &= \text{place} \\
\text{displacement} + \text{displacement} &= \text{displacement} \\
\text{place} + \text{place} &= \text{nothing at all}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first one is the workhorse. **The displacement from A to B is `B - A`**, and it is probably the
single most written line in game code: from a player to a target, from a bullet to a wall, from a
camera to the thing it is following.

$$
\text{towards the target} = \text{target} - \text{player}
$$

Get the order backwards and you get $(-8, -4)$ instead of $(8, 4)$ — a displacement of exactly the
same size pointing exactly the wrong way. It compiles, it runs, and the enemy runs away from you.

The last one is the interesting one, and the reason this Section exists.

## The Origin Test

Why is adding two places meaningless? Not because a rule says so. Because **the answer depends on
where you happen to have put the origin**, which means it was never a fact about the two places.

<DemoPanel name="2d-arrow" />

Drag A and B around, then slide the origin out from under them.

Watch what happens. **Both places get new numbers.** They haven't moved — the origin did — but every
reading changes, because a place is measured _from_ somewhere.

**The arrow between them does not change.** Same length, same direction, same two numbers. It was
never measured from anywhere; it only ever said how far and which way.

That is the real difference between the two, and it is worth more than any naming convention:

$$
\text{a displacement survives moving the origin. A place does not.}
$$

<DemoPanel name="2d-kinds" />

The numbers make it concrete. Measured from three different origins, `B - A` is $(8, 4)$ every single
time. Meanwhile `A + B` goes from $(14, 8)$ to $(4, -2)$ to $(214, -72)$ — three different answers to
the same question, which is how you know it wasn't a question.

The check sweeps **400 origins** and requires the displacement to be identical at every one, and the
sum to differ at essentially all of them. A claim like that shouldn't rest on the one origin a figure
happened to use.

:::tip[The midpoint is a real exception, and worth understanding]
Averaging two places _does_ work. `(A + B) / 2` gives the point halfway between, and it survives the
origin test — the check confirms it stays at $(7, 4)$ from every origin.

Written that way it looks like it shouldn't. Written honestly, it does:

$$
\text{midpoint} = A + \tfrac{1}{2}(B - A)
$$

which is a place, plus half a displacement. A legal sentence, and it happens to give the same answer.

The rule underneath: a weighted sum of places is meaningful **when the weights add up to one**.
Halves add to one. A plain sum's weights add to two, which is why it drifts.
:::

## Scaling

Multiplying a displacement does what you would hope: doubling it means going twice as far the same
way, and multiplying by $-1$ turns it around. Scaling also survives the origin test, because it is
built out of a displacement and nothing else.

Multiplying a **place** by two does not mean anything useful. It doubles the distance from the origin,
so the result depends entirely on where the origin is — the check shows the same point landing at
$(6, 4)$ from one origin and $(1, -1)$ from another.

If you ever find yourself scaling a position, what you almost certainly want is to scale the
**displacement from something** and then apply it: `anchor + (p - anchor) * k`. Same shape as the
midpoint, same reason it works.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot has one `Vector2` used for both, with `+`, `-` and `*` as you would expect. `Vector2.direction_to()`
is `(b - a)` normalised, which Section 1.3 covers, and `distance_to()` is its length.

Unity splits them slightly: `Vector2` and `Vector3` are used for both places and displacements, but
`Transform.position` and `Transform.localPosition` make clear which frame a place is measured in.

Neither engine will stop you adding two positions together. No language does. The distinction is one
you keep by naming things carefully, which is the entire reason to have read this Section.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Chasing, aiming and following**, all of which start with `target - self`.
- **Anything that moves**, which is a place plus a displacement every single frame.
- **Combining influences** — input plus knockback plus wind — which is displacements adding.
- **Cameras**, which follow a place while thinking in displacements from it.
- **Section 1.3**, which takes the displacement from here and asks how long it is.
- **Section 1.4**, which asks what angle it makes.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
A place is measured from an origin; a displacement is not measured from anywhere. Subtract two places
and you get the displacement between them — `B - A`, in that order, or your enemy walks away from you.
Add a displacement to a place and you get a place. Add two displacements and you get a displacement.
Add two places and you get a number that changes when you move the origin, which is how you know it
means nothing. Both are `{ x, y }` and no compiler will help you, so the distinction is one you keep
by hand.
:::
