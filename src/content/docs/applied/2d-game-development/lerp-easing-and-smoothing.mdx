## What You'll Learn

Four functions do most of the arithmetic in a game: `lerp` to blend, `inverseLerp` to ask how far
along, `remap` to move a value between ranges, and `clamp` to keep it in bounds. Everything else in
this Section is a **shape** laid on top of them.

The shape is not decoration. A menu that slides in at a constant speed and a menu that eases to a
stop are the same movement over the same time, and readers describe one as cheap and the other as
polished without being able to say why. The difference is a curve, and choosing it deliberately is
most of what "feel" means.

## Easing Is Not Damping, And The Difference Decides Which To Use

Section 4.1 ended with exponential decay. This Section is not a nicer version of it — the two solve
different problems, and reaching for the wrong one is the common mistake.

|                     | Easing                                 | Decay (Section 4.1)                |
| :------------------ | :------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------- |
| You must know       | start, end **and** duration            | only the current target            |
| The target can move | no, not without restarting             | yes, that is the point             |
| Arrival             | **exact**, at exactly the stated time  | asymptotic, never quite            |
| Can overshoot       | yes, on purpose                        | no                                 |
| Natural use         | a menu, a door, a scripted camera move | a follow camera, a health bar, aim |

The arrival row is the one that matters. With a $0.15$ s half-life, decay has closed $96.875\%$ of the
gap after $0.75$ s, $99.902\%$ after $1.5$ s, and never all of it. A tween with a $0.75$ s duration is
at the target at $0.75$ s exactly, and the build asserts that as an equality rather than a tolerance —
for all eight curves, at five different frame rates.

:::note[The one place "never" needs care]
Decay never arriving is true of the mathematics and **false of a `double`**. The remaining gap stops
being representable after $54$ half-lives, which is $8.1$ s at the half-life above, and from then on
the value _is_ the target. That is long after anything a game waits for, so the useful phrasing is not
"never" but "not at a time you can name" — which is exactly why you cannot schedule anything on it.
:::

## The Four Workhorses

$$
\text{lerp}(a, b, t) = a + (b - a)t
\qquad
\text{inverseLerp}(a, b, v) = \frac{v - a}{b - a}
$$

They are each other's inverse, and `remap` is simply the two composed:

$$
\text{remap}(v) = \text{lerp}\!\left(c, d, \text{inverseLerp}(a, b, v)\right)
$$

That one line removes most of the arithmetic from gameplay code. Stick position $-1 \ldots 1$ to a turn
rate of $-180 \ldots 180$. Health $0 \ldots 100$ to a bar width in pixels. Distance $2 \ldots 20$ to a
volume of $1 \ldots 0$, which is a fade-out written as one expression.

<DemoPanel name="2d-remap" />

:::caution[`lerp` is not clamped, and that is not a bug]
`lerp(0, 100, 2)` is $200$. Sometimes that is exactly right — continuing a trajectory, predicting
ahead of a target, extending a line. Sometimes it is a projectile leaving the level, a colour channel
above $1$, or a health bar wider than its frame. Know which one you are asking for, and reach for
`lerpClamped` or `remapClamped` when you meant the other.

`inverseLerp` on a zero-width range returns $0$ rather than dividing. Every value is simultaneously at
the start and the end of an empty range, so there is no right answer — but there is a wrong one, and
it is an `Infinity` that propagates into a position and makes a sprite disappear with no error.
:::

## The Gallery

One slider, six curves, one instant. The faint marks are **eleven equally spaced moments**, so their
spacing along each track is that curve's speed: bunched where it is slow, spread where it is fast.

<DemoPanel name="2d-gallery" />

Read it by the marks rather than by the shapes. `easeInQuad` has covered $6.3\%$ of the distance in
the first quarter of the time and $43.8\%$ in the last quarter; `easeOutQuad` is those two numbers
swapped. `linear` is $25\%$ in every quarter, which is why it is the only one with a sudden stop.

- **`easeOut` is the one to reach for by default.** Things that arrive and settle read as having mass.
- **`easeIn` is for leaving**, not arriving. Something easing _in_ to a stop looks broken.
- **`easeInOut` for anything that both starts and stops on screen.**
- **`linear` on purpose, not by omission** — a conveyor belt, a clock hand, a health bar draining.

## What "Smooth" Actually Means

Here is a claim worth being careful about, because the first version of this Section got it wrong:
smoothstep is often introduced as the curve with **zero slope at both ends**. It has that. So do
`easeInOutQuad`, `easeInOutCubic` and `smootherstep`. Zero end slope separates none of them.

What separates them is **curvature** — the second derivative, which is the acceleration a reader
feels as a lurch. All four are measured against their closed forms across the interval at build time:

| Curve            | Curvature at the ends | In the middle             | Peak speed    |
| :--------------- | :-------------------- | :------------------------ | :------------ |
| `linear`         | $0$                   | $0$                       | $1.0\times$   |
| `easeInOutQuad`  | $4$                   | jumps $+4 \to -4$         | $2.0\times$   |
| `easeInOutCubic` | $0$                   | jumps $+11.76 \to -11.76$ | $3.0\times$   |
| `smoothstep`     | $6$                   | passes through $0$        | $1.5\times$   |
| `smootherstep`   | $0$                   | passes through $0$        | $1.875\times$ |

$$
\text{smoothstep}(t) = 3t^2 - 2t^3
\qquad
\text{smootherstep}(t) = 6t^5 - 15t^4 + 10t^3
$$

Two things fall out of that table. **Smoothstep is a single polynomial**, so its curvature slides
smoothly through zero in the middle instead of flipping sign, and its peak speed is $1.5\times$ the
average rather than $2\times$ — the same distance in the same time with less of a surge. And
**smootherstep is the one with zero curvature at the ends**; smoothstep's is exactly $6$, so its
acceleration still arrives as a step even though its speed does not.

Which to use: smoothstep, almost always. Smootherstep when the eased value is itself differentiated —
a camera whose velocity feeds something else — and you are willing to pay $1.875\times$ peak speed for
it. The gentler ends have to be paid for in the middle; there is no free smoothness.

:::tip[The other reason smoothstep is worth knowing]
It is the same function GLSL and HLSL provide, with the same name and the same definition, and it
takes two edges rather than a plain $t$: `smoothstep(edge0, edge1, x)` does the `inverseLerp` and the
clamp for you. So the shader version and the gameplay version of a fade can be the same curve, which
matters more than it sounds like it should when they are meant to line up.
:::

## The Ones That Leave The Range

Three curves overshoot, and the build measures each peak instead of quoting it from a reference:

- **`easeOutBack` peaks at $1.100004$**, at $t = 0.580$. That $10\%$ is what the famous $1.70158$
  constant is _for_: rounding it to $1.7$ gives $1.099843$, and $2$ gives $1.131687$. The digits buy a
  round number in the output, which is the number somebody was actually choosing.
- **`easeOutElastic` peaks at $1.373098$** and crosses the target seven times on its way to settling.
  It is a strong effect and it wears out its welcome fast.
- **`easeOutBounce` never exceeds $1$ at all.** It reaches the target four times — at $t = 0.3636$,
  $0.7273$, $0.9091$ and $1$ — falling back by $0.25$, then $0.0625$, then $0.015625$ between them.
  Each dip is **exactly a quarter** of the one before, which is where the dropped-object reading comes
  from.

So bounce is the safe one on a value with a hard ceiling: an opacity, a colour channel, a health
fraction, a UI width that must not exceed its container. Back and elastic on any of those are a bug
that only shows up at the moment the animation peaks.

:::note[The endpoint case in elastic is load-bearing]
`easeOutElastic` special-cases $t = 1$ and returns exactly $1$. That is not tidying. Left to the
formula alone it returns $1.00048828125$ at $t = 1$ — and there is nothing after $t = 1$ to bring it
back, so it stays there. The build keeps a copy of the bare formula next to the real one and asserts
both facts: that the copy lands past the target, and that the real one lands on it exactly. Deleting
the case on purpose is one of the ten sabotages this Section's check was tested against, and it was
caught by that assertion.
:::

## Writing One, And Getting The Pair For Free

An in and an out are mirror images:

$$
\text{out}(t) = 1 - \text{in}(1 - t)
$$

Worth having as a function rather than as a second hand-written formula. `reverse(easeInQuad)` and
`easeOutQuad` agree to the last bit across two thousand samples, and reversing any curve twice returns
the original — both asserted, so the pair can never drift apart under editing.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot has `lerp()`, `inverse_lerp()`, `remap()`, `clamp()` and `smoothstep()` as global functions, and
`Tween` with `set_ease()` and `set_trans()` for the curve families — `Tween.TRANS_BACK`,
`TRANS_ELASTIC`, `TRANS_BOUNCE` are the three above. `@export var curve: Curve` gives a designer a
hand-drawn one.

Unity has `Mathf.Lerp` (clamped), `Mathf.LerpUnclamped` (not), `Mathf.InverseLerp` and
`Mathf.SmoothStep`. There is no built-in `remap`; it is two lines. `AnimationCurve` is the
hand-authored equivalent, and `Mathf.SmoothDamp` is a spring rather than an easing — it belongs with
Section 4.1's damping, not here, because it has no duration and no exact arrival.

One sign trap worth naming: `Mathf.Lerp` clamps and `lerp` in most shader languages does not, so the
same expression ported between the two behaves differently the moment $t$ leaves $0 \ldots 1$.
:::

- **Any UI at all**: panels, fades, bars, counters, damage numbers.
- **The capstone**, where coyote time and jump buffering turn out to be interpolation problems.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
`lerp`, `inverseLerp`, `remap` and `clamp` remove most of the arithmetic from gameplay code, and
`lerp` is unclamped, which is a feature until it is a projectile leaving the level. On top of them,
the easing curve is what the motion _communicates_: `easeOut` to arrive with weight, `easeIn` to
leave, `easeInOut` for both. Prefer `smoothstep` among the S-curves — not for its zero end slope,
which three others share, but because it is one polynomial with no curvature kink in the middle and a
peak speed of $1.5\times$ rather than $2\times$. Check before overshooting: `easeOutBack` reaches
$1.1$ and `easeOutElastic` $1.37$, so neither belongs on an opacity, while `easeOutBounce` never
leaves the range. And keep easing separate from Section 4.1's damping — easing needs a duration and
lands exactly on time, damping chases a moving target and never quite arrives.
:::
