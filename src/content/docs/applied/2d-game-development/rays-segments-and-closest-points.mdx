## What You'll Learn

$$
P(t) = A + t\,(B - A)
$$

That is a line, a ray **and** a segment. The only difference between the three is how far $t$ is allowed
to go:

| Shape       | Range of $t$    | What it is                      |
| :---------- | :-------------- | :------------------------------ |
| **Line**    | anything        | infinite in both directions     |
| **Ray**     | $t \ge 0$       | starts somewhere, never returns |
| **Segment** | $0 \le t \le 1$ | two ends                        |

Nearly every bug in this Section is a place where the wrong one of those three was used. A wall read as
a line blocks a view from across the level. A ray read as a line hits things behind the shooter. A
segment read as a line is nearest to a point that is nowhere near it.

Notice also that this is `lerp` from Section 4.2, applied to points. A segment **is** an interpolation
between its ends — and unlike Section 4.3's Bezier parameter, this one _is_ proportional to distance,
which the build checks.

## The Closest Point: Project, Then Clamp

$$
t = \frac{(P - A) \cdot (B - A)}{|B - A|^2}
$$

A dot product over a squared length — Section 1.4's projection, divided so the answer comes out as a
fraction of the way along. Then **clamp $t$ into the shape's range** and evaluate.

You have done this before. Section 5.1 found the nearest point on a box by clamping a **coordinate**
into a range. This clamps a **parameter** into a range. Same move, one dimension over, and that is
worth noticing because it is the shape of almost every closest-point problem.

<DemoPanel name="2d-nearest" />

The shaded regions are where $t$ leaves $0 \ldots 1$. Drag into one and the two answers come apart:
green stays on the wall, red runs off up the wall's imaginary continuation.

:::caution[Dropping the clamp gives a distance that is always too small]
Never too large. The infinite line contains every point the segment does and more, so it can only tie
or win — the build sweeps a grid confirming it never loses.

That one-sidedness makes the failure specific. **The unclamped test reports things as closer than they
are**, so it fires collisions that should not happen and passes range checks that should fail. Over the
demo's own view the clamp changes the answer at more than **half** of all positions.

And the worst case is not subtle. Step $0.01$ off the wall's line, well past its end, and the unclamped
answer says $0.01$ — all but touching — while the nearest actual wall is over three units away. That is
a factor of more than **300**, and it is what a bullet stopping in mid-air beyond the end of a wall
actually is.
:::

## Where Two Of Them Cross

With $r = B - A$ and $s = D - C$, both parameters come out of one division:

$$
t = \frac{(C - A) \times s}{r \times s}
\qquad
u = \frac{(C - A) \times r}{r \times s}
$$

That $\times$ is Section 2.1's 2D cross product — one number, $a_x b_y - a_y b_x$. Getting two answers
from one denominator is why this is the standard form.

**Then test both parameters against the ranges of the two shapes you actually have.** Two segments cross
when $0 \le t \le 1$ _and_ $0 \le u \le 1$. A ray hits a wall when $t \ge 0$ and $0 \le u \le 1$ — the
asymmetry is the whole point, and using the same test for both is how a ray ends up hitting things
behind the shooter.

<DemoPanel name="2d-param" />

Testing only $t$ is the specific mistake worth naming, because it looks like it works. In the demo above
a diagonal meets a short wall at $t = 0.667$ — comfortably in range — while $u = -1$, meaning the
crossing is a whole wall-length short of the wall itself. Check $t$ alone and that wall blocks
everything along its line, for ever.

## Line Of Sight, And What Dropping `u` Costs

<DemoPanel name="2d-sight" />

Each spoke around the guard asks: can I see a target six units out this way? Then tick the box, which
reads every wall as the infinite line it lies on.

|                              |          Clear arc |
| :--------------------------- | -----------------: |
| Walls as segments, correctly |          $301.76°$ |
| Walls as infinite lines      |          $172.34°$ |
| **Wrongly blocked**          | $\mathbf{129.42°}$ |

Three short walls, and reading them as lines throws away **129.42 degrees** of the guard's view — 43% of
everything it could actually see.

Those figures are derived in closed form and then checked against a sweep, which is worth doing rather
than trusting either alone. At six units only the nearest wall is reachable as a segment, so the honestly
blocked arc is simply the angle it subtends at the guard: $58.24°$. As lines, that wall's line blocks
$2\arccos(3/6) = 120°$ exactly, the second's blocks $180° - 2\arcsin(4.75/6) = 75.32°$, and they overlap
by $7.66°$ — a union of $187.66°$, leaving $172.34°$ clear.

:::note[The figure is meaningless without the distance]
That $129.42°$ is for targets **six units out**, which is why the scene gives you a slider for it. At
three units the two tests agree **exactly**: nothing that close sits behind any wall's extension. Push
the reach out and the arc grows.

This is not measurement noise; it is a real property of the question. A direction can be clear at three
units and blocked at six, so "how much of the view is blocked" has no answer until you say how far away
the target is. The build sweeps every radius the slider can reach and asserts each one behaves.
:::

Like Section 5.1's grown box, the error is **one-sided**: reading a wall as a line can only ever block a
view, never open one. The build sweeps 720 directions at four radii confirming it. So the wrong version
is usable as a cheap first pass — if the line test says "clear", it really is clear — and fatal as an
answer.

## The Two Divisions That Need Guarding

Both formulas above divide, and both denominators can be zero. Neither case throws.

**A segment with no length.** Its direction is the zero vector, so the projection is $0/0$. `projectionT`
returns `null` rather than `NaN`, because a `NaN` position propagates into everything it touches and
surfaces as a sprite that has vanished, with nothing in the console.

**Two parallel shapes.** The cross product $r \times s$ is zero, so `lineCrossing` returns `null`. Left
unguarded it produces `NaN` — and `NaN` compares false against every range test, so the hit is silently
**dropped** rather than reported. That matters most in the case that is not merely parallel but
**collinear**:

Two segments on the same line either share infinitely many points or none, and "where do they cross" has
no single answer. So the general formula cannot help, and a sight line lying exactly along a wall gets
reported as unblocked — a wall a character can walk through, but only when perfectly aligned with it.
Rare, perfectly reproducible, and maddening to find.

The fix is to ask a different question: project both onto the shared direction and compare the two
ranges. Which is Section 5.1's range check, doing its third job in three Sections.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot: `Geometry2D.get_closest_point_to_segment()` is the clamped version and
`get_closest_point_to_segment_uncapped()` is the unclamped one — the two are separate functions with
explicit names, which is the right design. `Geometry2D.segment_intersects_segment()` returns the point
or `null`, matching the guard above. For line of sight against real colliders,
`PhysicsDirectSpaceState2D.intersect_ray()` with `RayQueryParameters2D`, or a `RayCast2D` node.

Unity 2D: `Physics2D.Linecast` is segment-shaped (start and end) and `Physics2D.Raycast` takes a
direction with an optional `distance` — so the ray/segment distinction is in which function you call, and
picking the wrong one is this Section's bug with an API attached. There is no built-in
closest-point-on-segment for plain maths; `Vector3.Project` gives you the unclamped projection and the
clamp is yours to add.

Both engines' raycasts return the **nearest** hit by default, which is `firstBlocker` above. If you want
all of them, that is a separate call — `RaycastAll`, or `intersect_ray` in a loop with exclusions.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Section 5.3**, where the separating axis test projects polygons onto axes — the same projection, used
  to compare ranges rather than to find a point.
- **Section 5.4**, which needs the nearest point on a wall to work out which way to push a character off
  it, and needs it to be the nearest point on the **segment**.
- **Section 5.1's clamp**, generalised from a coordinate to a parameter.
- **Section 2.1's cross product**, doing the crossing arithmetic.
- **The capstone**, where a character's swept motion is a segment against tile edges.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
One equation, $P(t) = A + t(B - A)$, and three shapes that differ only in how far $t$ may go: anything
for a line, $t \ge 0$ for a ray, $0 \le t \le 1$ for a segment. The nearest point on a segment is the
projection with $t$ **clamped**, which is Section 5.1's clamp moved from a coordinate to a parameter, and
without it the answer is always too close — by a factor of 300 in the demo's worst case. Two shapes cross
where $t$ and $u$ both fall in their own ranges, and testing only $t$ makes a short wall block its whole
line: reading three walls as lines costs a guard $129.42°$ of view at six units out. Guard both divisions.
A zero-length segment has no direction, and two parallel shapes have no crossing point — and if you let
either produce a `NaN`, it will fail every comparison and drop the collision silently.
:::
