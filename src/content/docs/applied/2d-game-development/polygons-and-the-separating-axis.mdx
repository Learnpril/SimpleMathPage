## What You'll Learn

**If you can find one direction along which two shapes do not overlap, they miss.**

That is the entire algorithm, and you have used it twice already without it having a name. Section 5.1's
box test is exactly this with the directions fixed to $x$ and $y$: a gap on either axis proves a miss.
What changes here is only _which_ directions are worth trying.

For convex polygons the answer is short — **the directions perpendicular to their edges** — and that is
where 2D earns its place in this Module. In three dimensions the candidate axes are the face normals of
both shapes _plus_ the cross product of every pair of edges: fifteen axes for two boxes, and the reason
SAT has a reputation for being fiddly. In 2D there are no cross-product axes at all. Two triangles need
six, and every one of them is an edge turned ninety degrees.

## Shadows

Project a polygon onto a direction and you get a **shadow**: the lowest and highest it reaches along that
direction. Dot every corner against the axis, keep the smallest and largest.

$$
\text{shadow} = \left[\min_i (P_i \cdot \hat{n}),\ \max_i (P_i \cdot \hat{n})\right]
$$

Only the **corners** need testing, and that is convexity paying for itself: every point of a convex
polygon is a blend of its corners, so no interior point can reach further along any direction than the
furthest corner already does. The build checks that by projecting a grid of interior points and confirming
none escapes the corners' shadow.

Then compare the two shadows with a range check — the same `rangesOverlap` from Section 5.1, doing its
fourth job in three Sections: two boxes, two collinear segments, and now two polygon shadows.

Watch the readout as you drag. When the shapes are apart, the first axis tried is often enough and the
loop stops — at the opening position it is settled by **axis 1 of 8**. When they overlap, all eight have
to be checked before you can say so. **Overlapping is the expensive case**, which is the opposite of what
most people guess.

## Why The Edges Are Enough

The claim deserves a reason rather than a rule. If two convex shapes are apart, there is a straight line
you can draw between them. Slide that line toward one of the shapes until it cannot move any further and
it ends up lying flush against an edge. So the direction perpendicular to _that_ edge separates them —
which means some edge normal always works, and testing the edges is testing everything.

Two practical notes fall out of that:

**Opposite edges are redundant.** A rectangle's four normals point in only two directions, and a shadow
does not care which way up its axis is. Two rectangles offer eight axes and **two** distinct directions.
Skipping the duplicates is the first optimisation anyone makes, and it is worth knowing before you make
it that the duplicates are harmless, only wasteful.

**Winding decides which way the normals face.** For a counter-clockwise polygon the interior lies to the
left of each edge, so the _right_ perpendicular points outward. The hit-or-miss answer does not care — an
axis and its opposite cast identical shadows — but the push-out direction in Section 5.4 does, and a
polygon loaded from a file can be wound either way. Normalizing the winding once is cheaper than reasoning
about signs everywhere afterwards.

<DemoPanel name="2d-normals" />

:::caution[Normalize the axes, for a reason that is not the obvious one]
Whether the shapes overlap does **not** depend on an axis's length. Scaling a direction scales both
shadows by the same factor, so the sign of the overlap cannot change. Unnormalized axes give a perfectly
correct hit test, always — the build asserts that across forty thousand configurations, and it is why this
bug survives every test anyone writes for it.

What breaks is the **depth**. Overlap along an axis comes out in units of that axis's length, so comparing
depths across axes of different lengths compares different units — and "the shallowest overlap" then picks
the wrong axis. With the demo's pentagon and triangle that happens at **47.56%** of overlapping
configurations, and the chosen direction is out by as much as **81.41°**.

So: a correct collision test, and a character shoved sideways out of a wall it should have been pushed off
the front of. That is Section 5.4's problem arriving early.
:::

## The Minimum Translation Vector

When no axis separates them, the axis with the **smallest** overlap is the shortest way to push them
apart. Every axis measures a distance you could move one shape to end the overlap along that direction,
and the smallest of those is the least disruptive.

That is the minimum translation vector, and Section 5.4 is about what to do with it. Here it is only a
measurement — drawn as the purple arrow when the shapes touch.

## SAT Needs Convex Shapes, And Will Not Tell You Otherwise

<DemoPanel name="2d-concave" />

The square starts inside the notch. No corner of either shape is inside the other, no edges cross, and
nothing whatsoever is touching. The test reports an overlap.

That is not a bug in the code. `isConvex` comes from Section 2.1, and here it is a **precondition** — a
test built on shadows can only ever describe a convex region, so handed a concave polygon it answers about
something closer to the shape's convex hull. The dashed outline shows that hull, and the shaded cells are
every probe position where the answer is measurably wrong: **1.307 square units, 3.19%** of the area
sampled around the shape.

:::note[The hull bounds the lie without being it]
It is tempting to say SAT on a concave polygon just tests its convex hull. Not quite. A concave polygon
offers normals from its **reflex** edges too, and those are not hull edges — so it has extra axes to try,
and occasionally one of them does separate. The false region is therefore a _subset_ of hull-minus-shape,
which is why the build measures it by sampling rather than computing the hull's area and calling it done.

The same measurement on the chevron's own hull finds **zero** wrong positions, which is what makes the
3.19% attributable to concavity rather than to the probe or the sampling.
:::

The fix is not to patch SAT. It is to **decompose** the shape into convex pieces — a chevron is two
quadrilaterals — and test each. Every engine does this, and it is why a physics editor makes you draw
convex collision shapes rather than tracing your sprite.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot: `ConvexPolygonShape2D` is the shape SAT applies to, and the name is a warning rather than a
description. `CollisionPolygon2D` has a `build_mode` of `Solids` or `Segments`, and a concave outline
becomes multiple convex pieces internally. `Geometry2D.decompose_polygon_in_convex()` does that
decomposition explicitly if you need it yourself, and
`Geometry2D.convex_hull()` is the hull above.

Unity 2D: `PolygonCollider2D` accepts concave outlines and splits them into convex paths behind the
scenes; the `points` you set and the `pathCount` you get back are not the same thing, which is this
Section's constraint showing through the API. `PhysicsShapeGroup2D` exposes the pieces.

Both engines cap the number of vertices on a convex shape — the axis count grows with it, and so does the
cost of the overlapping case. If you are hitting that cap, the answer is usually fewer, simpler shapes
rather than one detailed one.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Section 5.4**, which takes the minimum translation vector and turns it into a push and a slide.
- **Section 5.1's box test**, which is this algorithm with the axes fixed to $x$ and $y$.
- **Section 5.1's range check**, still doing the comparison.
- **Section 1.4's dot product**, doing every projection.
- **Section 2.1's cross product**, deciding convexity and spotting parallel axes.
- **Rotated hitboxes generally** — the moment a collision shape can turn, this is the test you need.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
One direction with a gap in it proves two convex shapes miss, and for polygons the directions worth trying
are just the edge normals — no cross-product axes, which is why 2D is where this idea is learnable. Project
by dotting the corners, compare the shadows with a range check, and stop at the first gap: missing is
cheap, overlapping is what costs you every axis. Normalize the axes, not because the verdict needs it but
because the **depth** does — unnormalized, the hit test stays perfectly correct while the shallowest-overlap
direction goes wrong by up to $81.41°$. And check convexity, because SAT on a concave shape reports
overlaps that are not there, silently, over $3.19\%$ of the area around it. Decompose into convex pieces
instead of patching the test.
:::
