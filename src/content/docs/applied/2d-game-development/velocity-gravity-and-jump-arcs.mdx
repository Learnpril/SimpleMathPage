## What You'll Learn

A physics step is two lines:

```js
v += a * dt;
p += v * dt;
```

No calculus, and no need for any. But the **order of those two lines changes the answer**, both orders are
wrong, and they are wrong by exactly the same amount in opposite directions — so their average is exact.
That last fact is free, and it is the difference between a jump that lands on the height you asked for and
one that lands near it.

The second half of the Section is the parameterisation. Nobody wants to choose a gravity constant. They want
a jump two units high that takes four tenths of a second to reach the top. Those are the same thing, and
inverting the relationship is the move Section 4.1 made when it exposed a half-life instead of a decay rate.

## Two Orders, Both Wrong

**Explicit Euler** moves by the old velocity, then updates it. It crosses the whole step at the velocity from
the _start_, so under gravity it travels too far upward.

**Semi-implicit Euler** updates the velocity first, then moves by the new one. It crosses the whole step at
the velocity from the _end_, so it comes out short. This is what nearly every engine does, chosen for its
stability with springs and damping rather than its accuracy here.

Each is off by

$$
\tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,\Delta t^2\,n \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,\Delta t\,t
$$

in opposite directions. Two things worth reading off that: the error grows **linearly** in time, not
quadratically, and it is proportional to the step size — halve the timestep and you halve the drift.

The purple crosses are the average of the orange and blue traces, and they land on the grey curve. That is
not approximately true; the build asserts three separate forms of it to floating-point dust across several
accelerations and step sizes:

- the two errors sum to zero
- their average equals the closed form $p_0 + v_0t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$
- **the midpoint step is that average, computed directly**

## The Fix Costs One Multiply

$$
p \mathrel{+}= \left(v + \tfrac{1}{2}a\,\Delta t\right)\Delta t
$$

Use the _average_ of the start and end velocities to cross the step, which is what the velocity actually
averages when acceleration is constant. For constant acceleration this is **not an approximation** — it
reproduces the closed form exactly at every step, at every step size. Also called velocity Verlet.

One multiply and one add. There is no accuracy-versus-speed trade here to agonise over.

:::note[Why engines still ship semi-implicit Euler]
Because acceleration is usually _not_ constant. A spring's force depends on position, so the "average
velocity over the step" is no longer known in advance and the midpoint trick stops being exact. Semi-implicit
Euler is chosen for being **stable** under those forces — it does not blow up — and stability matters more
than accuracy when a simulation might otherwise explode.

Gravity, though, is constant. If the only acceleration on your projectile is gravity, the exact version is
available and free.
:::

## A Jump You Can Describe

Solve the closed form twice — the apex is where $v_0 + at = 0$, and the height there is $-v_0^2/2a$ — and you
get the two numbers a loop needs from the two a person can picture:

$$
v_0 = \frac{2h}{t_{\text{up}}}
\qquad
a = -\frac{2h}{t_{\text{up}}^2}
$$

So "two units high, four tenths of a second to the top" is a launch speed of $10$ and a gravity of $-25$.
The round trip is exact, and the build checks it both ways on five different jumps.

<DemoPanel name="2d-airtime" />

This is the whole reason to do the algebra. `gravity = -25` is a number nobody can picture; a jump height and
a rise time can be tuned by someone who has never opened the physics code.

## The Jump That Comes Out Short

<DemoPanel name="2d-leap" />

Ask for two units at 60 fps with semi-implicit Euler and you get **1.9167** — short by $0.0833$, which is
**4.17%** of the jump. The drift formula predicts that number before the arc is stepped. Explicit Euler
overshoots by exactly the same amount. The midpoint step lands on $2$.

Drop the frame rate in the scene and watch the gap widen: at 10 fps that same jump reaches $1.5$, a quarter
short.

:::caution[There are two different reasons a jump comes out short, and only one is fixable]
I wrote an assertion that the midpoint step always reaches the requested height. It failed at 10 fps on a
$0.25$ s rise — and the code was right.

The apex falls **halfway between frames 2 and 3**. The integrator is exact at every step it takes; it simply
never takes one at the top. That is a sampling problem, not a drift problem, and it costs

$$
\tfrac{1}{2}|a|\,\delta^2
$$

where $\delta$ is the gap to the nearest frame. **No integrator can fix it**, because nothing is being
computed incorrectly.

The two have different signatures, which is how you tell them apart: drift is **linear** in the step size and
sampling is **quadratic** in it. Both are asserted. It is the same distinction Section 4.1 drew between
frame-rate dependence and sampling coarseness, and it took a failing test to notice it here.
:::

## Terminal Velocity Is Section 4.1 Again

Gravity with linear drag:

$$
\frac{dv}{dt} = a - kv
\qquad
v_\infty = \frac{a}{k}
$$

The speed at which drag exactly cancels gravity. With a gravity of $-25$ and a drag of $4$, terminal velocity
is $-6.25$, and the approach to it is

$$
v(t) = v_\infty + (v_0 - v_\infty)e^{-kt}
$$

which is **`decay` from Section 4.1 with different labels on the variables**. Same equation, same
frame-rate independence, same exponential approach that never quite arrives. Noticing that the two are the
same thing is worth more than either alone.

:::tip[Or just clamp the fall speed]
`Math.max(v, -limit)` is one comparison. Not physical, and often the better choice: it gives an exact maximum
fall speed you can design a level around, and it **never touches a rise**.

Drag does. Drag slows the ascent as well, so a jump tuned before adding it comes out short afterwards — which
is the Section's own theme arriving from a third direction.
:::

## Two Things Platformers Do That Physics Does Not

**Different gravity going up and coming down.** Each half is its own parabola through the same apex, so each
half gets the same formula with its own time. Rising in $0.4$ s and falling in $0.28$ s makes the fall
$2.0408$ times stronger — which is $(0.4/0.28)^2$, and knowing that the ratio is a _square_ is what makes
tuning it by feel less mysterious. Completely unphysical, and it is what makes a jump feel controllable.

**Cutting the jump short on release.** Scale the upward velocity down. The thing to know is that height goes
as the **square** of the velocity, so keeping half the speed keeps a **quarter** of the height — which is why
a tapped jump feels so much shorter than expected. And guard it: applied while falling, it would slow the
fall, which reads as the character snagging on something.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot: `CharacterBody2D` gives you `velocity` and expects you to integrate it yourself, so all of the above
applies directly — `velocity.y += gravity * delta` is semi-implicit Euler and comes out short. The project
setting is `physics/2d/default_gravity`. `RigidBody2D` integrates for you and
`linear_damp` is the drag above. Godot's own docs use the semi-implicit form in every example, which is worth
knowing rather than copying blindly.

Unity: `Rigidbody2D.gravityScale` scales `Physics2D.gravity` per body, and `linearDamping` is the drag term.
Unity's integrator is semi-implicit, and `Rigidbody2D.velocity` is read after the step. For a character
controller you are integrating by hand again, in `FixedUpdate` where `Time.fixedDeltaTime` is constant —
which is Section 6.1's arithmetic with a guaranteed `dt`.

Both engines run physics on a **fixed** timestep, which is what makes the drift a constant offset you can
tune around rather than something that varies with the frame rate. That is why it usually goes unnoticed:
it is consistently wrong, and consistency is most of what feels right.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **The capstone**, where the character's jump is tuned by height and airtime and integrated on a fixed step.
- **Section 5.4's tunnelling**, which is this Section's velocity meeting that Section's thin wall.
- **Section 4.1's delta time and decay**, which terminal velocity turns out to be.
- **Section 4.3's arc**, which was a Bezier where this one is a parabola — and a parabola _is_ a quadratic
  Bezier, which is worth noticing.
- **Anything thrown, dropped, or launched.**

:::note[The one thing to take away]
Two lines of arithmetic, and their order matters: explicit Euler overshoots, semi-implicit undershoots, and
both are off by $\tfrac{1}{2}a\,\Delta t\,t$ — linear in time, proportional to the step. Their average is the
closed form **exactly**, and the midpoint step $p \mathrel{+}= (v + \tfrac{1}{2}a\Delta t)\Delta t$ is that
average for one extra multiply. Specify jumps as a height and a rise time, not as a gravity constant:
$v_0 = 2h/t$ and $a = -2h/t^2$. Expect the stepped result to be short — $4.17\%$ at 60 fps for a two-unit
jump — and know that part of any remaining shortfall is **sampling**, not drift, because the apex may fall
between frames where nothing can observe it. Terminal velocity is $a/k$ and its approach is Section 4.1's
decay; a fall-speed clamp is the blunt alternative that never slows a rise. And cutting a jump short scales
the height by the **square** of what you scale the velocity by.
:::
