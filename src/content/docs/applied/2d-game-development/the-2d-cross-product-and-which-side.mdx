## What You'll Learn

The number the dot product could not give you: which side. One subtraction of two products, and it
answers left or right, how far off a line something is, how big a polygon is, and which way round its
corners were listed. In three dimensions this is the operation people find hardest. Here it is a
single number.

## One Number, Two Readings

$$
a \times b = a_x b_y - a_y b_x
$$

Note what it is **not**: it is not a vector. The 3D cross product returns one, which is where its
right-hand rules and handedness conventions come from. In two dimensions there is no third axis for
the result to point along, so what you get back is a plain number.

The meaning, as with the dot product, lives in the other way of writing it:

$$
a \times b = |a|\,|b|\sin\theta
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle **from $a$ to $b$**, counter-clockwise. Put the two side by side and the
division of labour is exact:

| Question                    | Operation    | Contains     | Symmetric?            |
| :-------------------------- | :----------- | :----------- | :-------------------- |
| do these agree in direction | $a \cdot b$  | $\cos\theta$ | yes, so no left/right |
| which way would I turn      | $a \times b$ | $\sin\theta$ | no, so it knows       |

Cosine is symmetric — $\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$ — which is precisely why Section 1.4 ended with
`acos` unable to tell a target $40°$ to your left from one $40°$ to your right. Sine is not:
$\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta$. **The asymmetry is the information.**

So swapping the arguments flips the sign, and gets checked at every angle rather than asserted here:
$1$ for $x \times y$, $-1$ for $y \times x$.

## The Sign Is a Side

Given a line running from $A$ to $B$, and a point $P$:

$$
(B - A) \times (P - A)
$$

Positive means $P$ is to the left of the direction of travel, negative means right, zero means on the
line.

<DemoPanel name="2d-side" />

Sweep the point's $y$ and watch the sign change exactly as it crosses. Nothing about the crossing is
special-cased; the number simply passes through zero.

Two things this test is worth being careful about.

**"Left" is relative to the line's direction, not to the world.** Tick the last box and every answer
inverts, because the same two endpoints read backwards is a line pointing the other way. This is the
first thing to check when a which-side test comes out consistently inverted — the geometry is
probably fine and the edge is probably reversed.

**Exactly zero is decided by rounding.** The same caveat as a dot product of zero: a sweep of
**58,564** positions across four line angles put exactly **124** of them on the line. Do not build
anything that needs the zero case to appear.

The sign test is verified against a completely different piece of arithmetic — a signed angle from
`atan2`, which Section 2.2 covers — over that whole sweep, with zero disagreements and a symmetric
$29{,}220$ left against $29{,}220$ right. Two unrelated methods agreeing is worth more than one
method matching what I expected.

## A Quarter Turn, For Free

Rotating by $90°$ needs no trigonometry at all. Swap the components, negate one:

$$
\text{perpLeft}(v) = (-v_y,\; v_x) \qquad \text{perpRight}(v) = (v_y,\; -v_x)
$$

Which one is which follows from the cross product: $v \times \text{perpLeft}(v)$ is positive, so
`perpLeft` really is the counter-clockwise one. Section 2.3 derives the general rotation formula, and
this is that formula with $\cos 90° = 0$ and $\sin 90° = 1$ substituted in — which is checked, because
"obviously the same" is how sign errors survive.

A wall's normal is this. So is the axis a separating-axis test needs in Section 5.3.

## How Far Off the Line

The cross product carries a distance too, once you divide out the line's length:

$$
d = \frac{(B - A) \times (P - A)}{|B - A|}
$$

The cross product is twice the area of triangle $ABP$; dividing by the base leaves the height. **One
number that carries both the side and the distance**, which is why it turns up in every
line-of-sight, lane-following and "is the player past the finish line" check.

That formula is checked against a brute-force minimum over sixty thousand sampled points along the
line rather than against itself rearranged, because a rearrangement of a wrong formula is still wrong.

## The Magnitude Is an Area

$|a \times b|$ is the area of the parallelogram the two vectors span, and half of it is the triangle:

$$
\text{parallelogram} = |a \times b| \qquad \text{triangle} = \frac{|a \times b|}{2}
$$

No square roots, no trigonometry, no separate base and height. Turn the shading on in the demo above
and that parallelogram is the one being measured.

Zero area means the two vectors are parallel, which sounds like a curiosity and is not: it is how you
detect two segments that will never meet, and Section 5.2 leans on it directly.

## Winding Order

Extend the area idea around a closed shape and you get the **shoelace formula**: sum the cross
products of consecutive corners and halve it.

$$
2A = \sum_i \left(x_i y_{i+1} - y_i x_{i+1}\right)
$$

Keep the sign and it tells you which way round the corners were listed. Positive is
counter-clockwise.

<DemoPanel name="2d-shoelace" />

Every term is a triangle fanned out from the origin, with the parts outside the shape cancelling
against the parts inside — so the origin can be anywhere at all, inside the polygon or a hundred units
away, and the answer does not move. The panel shows that rather than claiming it.

Winding matters more than it looks like it should. It decides which way a polygon's edge normals face,
whether a separating-axis test finds the outside or the inside, and whether a shape ends up
inside-out. **A wrongly wound shape usually does not error.** It just turns itself inside out, quietly,
which is this module's version of the mirrored-basis problem the 3D module has.

:::caution[The canvas reverses it]
Counter-clockwise means counter-clockwise **in world coordinates, with Y up**. A canvas has Y down, so
the same list of corners appears to wind the other way once drawn. Nothing about the shape changed.

This is checked, and the check pins both halves of it: the rectangle that is counter-clockwise in the
world comes out clockwise in pixels, and its area grows by exactly the square of the pixels-per-unit
scale. If your winding tests pass on paper and fail on screen, this is why, and Section 1.1 is the
Section that explains it.
:::

## Three Sides Make an Inside

Ask "which side" of all three edges of a triangle. If the answers agree, the point is inside.

That is three cross products, no division, and it works for either winding, because it only asks
whether the three signs match rather than what they are. Checked against barycentric coordinates —
different arithmetic, same question — over **32,361** grid cells with no disagreements. Counting the
cells that came out inside gives an area of $16.05$ against the formula's exact $16$, which is a
third, independent way of arriving at the same shape.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot spells it `Vector2.cross()`, and it returns a float, exactly as above. `Vector2.orthogonal()` is
the free quarter turn.

Unity has no `Vector2.Cross`, which catches people out. Write it yourself as
`a.x * b.y - a.y * b.x`, or use `Vector3.Cross(a, b).z`, which is the same number arrived at
expensively. `Vector2.Perpendicular()` is the quarter turn.

Both engines' polygon helpers care about winding — `Geometry2D.is_polygon_clockwise()` in Godot is
worth knowing before you debug a triangulation that came out hollow.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Which side of a wall, a lane or a finish line** something is, which is one cross product.
- **Turning the right way**: the sign says which direction to rotate, and Section 2.3 uses it.
- **Polygon winding and convexity**, and therefore every collision routine in Part 5.
- **Segment intersection**, which is four which-side tests. Section 5.2.
- **The separating axis test**, whose axes are the free perpendiculars above. Section 5.3.
- **Triangle areas and point-in-triangle**, used for hit testing and for navigation meshes.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
The 2D cross product is $a_xb_y - a_yb_x$, a single number, and it equals $|a||b|\sin\theta$. Because
sine is not symmetric, the sign answers the question the dot product cannot: which side, or
equivalently which way to turn. Left is relative to the line's direction, so reversing the endpoints
inverts every answer. Divide by the line's length and the same number becomes a signed distance.
Without dividing, its size is the area of a parallelogram — sum it around a polygon and you have the
shoelace formula, whose sign is the winding, which the canvas's flipped Y-axis reverses on screen.
:::
