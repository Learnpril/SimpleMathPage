## What You'll Learn

Circles and axis-aligned boxes. Between them they cover almost every collision a 2D game actually
ships, and there are only three pairings to learn: circle–circle, box–box, circle–box.

The reason that is three lessons and not three formulas is that **all three are one distance and one
comparison**, and the third reduces to the first by a single clamp. Once you see that, collision stops
being a list of cases to memorise.

This Section answers only "did they touch". Working out how deep the overlap is and which way to push
is Section 5.4, and keeping the two questions apart is most of what makes collision code readable.

## Circle Against Circle

$$
(b_x - a_x)^2 + (b_y - a_y)^2 < (r_a + r_b)^2
$$

Compare the distance between the centres against the sum of the radii. **Squared on both sides**, so
there is no square root anywhere — Section 1.3's shortcut, and this is where it earns its keep, because
a broad-phase check runs this thousands of times a frame.

Strictly less than, so circles that touch at exactly one point are **not** overlapping. That is a
decision rather than a fact, and it is the one to make: standing on the ground is touching, and a test
that calls that a collision leaves a character permanently colliding with the floor it is resting on.

:::caution[Square the sum, not the radii]

$$
(r_a + r_b)^2 = r_a^2 + 2r_ar_b + r_b^2
$$

Writing `ra*ra + rb*rb` drops the $2r_ar_b$, and that term is usually most of the total. For two circles
of equal radius the reach shrinks from $2r$ to $r\sqrt{2}$ — a shortfall of **29.29%**, so contact is not
reported until the circles are already well inside each other.

It never crashes and it never produces a wrong-looking number. It reads as "collision feels late", which
is why it survives.
:::

## Box Against Box

Two axis-aligned boxes overlap only if they overlap on **both** axes. It is easier to get right stated
negatively: they miss if there is **any** axis on which one lies entirely to one side of the other.

$$
\text{overlap} \iff (a_{\min x} < b_{\max x} \wedge b_{\min x} < a_{\max x}) \wedge (\text{same for } y)
$$

That sentence — one separating axis is enough to prove a miss — is the whole of Section 5.3, which does
the same thing for shapes whose axes are not the world's. Seeing the box test as "check every axis"
rather than as four inequalities makes that Section a small step instead of a new subject.

<DemoPanel name="2d-shapes" />

Note what "axis-aligned" is buying. The box's edges are always parallel to the axes, so no test here
ever considers an angle. A box that can rotate costs a great deal more, and that is why so many games
give a rotating sprite an upright collision box and accept the mismatch.

:::caution[An inverted box collides with nothing, silently]
Every test here assumes `min` really is the smaller corner. A box built from two dragged points often
fails that, and then it reports **no collision with anything, ever**. A wall becomes a doorway, and
there is no error, no `NaN` and nothing in the console. Run anything user-built or file-loaded through a
normalizing step, which is two `Math.min` calls.

Related, and just as quiet: min/max and centre/half-extents are both common ways to store a box and they
are not interchangeable. Godot's `Rect2` is position and size, Unity's `Bounds` is centre and extents.
Pass one where the other belongs and you get a box of the wrong size in the wrong place — which still
looks exactly like a box.
:::

## Circle Against Box, Which Is Circle Against Circle

Here is the good part. **Clamp the circle's centre into the box**, one axis at a time:

$$
q = \left(\text{clamp}(p_x, \min_x, \max_x),\ \text{clamp}(p_y, \min_y, \max_y)\right)
$$

That gives the point of the box nearest the circle's centre. Then it is a distance against a radius —
test one, unchanged.

Two clamps. That is the entire thing, and it is worth pausing on why it works: the axes are
**independent**, so the nearest allowed $x$ cannot depend on $y$. It handles all three cases — nearest
to a face, nearest to a corner, and the centre already inside the box — with no branch distinguishing
them. The build checks it against a brute-force scan of the box's whole perimeter.

## The Corner Bug

Now the mistake nearly everybody makes first, because it is simpler and it is nearly right: **grow the
box by the radius and test whether the centre is inside it.**

<DemoPanel name="2d-corners" />

Growing a rectangle by $r$ gives you **square** corners. The region where a circle's centre can sit and
actually touch the box has **rounded** ones, of radius exactly $r$. So the naive test reports a hit
while the circle is still short of the corner, touching nothing: a shot that stops in mid-air near a
crate, which usually gets blamed on the renderer.

The size of the mistake is not a matter of opinion. The false region is four corner squares minus four
quarter-discs:

$$
4r^2 - \pi r^2 = (4 - \pi)\,r^2 \approx 0.8584\,r^2
$$

**Independent of the box entirely.** The build measures that area by counting grid cells where the two
tests disagree and compares the count against the formula — two completely different computations,
agreeing to under half a percent.

And at a corner specifically, the over-reach has a clean value. The grown box's corner sits $r\sqrt{2}$
from the real corner while the true region reaches only $r$, so the naive test accepts circles up to

$$
r\left(\sqrt{2} - 1\right) \approx 0.4142\,r
$$

past the point of contact — **41.42% of the radius, whatever the radius, whatever the box.**

Along a face the naive test is exactly right, which is precisely why it survives. If you test it by
walking a circle straight at a wall, it passes.

:::tip[It errs in one direction only]
The naive test never misses a real overlap; it only invents ones that are not there. The build sweeps a
grid confirming that, and it is worth knowing rather than assuming: it means the naive version is safe
as a **broad phase** — a cheap first pass that may over-report, followed by the real test on whatever
survives. Used that way it is not a bug at all. Used as the answer, it is.
:::

## Where The Square Roots Went

None of the three tests needs one. Circle–circle squares the reach, box–box only compares coordinates,
and circle–box squares the radius. That matters less than it used to for a handful of shapes and matters
a great deal at the scale collision runs at — and it costs nothing in clarity once the habit is to
square the threshold rather than root the distance.

The trap is that **a squared distance is not a distance**. Compare it against a squared threshold or the
numbers are nonsense, and it is nonsense that looks plausible: a radius of 5 quietly becomes a radius of
25 rather than an error.

:::note[In an engine]
Godot: `Rect2.intersects()` for box–box, `Rect2.has_point()` for containment, and `Rect2.grow()` is
literally the naive expansion above — useful, and not a circle test. Real circle collision goes through
`CircleShape2D` and the physics server, or `Geometry2D` helpers for one-off queries.
`Rect2.abs()` is the normalizing step, and it exists because inverted rectangles are common enough to
need it.

Unity 2D: `Bounds.Intersects()`, `Bounds.Contains()`, and `Bounds.ClosestPoint()` — that last one is the
two clamps, already written. `Physics2D.OverlapCircle` is the engine-side version.
`Bounds` is centre-and-extents, and `extents` is the **half** size while `size` is the full one; mixing
them up is the representation bug above with a specific name.

Both engines separate a cheap bounding-volume pass from an exact one, which is the broad-phase idea in
the tip above rather than an implementation detail.
:::

:::note[The one thing to take away]
Three pairings, one idea. Circle–circle compares centre distance against the sum of the radii, squared on
both sides so there is no square root — and square the **sum**, because dropping the $2r_ar_b$ costs
29.29% of the reach for equal radii. Box–box is two range checks, and one separating axis is enough to
prove a miss, which is Section 5.3 in a sentence. Circle–box is circle–circle with the centre clamped
into the box first, two lines handling face, corner and inside with no branch. Do **not** instead grow
the box by the radius: that gives square corners where the truth is rounded, over-reporting by
$(4-\pi)r^2$ of area and reaching $0.4142\,r$ past contact at every corner. And normalize any box whose
corners came from a drag, because an inverted box collides with nothing and says nothing.
:::
