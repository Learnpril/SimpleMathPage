## What You'll Learn

One number, built from two multiplications and an add, that answers "do these two directions
agree?" It does three jobs that look unrelated: a facing test, a vision cone, and splitting a
vector into two pieces. Then the two ways it goes wrong, both of which ship regularly.

## Two Ways to Write the Same Number

Multiply the matching components and add the results:

$$
a \cdot b = a_x b_x + a_y b_y
$$

That is the computation. This is what it means:

$$
a \cdot b = |a|\,|b|\cos\theta
$$

**Nothing in the code computes an angle**, and yet the answer contains one. That gap is the whole
reason the dot product is everywhere in game code: it is the cheap way to ask an expensive question.

Read the second form and everything else follows. The lengths are always positive, so the **sign
comes entirely from $\cos\theta$**:

| Angle between them | $\cos\theta$ | Dot product   | Meaning        |
| :----------------- | :----------- | :------------ | :------------- |
| $0°$               | $1$          | largest       | same direction |
| under $90°$        | positive     | positive      | roughly agree  |
| exactly $90°$      | $0$          | zero          | perpendicular  |
| over $90°$         | negative     | negative      | roughly oppose |
| $180°$             | $-1$         | most negative | opposite       |

## The Sign Test, and What It Actually Says

"Is the enemy in front of me?" Dot your facing with the displacement to the enemy. Positive means
yes.

This is the cheapest useful thing in the whole track. **No normalizing, no square root, no
trigonometry** — lengths cannot change a sign, only a magnitude, so scaling either vector leaves the
answer alone.

The trap is what "in front" means here. Positive dot means **within 90°**, which is a $180°$ wedge —
half the entire plane. Something directly at your shoulder passes this test. The build-time check
counts it: of 360 directions around the guard, the sign test accepts **181 of them**.

If you wanted a narrow cone, you need a threshold, not a sign.

That count should be 180, and the extra one is worth a moment. At exactly $\pm 90°$ the dot product
comes out as $2.4 \times 10^{-16}$ rather than $0$, because `Math.cos(Math.PI / 2)` is not exactly
zero. So both perpendicular directions land on the positive side. **Exactly perpendicular is decided
by rounding**, which is fine here — nothing depends on which way a knife edge falls — but it is worth
knowing before you write a test that expects a dot product to equal zero.

## A Vision Cone Is One Comparison

Normalize both directions and the lengths drop out of $|a||b|\cos\theta$, leaving the cosine of the
angle by itself. So compare it against the cosine of the angle you actually want:

$$
\hat{f} \cdot \hat{t} \ge \cos\phi
$$

where $\phi$ is the cone's half-angle. **Still no `acos`.** You wanted to know whether an angle is
small enough, and comparing cosines answers that without ever producing the angle.

One thing about this runs backwards from intuition. Cosine _falls_ as the angle grows, so **a wider
cone is a smaller threshold**:

| Half-angle | Threshold | Cone is       |
| ---------: | --------: | :------------ |
|      $15°$ |     0.966 | very narrow   |
|      $45°$ |     0.707 | typical guard |
|      $60°$ |     0.500 | generous      |
|      $90°$ |     0.000 | the sign test |

The last row is worth noticing: the sign test isn't a different technique, it is this one with the
cone opened to its widest.

Sweep the target's angle with the checkbox left on, and the answer changes exactly where the cone
edge is. Sweep the distance and nothing changes at all — as it should not, because distance is not
an angle.

Now uncheck the box.

## Forgetting to Normalize Is the Bug Worth Remembering

With the normalize removed, the code compares the raw dot product against the threshold. That dot
product is $d\cos\theta$, where $d$ is the distance — so **it grows as the target walks away**.

Hold the angle at $60°$, well outside a $45°$ cone, and watch:

| Distance | Raw dot | Passes $\ge 0.707$? | The honest answer |
| -------: | ------: | :------------------ | :---------------- |
|  $1.2$ m |   0.600 | no                  | no                |
|    $2$ m |   1.000 | **yes**             | no                |
|    $5$ m |   2.500 | **yes**             | no                |
|   $12$ m |   6.000 | **yes**             | no                |

The threshold has stopped meaning an angle. Solving $d\cos 60° \ge \cos 45°$ says the false
positives begin at exactly $\sqrt{2}$ meters, which the check pins to 12 decimal places.

**And it fails in the least helpful direction possible.** Close in it is correct, so it survives
testing in a corridor. Far away it is wrong, and the further away the target gets the more certain
the guard becomes that it can see them. A bug that improves under close inspection is the kind that
reaches players.

The correct version is verified against a genuinely different piece of arithmetic: an `acos`-based
angle comparison, at **57,600 positions**, with zero disagreements and both answers appearing. Two
implementations agreeing is worth more than one implementation matching my expectations.

## Projection: The Part That Goes That Way

Same formula, different question. **How far along a direction does this vector reach?**

$$
\text{along} = \frac{v \cdot d}{|d|}
$$

And when $d$ is already unit length the division vanishes, so the projection _is_ the dot product.
That is the main practical reason to keep unit vectors around.

<DemoPanel name="2d-project" />

The green arrow is the part of the vector that goes along the direction. The purple arrow is
everything left over. Notice two things as you move the sliders:

- **The two parts always add back to the original vector.** Exactly, not approximately.
- **They are always at a right angle to each other**, which is why their lengths satisfy
  Pythagoras with the original.

At $90°$ the green arrow disappears: the vector goes nowhere along the direction. Past $90°$ it
flips and the number goes negative, which is the sign test again, arriving from a different door.

This one split does more work later than anything else in Part 1. Sliding along a wall is throwing
away the part that goes into the wall and keeping the part that goes along it. Section 5.4 is
mostly this picture.

:::tip[Why divide by $d \cdot d$]
The vector projection is usually written

$$
\text{proj}_d\,v = \frac{v \cdot d}{d \cdot d}\,d
$$

which looks like a different formula but isn't. Dividing by $|d|$ once gives the length along $d$;
dividing a second time turns the $d$ on the right into a unit vector. Two divisions by $|d|$ is one
division by $|d|^2$, and $d \cdot d$ **is** $|d|^2$ — so this form gets you a normalized direction
without calling `normalize`.

Which means the projection doesn't care how long $d$ is. The check scales it by a million and the
answer doesn't move.
:::

## The Angle, and the Clamp That Is Not Optional

If you genuinely need the angle, rearrange the cosine form:

$$
\theta = \arccos\!\left(\frac{a \cdot b}{|a|\,|b|}\right)
$$

`Math.acos` is only defined on $[-1, 1]$. Outside that it returns `NaN`. And **rounding will put you
outside it.**

<DemoPanel name="2d-acos" />

Normalize a direction, dot it with itself, and you get `1.0000000000000002`. That is a legitimate
floating-point result and an impossible cosine, so `acos` gives up.

How often? Over 200,000 directions swept around a circle, the unclamped version returns `NaN`
**60,461 times — 30.2%**. This is not an edge case to guard against out of habit. It is the common
case, and the situation that triggers it most reliably is the most innocent one imaginable: asking
for the angle between a direction and itself.

So clamp before you call it:

```js
const cosine = dot(a, b) / (length(a) * length(b));
return Math.acos(Math.min(1, Math.max(-1, cosine)));
```

**A `NaN` is worse than a wrong number**, which is why this matters more than the size of the error
suggests. A wrong angle gives you a visibly odd result you can chase. A `NaN` compares `false`
against everything — `NaN > 0` and `NaN < 0` are both false — so every guard clause silently lets it
through, and it spreads to every number it touches.

:::note[`acos` cannot tell left from right]
The angle from `acos` runs from $0°$ to $180°$ and is **never signed**. A target $40°$ to your left
and one $40°$ to your right give the same answer, which the check confirms is exact rather than
approximate.

That is fine for a vision cone, which is symmetric anyway. It is not fine for "which way should I
turn", and no amount of care with `acos` will fix it — the information is not in the dot product.
Section 2.1 introduces the 2D cross product, which is the number that knows.
:::

:::note[In an engine]
Godot's `Vector2.dot()`, plus `angle_to()` which gives a **signed** angle and does the clamping for
you. If you are reaching for `acos` in Godot, `angle_to()` is almost always what you wanted.

Unity's `Vector2.Dot()`, with `Vector2.Angle()` for the unsigned angle and `Vector2.SignedAngle()`
for the signed one. Both handle the clamp internally.

`Vector3.Project` and `Vector3.ProjectOnPlane` in Unity are the projection and the leftover part,
which are the two halves of the split above. Godot spells them `Vector2.project()` and
`Vector2.slide()`.
:::

## Where This Shows Up

- **Vision cones and aggro checks**, which are one comparison and no trigonometry.
- **Facing tests** — is this behind me, is this wall pointing at me, is the door on my side.
- **Sliding along walls**, which is the split with one part discarded. Section 5.4.
- **Lighting**, where surface brightness is a dot product of a normal and a light direction.
- **Section 2.1**, the cross product, which supplies the "which side" the dot product cannot.

:::note[The one thing to take away]
The dot product is $a_xb_x + a_yb_y$, and it equals $|a||b|\cos\theta$ — so it measures agreement in
direction without computing an angle. Its sign alone answers "roughly the same way?", which is a
$180°$ wedge and not a vision cone. For a real cone, normalize both directions and compare against
$\cos$ of the half-angle, remembering that a wider cone means a smaller threshold. Skip that
normalize and the test starts passing targets that are further away, which is exactly backwards. And
if you ever call `acos` on a dot product, clamp it to $[-1, 1]$ first, because rounding pushes it
outside that range about 30% of the time and hands you a `NaN` that quietly poisons everything
downstream.
:::
