## The Real Numbers

### Completeness

The one axiom separating $\mathbb{R}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$:

> **Every nonempty set of reals that is bounded above has a least upper bound.**

$\mathbb{Q}$ fails it: $\{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x^2 < 2\}$ is bounded above with no rational supremum. Every existence theorem in the section traces back to this axiom.

The $\varepsilon$-characterization, which is how supremum is used in practice: $s = \sup S$ iff $s$ is an upper bound and for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $x \in S$ with $x > s - \varepsilon$.

Consequences: the **Archimedean property**, density of $\mathbb{Q}$ and of the irrationals, the nested interval property, and the existence of $\sqrt{2}$.

### The three main structural theorems

| Name                 | Statement                                                        |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Monotone convergence | A bounded monotone sequence converges                            |
| Bolzano–Weierstrass  | Every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence              |
| Heine–Borel          | A subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is compact iff it is closed and bounded |

These are three faces of completeness, and each is used to build a point out of nothing but bounds.

## Sequences

### Definitions

$a_n \to L$ means: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ with $|a_n - L|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$. Written in that order, $N$ depends on $\varepsilon$ only.

- Limits are **unique**.
- Convergent sequences are **bounded**; bounded sequences need not converge.
- **Limit laws** hold for sums, products, quotients with nonzero limit.
- **Squeeze theorem**: $a_n \le b_n \le c_n$ with $a_n, c_n \to L$ forces $b_n \to L$.

### Monotone and Cauchy

A **monotone** bounded sequence converges, to the sup or inf of its range. This needs no candidate limit, which is why it is the first tool to reach for.

A sequence is **Cauchy** if its terms eventually cluster together: for every $\varepsilon$ there is $N$ with $|a_n - a_m|<\varepsilon$ for all $n,m\ge N$.

$$
\text{Cauchy} \iff \text{convergent} \quad \text{(in } \mathbb{R}\text{)}
$$

The forward direction is easy; the converse is completeness in yet another form. In $\mathbb{Q}$ it fails, which is exactly what "$\mathbb{Q}$ has holes" means.

**Not sufficient:** consecutive terms getting close. $a_n = \sqrt{n}$ has $a_{n+1}-a_n \to 0$ and diverges. The Cauchy condition must hold for **all** pairs beyond $N$, not just neighbours.

### Subsequences

- If $a_n \to L$ then every subsequence converges to $L$.
- Two subsequences with different limits proves divergence — the cleanest divergence proof there is.
- $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ are the largest and smallest subsequential limits, and always exist.

## Series

$\sum a_n$ converges when the partial sums do. That is the definition; everything else is a test.

- **nth-term test.** $a_n \to 0$ is necessary, never sufficient.
- **Geometric.** $\sum r^n$ converges iff $|r|<1$.
- **$p$-series.** $\sum 1/n^p$ converges iff $p>1$. The harmonic series $p=1$ diverges.
- **Comparison, ratio, root, integral tests** for positive series.
- **Alternating series test** for decreasing terms with $a_n\to0$.

**Absolute versus conditional.** Absolute convergence implies convergence. A conditionally convergent series such as $\sum(-1)^{n+1}/n$ can be **rearranged to sum to anything**, which is Riemann's rearrangement theorem, while an absolutely convergent series is rearrangement-proof.

## Topology of the Line

- **Open**: every point has a neighbourhood inside the set. **Closed**: the complement is open, equivalently the set contains all its limit points.
- $\varnothing$ and $\mathbb{R}$ are **both** open and closed. $[0,1)$ is neither. "Not open" does not mean closed.
- Arbitrary unions of open sets are open; only **finite** intersections are. Dually for closed sets, and $\bigcap(-1/n,1/n)=\{0\}$ shows why the finiteness matters.
- **Compact** means every open cover has a finite subcover, and by **Heine–Borel** that is the same as closed and bounded.
- Compactness is what makes $[a,b]$ special: $(0,1]$ and $[0,\infty)$ each fail one condition, and each breaks a theorem.

## Limits and Continuity

### The definitions

$$
\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L: \quad \forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0:\ 0<|x-c|<\delta \implies |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon
$$

The value $f(c)$ plays no part, and $c$ need not be in the domain.

$f$ is **continuous at $c$** iff $c$ is in the domain and $\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c)$. Equivalently, every sequence $x_n\to c$ has $f(x_n)\to f(c)$ — the **sequential criterion**, which is the fastest way to prove *dis*continuity.

### The compact-interval theorems

For $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$:

| Theorem            | Conclusion                                      |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Boundedness        | $f$ is bounded                                  |
| Extreme value      | $f$ attains a maximum and a minimum             |
| Intermediate value | $f$ takes every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ |
| Uniform continuity | $f$ is uniformly continuous                     |

All four need **both** closed and bounded. $f(x)=1/x$ on $(0,1]$ is unbounded; $f(x)=x$ on $[0,\infty)$ attains no maximum; $\sin(1/x)$ on $(0,1]$ is continuous and not uniformly continuous.

**Uniform continuity** moves the quantifier: one $\delta$ for the whole domain rather than one per point. Lipschitz implies uniformly continuous implies continuous, and both implications are strict — $\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,1]$ is uniformly continuous and not Lipschitz.

## Differentiation

$$
f'(c)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}
$$

Differentiable implies continuous; the converse fails at every point for $|x|$ at 0, and Weierstrass built a function continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere.

**Interior extremum theorem:** an interior extremum of a differentiable function has $f'=0$. That plus EVT gives **Rolle**, and Rolle after subtracting the secant gives the **mean value theorem**.

MVT consequences, all used freely in calculus and proved only here:

- $f'=0$ on an interval $\implies f$ constant. This is what makes "+C" a theorem.
- $f'>0 \implies f$ strictly increasing.
- $|f'|\le K \implies f$ is Lipschitz with constant $K$.

**Taylor's theorem** is Rolle applied $n+1$ times:

$$
f(x)=P_n(x)+\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}
$$

an **equality** with only $\xi$ unspecified. It gives the usable bound $|R_n| \le \frac{K}{(n+1)!}|x-a|^{n+1}$.

**Smooth is not analytic.** $e^{-1/x^2}$ has every derivative zero at 0, so its Taylor series converges everywhere to the wrong function. The series converging and the series converging to $f$ are different claims, and the second is exactly $R_n \to 0$.

## Integration

For bounded $f$, partitions give $L(f,P)=\sum m_i\Delta x_i$ and $U(f,P)=\sum M_i\Delta x_i$. Refining raises $L$ and lowers $U$, and every lower sum is below every upper sum.

> **Integrable** means $\sup_P L = \inf_P U$. Equivalently (**Riemann criterion**), for each $\varepsilon$ some $P$ has $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$.

- **Continuous $\implies$ integrable**, via uniform continuity on the compact interval.
- **Monotone $\implies$ integrable**, even with countably many jumps.
- The **Dirichlet function** (1 on rationals, 0 on irrationals) is bounded and not integrable: $L=0$ and $U=1$ for every partition.

**Fundamental theorem, part one.** $F(x)=\int_a^x f$ is continuous whenever $f$ is integrable, and $F'(c)=f(c)$ wherever $f$ is continuous. Integration smooths.

**Fundamental theorem, part two.** $\int_a^b f = F(b)-F(a)$ for any antiderivative $F$, proved by applying the **MVT** on each subinterval and telescoping.

## Uniform Convergence

Pointwise: $N$ may depend on $x$. Uniform: one $N$ for all $x$, equivalently $\|f_n-f\|\to0$.

| Preserved by uniform convergence | Not preserved         |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Continuity of the limit          | Differentiability     |
| The value of the integral        | Anything about $f_n'$ |
| The uniform Cauchy property      | —                     |

- $x^n$ on $[0,1]$: continuous terms, discontinuous limit, $\|f_n-f\|=1$ always. Uniform on $[0,a]$ for $a<1$.
- $\frac{\sin(nx)}{\sqrt n} \rightrightarrows 0$ while $f_n'=\sqrt n\cos(nx)$ diverges.
- A spike of height $n$ and width $1/n$: pointwise limit 0, every integral 1.
- **Weierstrass M-test.** $|u_n(x)|\le M_n$ with $\sum M_n<\infty$ gives uniform convergence, and licenses term-by-term integration of power series.

| Theorem                         | Statement                                                        |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Completeness axiom              | Every nonempty set bounded above has a supremum                  |
| Archimedean property            | For any real $x$ there is a natural $n > x$                      |
| Monotone convergence            | A bounded monotone sequence converges                            |
| Bolzano–Weierstrass             | Every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence              |
| Cauchy criterion                | A sequence of reals converges iff it is Cauchy                   |
| Riemann rearrangement           | A conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to any sum   |
| Heine–Borel                     | Compact in $\mathbb{R}$ = closed and bounded                     |
| Extreme value                   | A continuous function on a compact interval attains its extremes |
| Intermediate value              | A continuous function on $[a,b]$ takes every intermediate value  |
| Uniform continuity theorem      | Continuous on a compact set implies uniformly continuous         |
| Rolle                           | Equal endpoint values give an interior critical point            |
| Mean value theorem              | $f'(c)$ equals the average rate for some interior $c$            |
| Taylor                          | $f = P_n + f^{(n+1)}(\xi)(x-a)^{n+1}/(n+1)!$                     |
| Riemann criterion               | Integrable iff some partition makes $U - L$ arbitrarily small    |
| Fundamental theorem of calculus | $F' = f$ where $f$ is continuous, and $\int_a^b f = F(b)-F(a)$   |
| Weierstrass M-test              | Uniform bounds with a convergent sum give uniform convergence    |

## The Counterexamples

They are worth memorizing as a set, because each one is the reason some hypothesis cannot be dropped.

| Object                          | Shows                                                    |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| $\{x\in\mathbb{Q}:x^2<2\}$      | $\mathbb{Q}$ is not complete                             |
| $(-1)^n$                        | Bounded need not imply convergent                        |
| $\sqrt{n}$                      | Consecutive terms close does not imply Cauchy            |
| $\sum 1/n$                      | $a_n\to0$ does not imply convergence                     |
| $\sum(-1)^{n+1}/n$              | Convergent but not absolutely; rearrangeable             |
| $[0,1)$                         | A set that is neither open nor closed                    |
| $\bigcap(-1/n,1/n)$             | Infinite intersections of open sets need not be open     |
| $1/x$ on $(0,1]$                | Non-compact domain breaks boundedness                    |
| $\sin(1/x)$ on $(0,1]$          | Continuous, not uniformly continuous                     |
| $x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$           | Continuous, not uniformly continuous on an unbounded set |
| $\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,1]$           | Uniformly continuous, not Lipschitz                      |
| $\lvert x\rvert$ at 0           | Continuous, not differentiable                           |
| $e^{-1/x^2}$                    | Smooth, not analytic                                     |
| Dirichlet function              | Bounded, not Riemann integrable                          |
| $x^n$ on $[0,1]$                | Pointwise limit of continuous functions can jump         |
| $\sin(nx)/\sqrt n$              | Uniform convergence says nothing about derivatives       |
| Height-$n$ spike of width $1/n$ | Limit and integral do not always commute                 |

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing sup with max.** $\sup[0,1) = 1$ and there is no maximum.
- **Letting $N$ depend on $x$** in a uniform statement, or on $n$ in a sequence statement. Quantifier order is the content.
- **Thinking $a_{n+1}-a_n\to0$ makes a sequence Cauchy.** $\sqrt n$ refutes it.
- **Using the nth-term test to prove convergence.** It can only prove divergence.
- **Rearranging a conditionally convergent series.**
- **Assuming "not open" means closed.**
- **Applying EVT or IVT on an open or unbounded interval.**
- **Forgetting that $f(c)$ is irrelevant to $\lim_{x\to c}f(x)$.**
- **Treating differentiable and continuous as interchangeable.**
- **Reading Taylor's theorem as an approximation.** It is an equality; the remainder is exact.
- **Assuming a smooth function equals its Taylor series.**
- **Concluding integrability from boundedness.** The Dirichlet function is bounded and not integrable.
- **Interchanging a limit with an integral or a derivative without uniformity.**
- **Expecting uniform convergence of $f_n$ to say something about $f_n'$.**

:::note[You've Got This]
Look back at what this section actually asked of you. Not long computations — a handful of definitions written with the quantifiers in the right order, one axiom, about sixteen theorems, and a stock of counterexamples that explain why every hypothesis is there. If the ε-δ and ε-N arguments started feeling mechanical somewhere around uniform continuity, that's the sign it worked: they _are_ mechanical once you see that each one is a negotiation where you're handed a tolerance and must produce a threshold. And this is the section where calculus stops being a set of rules you were given and becomes a set of theorems you can prove. That doesn't wear off.
:::
