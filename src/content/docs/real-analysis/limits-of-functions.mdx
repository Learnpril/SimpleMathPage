## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn the epsilon-delta definition of a function limit, the standard proof template, why the point $a$ itself is excluded, and the sequential criterion that makes disproving limits easy. This is the definition the whole subject is built on.

## The Concept

### The definition

> $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ means
>
> $$
> \forall \varepsilon>0 \; \exists \delta>0 \; \text{such that} \; 0 < |x - a| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon
> $$

The same game as epsilon-N. Your opponent picks a tolerance $\varepsilon$ on the **output**; you must produce a tolerance $\delta$ on the **input** that guarantees it.

<EpsilonDelta />

Geometrically: draw a horizontal band of half-width $\varepsilon$ around $L$. You must find a vertical strip of half-width $\delta$ around $a$ such that the graph over that strip stays inside the band. Thinner band, thinner strip.

### Why $0 < |x-a|$

That strict inequality excludes $x = a$, and it is deliberate.

**The limit does not care about $f(a)$.** The function need not even be defined at $a$. This is essential, because the derivative is defined as

$$
\lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}
$$

and that quotient is undefined at $h = 0$. If limits required the value at the point, calculus could not start.

So three situations are all consistent with $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$: $f(a) = L$, $f(a) \ne L$, and $f(a)$ undefined. Continuity is the extra condition that $f(a)$ exists and equals $L$ - the next lesson.

### The proof template

Same shape as before.

1. **Scratch.** Start from $|f(x)-L| < \varepsilon$. Factor out $|x-a|$ and bound whatever is left over.
2. **Choose $\delta$.** Usually a min of two things.
3. **Verify.** Assume $0<|x-a|<\delta$, derive the conclusion forwards.

For a linear function it's clean. To show $\lim_{x\to 3}(2x+1) = 7$:

_Scratch._ $|(2x+1) - 7| = |2x - 6| = 2|x-3|$, and $2|x-3| < \varepsilon \iff |x-3| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$.

_Proof._ Given $\varepsilon>0$, take $\delta = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$. If $0<|x-3|<\delta$ then $|(2x+1)-7| = 2|x-3| < 2\delta = \varepsilon$. ∎

### The min trick

Non-linear functions need one extra step. For $\lim_{x\to 2} x^2 = 4$:

$$
|x^2 - 4| = |x-2||x+2|
$$

We control $|x-2|$, but $|x+2|$ is a nuisance factor. **Bound it first by restricting $\delta$.**

If $\delta \le 1$ then $|x-2|<1$ gives $1 < x < 3$, so $|x+2| < 5$. Then

$$
|x^2-4| < 5|x-2|
$$

which is below $\varepsilon$ once $|x-2| < \frac{\varepsilon}{5}$. So take

$$
\delta = \min\left(1, \frac{\varepsilon}{5}\right)
$$

The min does two jobs at once: the 1 tames the nuisance factor, and the $\frac{\varepsilon}{5}$ delivers the tolerance. **This pattern appears in nearly every non-linear epsilon-delta proof.**

### The sequential criterion

> $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ $\iff$ for **every** sequence $x_n \to a$ with $x_n \ne a$, we have $f(x_n) \to L$.

This connects function limits to sequence limits, and it hands you two useful things.

**All the sequence limit theorems transfer.** Sums, products, quotients and the squeeze theorem for function limits follow immediately, with no new epsilon-delta work.

**Disproving a limit becomes easy.** Exhibit two sequences approaching $a$ whose images have different limits, and the limit cannot exist. Negating the epsilon-delta definition directly is much more painful.

### One-sided limits

$\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L$ uses $a < x < a+\delta$; the left limit uses $a - \delta < x < a$. Then

$$
\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L
$$

For $f(x) = \frac{|x|}{x}$ the right limit at 0 is 1 and the left is $-1$, so the two-sided limit does not exist.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Prove $\lim_{x\to 4}(3x - 5) = 7$.**

**Solution.** _Scratch._ $|(3x-5)-7| = |3x-12| = 3|x-4| < \varepsilon \iff |x-4| < \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$.

_Proof._ Let $\varepsilon>0$ and take $\delta = \frac{\varepsilon}{3}$. If $0<|x-4|<\delta$ then

$$
|(3x-5)-7| = 3|x-4| < 3\delta = \varepsilon
$$

∎

For any linear $f(x) = mx+c$ with $m \ne 0$, $\delta = \frac{\varepsilon}{|m|}$ always works.

**Example 2: Prove $\lim_{x\to 3} x^2 = 9$.**

**Solution.** _Scratch._ $|x^2-9| = |x-3||x+3|$. Restrict $\delta \le 1$, so $2<x<4$ and $|x+3| < 7$. Then $|x^2-9| < 7|x-3|$, below $\varepsilon$ when $|x-3| < \frac{\varepsilon}{7}$.

_Proof._ Given $\varepsilon>0$, take $\delta = \min\left(1, \frac{\varepsilon}{7}\right)$. If $0<|x-3|<\delta$ then $|x-3|<1$ so $|x+3|<7$, and

$$
|x^2-9| = |x-3||x+3| < 7|x-3| < 7 \cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{7} = \varepsilon
$$

∎

**Example 3: A limit where the function is undefined at the point.**

Compute $\lim_{x\to 2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}$.

**Solution.** At $x = 2$ the expression is $\frac00$, undefined. But the definition only looks at $x \ne 2$, and there

$$
\frac{x^2-4}{x-2} = \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = x+2
$$

The cancellation is legal precisely because $x \ne 2$. So the limit is $\lim_{x\to2}(x+2) = 4$.

**The limit is 4** even though $f(2)$ does not exist. This is exactly the situation every derivative is in.

**Example 4: Disprove a limit with sequences.**

Show $\lim_{x\to 0}\sin\left(\frac1x\right)$ does not exist.

**Solution.** Take two sequences approaching 0.

$$
x_n = \frac{1}{2\pi n} \implies \sin\left(\frac{1}{x_n}\right) = \sin(2\pi n) = 0
$$

$$
y_n = \frac{1}{2\pi n + \frac{\pi}{2}} \implies \sin\left(\frac{1}{y_n}\right) = \sin\left(2\pi n + \frac\pi2\right) = 1
$$

Both $x_n \to 0$ and $y_n \to 0$, but the images converge to 0 and 1.

By the sequential criterion, **the limit does not exist.** ∎

Compare how much work negating the epsilon-delta definition would have been. Two sequences settled it.

**Example 5: Delta depending on the point.**

For $f(x) = \frac1x$ on $(0,\infty)$, how does $\delta$ depend on $a$ for a fixed $\varepsilon$?

**Solution.**

$$
\left|\frac1x - \frac1a\right| = \frac{|a-x|}{|ax|}
$$

Restrict $|x - a| < \frac a2$, so $x > \frac a2$ and $|ax| > \frac{a^2}{2}$. Then

$$
\left|\frac1x-\frac1a\right| < \frac{2|x-a|}{a^2}
$$

so $\delta = \min\left(\frac a2, \frac{\varepsilon a^2}{2}\right)$ works.

**$\delta$ shrinks like $a^2$ as $a \to 0$.** The function gets steeper near 0, so the same output tolerance demands a much tighter input tolerance. That dependence on the _point_, not just on $\varepsilon$, is what fails to be uniform - and uniform continuity, four lessons ahead, is exactly the condition that rules it out.

**Example 6: A limit of 0 via squeezing.**

Compute $\lim_{x\to 0} x\sin\left(\frac1x\right)$.

**Solution.** The previous example shows $\sin\frac1x$ has no limit. But it is bounded, and

$$
\left|x\sin\frac1x\right| \le |x|
$$

so $-|x| \le x\sin\frac1x \le |x|$. Both bounds $\to 0$, so by squeezing the limit is **0**.

A bounded factor times a vanishing one goes to zero, whether or not the bounded factor has a limit. Same result as for sequences, now for functions.

**Example 7: One-sided limits differing.**

Analyse $f(x) = \frac{|x|}{x}$ at $x = 0$.

**Solution.** For $x>0$, $f(x) = 1$. For $x<0$, $f(x) = -1$. Undefined at 0.

$$
\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x) = 1, \qquad \lim_{x\to0^-}f(x) = -1
$$

Different, so the two-sided limit **does not exist**. Sequentially: $\frac1n \to 0$ gives images 1, and $-\frac1n \to 0$ gives images $-1$.

This is a **jump discontinuity**, and it is what makes the sign function non-integrable-by-inspection arguments fail and why step functions need care.

## Real-World Applications

**Tolerance engineering.** "The output must be within $\varepsilon$, so how tightly must the input be controlled?" is literally the epsilon-delta question. Manufacturing tolerance stack-up analysis computes $\delta$ from a required $\varepsilon$, and the derivative is the local conversion factor between them.

**Numerical differentiation.** Approximating $f'(a)$ by a difference quotient with small $h$ relies on the limit existing. Too large an $h$ gives truncation error and too small an $h$ gives catastrophic floating-point cancellation, so there is an optimal $h$ - a very practical consequence of the limit being approached rather than reached.

**Sensor calibration.** A sensor's sensitivity is a derivative, and the amplification of input noise into output noise is the $\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}$ ratio. Example 5's point matters here: near a steep part of the response curve, the same output precision demands far better input precision.

**Control systems.** Steady-state error is a limit as $t \to \infty$, and a controller specification like "settle within 2% of setpoint" is a choice of $\varepsilon$ with a corresponding settling time.

**Graphics and continuity of rendering.** A shader with a discontinuity produces visible seams. Detecting where a piecewise definition fails to have matching one-sided limits is exactly how those artifacts get diagnosed.

:::note[You've Got This]
This is the definition the whole section is built on, so it's worth drilling until the shape is reflex. Two techniques carry nearly every proof: factor out |x − a| and bound the leftover nuisance factor, and use δ = min(something, ε/something) so the min tames the nuisance while the second term delivers the tolerance. And when you need to show a limit _doesn't_ exist, always reach for two sequences rather than negating the definition. That single habit will save you a great deal of algebra.
:::
