## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn the intermediate value theorem, the supremum proof, why it fails over $\mathbb{Q}$, and the fixed-point and root-finding consequences that follow from it.

## The Concept

### The statement

> **Intermediate value theorem.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $y$ lies between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there exists $c \in [a,b]$ with $f(c) = y$.

The special case $y = 0$ is the one used most: **a continuous function that changes sign has a root.**

<IVTDiagram />

Two hypotheses, and both are doing work.

- **Continuity.** A jump can step over the value entirely, as the right panel shows.
- **A closed interval.** On an open interval the crossing point may be exactly the endpoint you excluded.

### The proof

Take $f(a) < 0 < f(b)$ and let

$$
S = \{x \in [a,b] : f(x) < 0\}
$$

$S$ is non-empty ($a \in S$) and bounded above by $b$. **By completeness, $c = \sup S$ exists.** That is where the axiom gets spent, and it is the only non-trivial step.

Now show $f(c) = 0$ by ruling out both alternatives.

**If $f(c) < 0$:** by continuity there is a neighbourhood of $c$ where $f$ stays negative. (Take $\varepsilon = |f(c)|$ in the definition.) So points slightly to the right of $c$ are in $S$, contradicting $c$ being an upper bound. Note $c \ne b$ here, since $f(b) > 0$.

**If $f(c) > 0$:** similarly $f$ stays positive on a neighbourhood of $c$, so some interval $(c - \delta, c]$ contains no points of $S$. Then $c - \delta$ is a smaller upper bound, contradicting leastness.

Hence $f(c) = 0$. ∎

Notice the shape: **the supremum is the candidate, and continuity kills both ways it could be wrong.** Sup for existence, continuity for correctness. That pairing recurs in the next lesson too.

### Why it fails over the rationals

Let $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ on $[0,2] \cap \mathbb{Q}$. Then $f(0) = -2 < 0$ and $f(2) = 2 > 0$, and $f$ is continuous as a function on the rationals.

But there is no rational $c$ with $c^2 = 2$. **The theorem fails.**

The proof breaks at exactly one place: $\sup S$ does not exist in $\mathbb{Q}$. This is the cleanest demonstration that IVT is a completeness theorem wearing geometric clothing. The picture of a curve crossing a line looks like it should be about geometry; it is about the number line having no holes.

### What it does not say

**It does not say the root is unique.** $f(x) = \sin x$ on $[0, 4\pi]$ has many.

**It does not say a value not between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ is skipped.** Values outside that range may also be attained; the theorem is one-directional.

**The converse is false.** A function can attain every intermediate value and still be discontinuous. $f(x) = \sin\frac1x$ for $x \ne 0$ with $f(0)=0$ has the intermediate value property on any interval containing 0 and is discontinuous there. So "hits every value in between" is strictly weaker than continuity.

### Consequences

**Root existence and bisection.** If $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs, a root lies between them, and halving repeatedly finds it. That is the bisection algorithm, and IVT is its correctness proof.

**Fixed points.** If $g: [0,1]\to[0,1]$ is continuous, it has a fixed point $g(c) = c$. Apply IVT to $f(x) = g(x) - x$: $f(0) = g(0) \ge 0$ and $f(1) = g(1)-1 \le 0$, so $f$ has a zero. This is the one-dimensional case of Brouwer's fixed point theorem.

**Odd-degree polynomials have a real root.** As $x \to \pm\infty$ an odd-degree polynomial takes both signs, so IVT applies on a large enough interval. Even-degree polynomials need not: $x^2+1$ has none.

**The pancake and ham sandwich style results.** Any continuous function on a circle takes the same value at some pair of antipodal points, which follows from IVT applied to $f(x) - f(-x)$.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Show $x^3 + x - 1 = 0$ has a real root.**

**Solution.** Let $f(x) = x^3+x-1$, a polynomial and so continuous everywhere.

$$
f(0) = -1 < 0, \qquad f(1) = 1 > 0
$$

By IVT there is $c \in (0,1)$ with $f(c) = 0$. ∎

To narrow it: $f(0.5) = 0.125+0.5-1 = -0.375 < 0$, so the root is in $(0.5, 1)$. Then $f(0.75) = 0.4219+0.75-1 = 0.1719 > 0$, so it is in $(0.5, 0.75)$. That is bisection, and each step halves the interval.

**Example 2: Show every positive real has a square root.**

**Solution.** Fix $y > 0$ and let $f(x) = x^2$ on $[0, y+1]$, continuous.

$$
f(0) = 0 \le y, \qquad f(y+1) = (y+1)^2 = y^2+2y+1 > y
$$

By IVT there is $c$ with $c^2 = y$. ∎

**The existence of $\sqrt y$ is a theorem, not a definition.** It requires completeness, which is why the rationals have no $\sqrt2$.

**Example 3: A continuous self-map has a fixed point.**

Let $g:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ be continuous. Show $g(c) = c$ for some $c$.

**Solution.** Let $f(x) = g(x) - x$, continuous as a difference of continuous functions.

Since $g(0) \in [0,1]$, $f(0) = g(0) \ge 0$. Since $g(1) \in [0,1]$, $f(1) = g(1) - 1 \le 0$.

If either is zero we are done. Otherwise $f(0) > 0 > f(1)$, and IVT gives $c$ with $f(c) = 0$, that is $g(c) = c$. ∎

Interpretation: any continuous way of stretching and folding $[0,1]$ into itself leaves at least one point where it started.

**Example 4: Where the closed interval matters.**

Does $f(x) = \frac1x$ on $(0,1)$ attain the value 5? What about IVT?

**Solution.** It does: $f(0.2) = 5$. But note IVT does not apply as usually stated, because $(0,1)$ is not closed and $f$ has no values at the endpoints to compare.

A better illustration of the failure: on $(0,1)$ consider $f(x) = x$. It takes values arbitrarily close to 0 and 1 without attaining either, so the value 0 is "between" the limits at the endpoints and is never attained. **Closedness is what makes the endpoints available.**

**Example 5: An odd-degree polynomial.**

Show $p(x) = x^5 - 3x^2 + 1$ has a real root.

**Solution.** $p(0) = 1 > 0$ and $p(-1) = -1 - 3 + 1 = -3 < 0$.

By IVT there is a root in $(-1, 0)$. ∎

Generally, for odd degree the leading term dominates at large $|x|$ and has opposite signs at $\pm\infty$, so IVT always applies. **Every odd-degree real polynomial has a real root**, while $x^2+1$ shows even degree gives no such guarantee.

**Example 6: Antipodal points.**

Show that at any moment there are two diametrically opposite points on the equator with the same temperature.

**Solution.** Let $T(\theta)$ be the temperature at angle $\theta$, assumed continuous, and define

$$
f(\theta) = T(\theta) - T(\theta + \pi)
$$

Then $f(0) = T(0) - T(\pi)$ and $f(\pi) = T(\pi) - T(2\pi) = T(\pi) - T(0) = -f(0)$.

So $f(0)$ and $f(\pi)$ have opposite signs (or both are 0). By IVT there is $\theta_0$ with $f(\theta_0) = 0$, meaning

$$
T(\theta_0) = T(\theta_0 + \pi)
$$

∎

The trick of forming $f(x) - f(x+\text{shift})$ and noting it changes sign is worth remembering; it powers a whole family of these results.

**Example 7: The converse fails.**

Give a discontinuous function with the intermediate value property.

**Solution.** Let $f(x) = \sin\frac1x$ for $x \ne 0$ and $f(0) = 0$.

On any interval containing 0, $f$ oscillates through every value in $[-1,1]$ infinitely often, so it attains every intermediate value.

Yet $f$ is discontinuous at 0, since $\lim_{x\to0}\sin\frac1x$ does not exist.

**The intermediate value property is strictly weaker than continuity.** IVT is a one-way implication.

## Real-World Applications

**Bisection and numerical solvers.** Every root-finding routine that brackets a root relies on IVT for correctness. It is slower than Newton's method but it cannot fail once a sign change is found, which is why robust solvers fall back to it.

**Control systems.** Showing a control input exists that achieves a target output is an IVT argument: the response is continuous and straddles the target, so some input hits it exactly. That is the formal basis of a calibration sweep.

**Thermostats and equilibrium.** Any continuous process that is too cold at one setting and too hot at another has a setting that is exactly right. Trivial-sounding, and it is the argument used to prove equilibrium existence in far less obvious settings.

**Economics.** Existence of a market-clearing price is IVT in one dimension and Brouwer's fixed point theorem in many. Excess demand is positive at low prices and negative at high ones, so it vanishes somewhere.

**Game theory.** Nash's existence theorem is a fixed-point argument, generalizing Example 3 to higher dimensions. The one-dimensional case here is the whole idea in miniature.

**Engineering tolerance.** If a design parameter gives too little clearance at one extreme and too much at the other, some intermediate value is exact. Bisection on a physical prototype is IVT applied by hand.

:::note[You've Got This]
The IVT looks like a picture of a curve crossing a line, and it is genuinely worth resisting that reading. Its content is that ℝ has no holes: the same statement is false over ℚ, where x² − 2 changes sign without ever vanishing. The proof is worth remembering in shape rather than detail - take a supremum for the candidate, then let continuity rule out both ways it could be wrong. And keep the one-directionality in mind: continuity gives the intermediate value property, but not conversely.
:::
