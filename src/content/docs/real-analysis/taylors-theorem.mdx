## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll see Taylor's theorem stated with an **exact** remainder rather than a vague "≈", learn to prove it by the same subtract-off-the-known-part trick that gave the mean value theorem, use the remainder to get honest error bounds, and understand the difference between a Taylor series converging and a Taylor series converging to the function it came from.

## The Concept

Calculus introduced Taylor series as an infinite sum and asked you to trust it. Analysis does the opposite: it starts with a **finite** polynomial plus an error term you can control, and only afterwards asks whether the error goes to zero.

### The Taylor polynomial

Suppose $f$ has $n$ derivatives at $a$. The **$n$th Taylor polynomial** of $f$ about $a$ is

$$
P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k
$$

It is the unique polynomial of degree at most $n$ whose first $n$ derivatives at $a$ match those of $f$. That matching is the whole design goal: $P_n$ agrees with $f$ in value, slope, curvature, and so on, up to order $n$, at the single point $a$.

The linear case $P_1(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)$ is the tangent line. Taylor's theorem is what tangent-line approximation grows into.

### The theorem

> **Taylor's theorem (Lagrange form).** Let $f$ be $n+1$ times differentiable on an open interval containing $a$ and $x$. Then there is a point $\xi$ strictly between $a$ and $x$ with
>
> $$
> f(x) = P_n(x) + \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}
> $$

Write $R_n(x) = f(x) - P_n(x)$ for the **remainder**. The theorem says $R_n$ has exactly the shape of the next term in the pattern, except that the derivative is evaluated at an unknown interior point instead of at $a$.

Two things to notice. First, this is an **equality**, not an estimate — nothing has been thrown away. Second, the only thing unspecified is _where_ $\xi$ sits, and that is precisely the same concession the mean value theorem makes. Taking $n = 0$ recovers the MVT exactly:

$$
f(x) = f(a) + f'(\xi)(x-a)
$$

So Taylor's theorem is the MVT extended to higher order, and it is proved the same way.

### Proof

_Proof._ Fix $x \ne a$ and choose the number $M$ so that

$$
f(x) = P_n(x) + M(x-a)^{n+1}
$$

which is possible since $(x-a)^{n+1} \ne 0$. The claim is that $M = f^{(n+1)}(\xi)/(n+1)!$ for some interior $\xi$.

Let

$$
g(t) = f(t) - P_n(t) - M(t-a)^{n+1}
$$

Because $P_n$ matches $f$ to order $n$ at $a$, we get $g(a) = g'(a) = \cdots = g^{(n)}(a) = 0$, and by the choice of $M$ we also get $g(x) = 0$.

Now apply Rolle repeatedly. Since $g(a) = g(x) = 0$, there is $\xi_1$ between them with $g'(\xi_1) = 0$. Since $g'(a) = g'(\xi_1) = 0$, there is $\xi_2$ between those with $g''(\xi_2) = 0$. Continuing $n+1$ times produces $\xi = \xi_{n+1}$ strictly between $a$ and $x$ with

$$
g^{(n+1)}(\xi) = 0
$$

But $P_n$ has degree at most $n$, so its $(n+1)$st derivative vanishes, and the $(n+1)$st derivative of $M(t-a)^{n+1}$ is $M(n+1)!$. Hence

$$
0 = f^{(n+1)}(\xi) - M(n+1)! \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}
$$

∎

**Subtract off the part you already understand, then apply Rolle.** That is the same move as in the MVT proof, iterated. It is worth seeing that the entire content of Taylor's theorem is Rolle applied $n+1$ times.

### Using the remainder

In practice you never know $\xi$, so you bound the derivative instead. If

$$
|f^{(n+1)}(t)| \le K \quad \text{for all } t \text{ between } a \text{ and } x
$$

then

$$
|R_n(x)| \le \frac{K}{(n+1)!}|x-a|^{n+1}
$$

That is the working form. Everything on the right is computable, and the $(n+1)!$ in the denominator is why Taylor approximation is so effective at short range.

Two separate effects shrink the error:

- Increasing the degree divides by a larger factorial.
- Moving $x$ closer to $a$ shrinks $|x-a|^{n+1}$, and shrinks it faster for larger $n$.

The second is why $P_n$ is a **local** tool. Away from $a$ the factor $|x-a|^{n+1}$ grows, and for a function whose derivatives are large the bound can be useless.

### Taylor series, and when it fails

Letting $n \to \infty$ gives the **Taylor series**

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k
$$

Here is where calculus quietly assumed something false. The series converging is **not** the same as the series converging to $f$. The correct statement is:

> The Taylor series converges to $f(x)$ if and only if $R_n(x) \to 0$.

And $R_n(x) \to 0$ can fail in two different ways.

**The series can diverge.** For $f(x) = \frac{1}{1-x}$ about 0, the series is $\sum x^k$, which diverges for $|x| \ge 1$ even though $f$ is perfectly well behaved at, say, $x = -2$.

**The series can converge to the wrong function.** Define

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x^2} & x \ne 0 \\ 0 & x = 0\end{cases}
$$

Every derivative of this function at 0 is 0. So its Taylor series about 0 is the zero series, which converges everywhere — to the zero function, which is not $f$. The remainder never goes to zero for $x \ne 0$.

That example is the reason **smooth** and **analytic** are different words. A function is smooth if it has derivatives of all orders; it is analytic at $a$ if it also equals its Taylor series near $a$. Analytic implies smooth, and this function shows the converse fails.

### Taylor's theorem in the little-o form

There is a weaker but often more convenient statement, requiring only $n$ derivatives:

$$
f(x) = P_n(x) + o\big((x-a)^n\big) \quad \text{as } x \to a
$$

meaning $R_n(x)/(x-a)^n \to 0$. This says nothing quantitative at a fixed $x$, but it is exactly what you want when computing limits, and it is the form behind the standard limit tricks.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Build $P_3$ for $\sin x$ about 0 and bound the error at $x = 0.5$.**

**Solution.** Derivatives at 0 cycle $0, 1, 0, -1$, so

$$
P_3(x) = x - \frac{x^3}{6}
$$

The fourth derivative is $\sin x$, so $K = 1$ works everywhere. Then

$$
|R_3(0.5)| \le \frac{1}{4!}(0.5)^4 = \frac{0.0625}{24} \approx 0.0026
$$

Checking: $P_3(0.5) = 0.5 - 0.0208333 = 0.4791667$ and $\sin(0.5) = 0.4794255$. The true error is about $0.00026$, ten times better than the bound.

**The bound is honest but not tight**, because it assumes the worst possible fourth derivative on the interval. That is typical and acceptable: a guaranteed bound is worth more than a sharp guess.

**Example 2: How many terms for $e$ to six decimal places?**

**Solution.** Take $f(x) = e^x$, $a = 0$, $x = 1$. All derivatives are $e^x$, bounded on $[0,1]$ by $e < 3$. So

$$
|R_n(1)| \le \frac{3}{(n+1)!}
$$

We want this below $5 \times 10^{-7}$, so we need $(n+1)! > 6 \times 10^6$. Since $10! = 3{,}628{,}800$ and $11! = 39{,}916{,}800$, take $n + 1 = 11$, that is $n = 10$.

$$
e \approx \sum_{k=0}^{10} \frac{1}{k!} = 2.718281801\ldots
$$

against $e = 2.718281828\ldots$ ✓

**This is how a library function decides where to stop.** The factorial growth means ten terms buy six digits.

**Example 3: Why $\frac{1}{1-x}$ has a radius of only 1.**

**Solution.** $f^{(k)}(x) = k!/(1-x)^{k+1}$, so $f^{(k)}(0) = k!$ and the series is $\sum x^k$.

That geometric series converges exactly when $|x| < 1$. Yet $f$ is smooth at every $x \ne 1$, including $x = -3$.

**A Taylor series about $a$ is blind to good behaviour beyond the nearest singularity**, even a singularity in the other direction. The radius here is 1 because the pole at $x = 1$ is at distance 1, and that limit applies symmetrically.

**Example 4: A smooth function that is not analytic.**

Show the Taylor series of $f(x) = e^{-1/x^2}$ (with $f(0) = 0$) about 0 is identically zero.

**Solution.** For $x \ne 0$, each derivative is $e^{-1/x^2}$ times a rational function of $x$. At 0, apply the limit definition:

$$
f'(0) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^{-1/h^2}}{h}
$$

Substituting $t = 1/h$ turns this into $\lim_{t\to\infty} t e^{-t^2} = 0$, since the exponential beats any power. The same argument, with a higher power of $t$, gives $f^{(k)}(0) = 0$ for every $k$.

So $P_n \equiv 0$ for all $n$, and $R_n(x) = f(x) \ne 0$ for $x \ne 0$. The remainder does not vanish.

**Smooth does not imply analytic.** This function is infinitely flat at the origin without being zero near it.

**Example 5: Estimate $\sqrt{4.2}$ with an error bound.**

**Solution.** Take $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$, $a = 4$, so $f(4) = 2$, $f'(4) = \tfrac14$, $f''(4) = -\tfrac{1}{32}$.

$$
P_2(x) = 2 + \tfrac14(x-4) - \tfrac{1}{64}(x-4)^2
$$

$$
P_2(4.2) = 2 + 0.05 - 0.000625 = 2.049375
$$

For the bound, $f'''(x) = \tfrac{3}{8}x^{-5/2}$, which on $[4, 4.2]$ is largest at $x=4$: $\tfrac{3}{8}\cdot\tfrac{1}{32} \approx 0.01172$. So

$$
|R_2(4.2)| \le \frac{0.01172}{6}(0.2)^3 \approx 1.6\times10^{-5}
$$

The true value is $2.0493902\ldots$, so the actual error is about $1.5\times10^{-5}$. **Here the bound is nearly tight**, because the third derivative barely varies on such a short interval.

**Example 6: Use Taylor to evaluate a limit.**

Find $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x - \sin x}{x^3}$.

**Solution.** From $\sin x = x - \frac{x^3}{6} + o(x^3)$,

$$
x - \sin x = \frac{x^3}{6} + o(x^3)
$$

Dividing by $x^3$ gives $\frac16 + o(1) \to \frac16$.

Compare L'Hôpital, which needs three rounds of differentiation. **Substituting a Taylor expansion is usually faster and always tells you which term is doing the work.**

**Example 7: Second-derivative test, derived.**

Suppose $f'(a) = 0$ and $f''(a) > 0$. Show $a$ is a local minimum.

**Solution.** Taylor with $n=1$ gives, for some $\xi$ between $a$ and $x$,

$$
f(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \frac{f''(\xi)}{2}(x-a)^2 = f(a) + \frac{f''(\xi)}{2}(x-a)^2
$$

If $f''$ is continuous at $a$ and positive there, it stays positive on some interval around $a$. For $x$ in that interval, $\xi$ lies in it too, so the last term is positive and $f(x) > f(a)$. ∎

**The second-derivative test is Taylor's theorem at $n=1$.** It also shows why the test is silent when $f''(a)=0$: the sign information came from that term, and there is none.

## Real-World Applications

**Numerical libraries.** Evaluating $\sin$, $\exp$, or $\log$ in hardware or software means reducing the argument to a small range and then using a polynomial. The degree is chosen from a remainder bound like Example 2, so the routine can promise a result correct to the last bit.

**Finite-difference methods.** Every discretization of a derivative, and the order of accuracy quoted for it, comes from expanding in Taylor series and seeing which terms cancel. The leading uncancelled term _is_ the error term.

**Physics linearization.** The small-angle approximation $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ that makes the pendulum solvable is $P_1$, and the remainder bound tells you the angle at which the model stops being trustworthy.

**Optimization.** Newton's method is built by minimizing $P_2$ at each step, and the quadratic convergence rate comes from the size of $R_2$. Quasi-Newton methods are approximations of the same quadratic model.

**Computer graphics and audio.** Polynomial approximation of transcendental functions is standard where a per-sample or per-pixel cost matters, and the acceptable degree is set by an error budget.

**Error propagation in measurement.** Expanding a formula to first order in its inputs is exactly how uncertainty is propagated through a calculation; the second-order remainder is what tells you when the linear rule is inadequate.

:::note[You've Got This]
The one idea worth carrying out of this lesson: Taylor's theorem is an equality, not an approximation. The polynomial plus the remainder is exactly $f$, and all the work is in bounding one derivative on one interval. That reframing turns "≈" into a guarantee, which is the difference between using an approximation and trusting it. And if the $e^{-1/x^2}$ example bothered you, good — it should. It's the example that shows why analysis insists on checking that the remainder actually goes to zero.
:::
