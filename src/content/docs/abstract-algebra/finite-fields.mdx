## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn exactly which finite fields exist, how to build $GF(p^n)$ from an irreducible polynomial, how to compute in $GF(4)$ and $GF(8)$, and why $GF(4)$ is not $\mathbb{Z}_4$.

## The Concept

### Which finite fields exist

<FiniteFieldOrders />

> **Classification of finite fields.** A field with $q$ elements exists **if and only if** $q = p^n$ for a prime $p$ and integer $n \ge 1$. For each such $q$ there is **exactly one** field of that order, up to isomorphism.

This is one of the most satisfying classification theorems in mathematics: a complete and short answer.

Why no field of order 6? A field's characteristic must be prime, and a finite field of characteristic $p$ is a vector space over $\mathbb{F}_p$, so its size is $p^n$. Since 6 is not a prime power, there's nothing to build.

Notation: $GF(q)$ or $\mathbb{F}_q$. When $q = p$ is prime, $GF(p) = \mathbb{Z}_p$.

### $GF(p^n)$ is not $\mathbb{Z}_{p^n}$

This is the single most common misconception in the topic, so it's worth stating bluntly.

$\mathbb{Z}_4$ is **not** a field: 2 has no inverse, since $2b$ is even and never $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$. But $GF(4)$ exists and is a field. They are different rings of the same size.

The differences:

- $\mathbb{Z}_4$ has characteristic 4; $GF(4)$ has characteristic **2**.
- In $GF(4)$, $x + x = 0$ for every element.
- $\mathbb{Z}_4$ is cyclic under addition; $GF(4)$'s additive group is $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$.

**Only when $n = 1$ do the two coincide.**

### Building $GF(p^n)$

The recipe uses the theorem from the previous lesson.

1. Find an **irreducible** polynomial $f$ of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$.
2. Form $\mathbb{F}_p[x]/\langle f \rangle$.

Since $f$ is irreducible, the quotient is a field. Its elements are the remainders on division by $f$, that is, polynomials of degree $< n$. There are $p$ choices for each of $n$ coefficients, giving $p^n$ elements exactly as required.

Different irreducible polynomials of the same degree give isomorphic fields, which is the uniqueness half of the classification. In practice a specific polynomial gets standardized so that implementations interoperate.

### $GF(4)$ in full

Take $p = 2$, $n = 2$, and $f = x^2 + x + 1$, which is irreducible over $\mathbb{F}_2$ because neither 0 nor 1 is a root.

Elements: $0, 1, a, a+1$, writing $a$ for the coset of $x$. The defining relation is

$$
a^2 + a + 1 = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad a^2 = a + 1
$$

using that $-1 = 1$ in characteristic 2.

Reading the multiplication table: $a \cdot a = a + 1$, and $a \cdot (a+1) = a^2 + a = (a+1) + a = 1$. So $a^{-1} = a+1$.

Note $a^3 = a \cdot a^2 = a(a+1) = 1$, so $a$ has multiplicative order 3 and generates $GF(4)^* = \{1, a, a^2\}$.

### The multiplicative group is always cyclic

> $GF(q)^*$, the nonzero elements under multiplication, is **cyclic** of order $q - 1$.

A generator is called a **primitive element**. This is a strong statement - it says every finite field's nonzero part is as simple as possible - and it's why discrete logarithms make sense in finite fields, and hence why Diffie-Hellman works there.

For $GF(4)$: $a$ is primitive, since $a^1 = a$, $a^2 = a+1$, $a^3 = 1$.

Combining with Lagrange: every nonzero $x$ satisfies

$$
x^{q-1} = 1, \qquad \text{so} \qquad x^q = x \ \text{ for all } x \in GF(q)
$$

including $x = 0$. That identity is used constantly in coding theory.

### The Frobenius map

In characteristic $p$, the map

$$
\phi(x) = x^p
$$

is a **field homomorphism**, because $(x+y)^p = x^p + y^p$ there - all the middle binomial coefficients are divisible by $p$. It's the "freshman's dream," true in characteristic $p$ and false over $\mathbb{R}$.

On $\mathbb{F}_p$ itself, $\phi$ is the identity by Fermat's little theorem. On $GF(p^n)$ it generates a cyclic group of $n$ automorphisms, and that group is the Galois group of the extension. Finite fields have the simplest possible Galois theory.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Why is there no field of order 6?**

**Solution.** Suppose $F$ had 6 elements. Its characteristic must be prime, so 2, 3, or 5. A finite field of characteristic $p$ contains $\mathbb{F}_p$ and is a vector space over it, so $|F| = p^n$ for some $n$.

$6 = 2 \cdot 3$ is not a prime power. **No such field exists.** ∎

$\mathbb{Z}_6$ exists as a ring, of course, but has zero divisors: $2 \cdot 3 = 0$.

**Example 2: Build $GF(8)$.**

**Solution.** $8 = 2^3$, so we need an irreducible cubic over $\mathbb{F}_2$. Candidates with nonzero constant term (or $x$ would divide them): $x^3+x+1$ and $x^3+x^2+1$.

Test $f = x^3+x+1$: $f(0) = 1$, $f(1) = 1+1+1 = 1$. No roots, and a cubic with no root is irreducible. ✓

$$
GF(8) = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/\langle x^3+x+1\rangle
$$

Elements are all $c_2x^2 + c_1x + c_0$ with $c_i \in \{0,1\}$: eight of them, matching one 3-bit byte fragment. The relation is $a^3 = a + 1$.

Powers of $a$: $a, a^2, a^3 = a+1, a^4 = a^2+a, a^5 = a^3+a^2 = a^2+a+1, a^6 = a^3+a^2+a = a^2+1, a^7 = a^3 + a = 1$.

Seven distinct powers before returning to 1, so $a$ is primitive and $GF(8)^* \cong \mathbb{Z}_7$.

**Example 3: Compute in $GF(4)$.**

Find $(a+1)^2$ and $a^{-1}$.

**Solution.** Using $a^2 = a+1$ and characteristic 2 (so $2a = 0$):

$$
(a+1)^2 = a^2 + 2a + 1 = a^2 + 1 = (a+1) + 1 = a
$$

For the inverse of $a$: from $a^2 = a + 1$, multiply by... simpler, note

$$
a(a+1) = a^2 + a = (a+1) + a = 1
$$

So $a^{-1} = a+1$. ✓ (And symmetrically $(a+1)^{-1} = a$.)

Notice $(a+1)^2 = a$ and $a^2 = a+1$: squaring swaps the two, which is the Frobenius map in action.

**Example 4: Why $GF(4) \ne \mathbb{Z}_4$.**

**Solution.** In $GF(4)$, characteristic 2 means $x + x = 0$ for every element, so

$$
1 + 1 = 0
$$

In $\mathbb{Z}_4$, $1 + 1 = 2 \ne 0$.

The additive groups differ: $GF(4)^+ \cong \mathbb{Z}_2\times\mathbb{Z}_2$ (every element of order 2), while $\mathbb{Z}_4$ is cyclic with 1 of order 4.

And decisively, $\mathbb{Z}_4$ isn't a field at all: $2 \cdot 0 = 0$, $2\cdot1 = 2$, $2\cdot2 = 0$, $2\cdot3 = 2$, so 2 never produces 1.

**Example 5: Solve a linear equation in $GF(4)$.**

Solve $a x + 1 = 0$.

**Solution.** In characteristic 2, $-1 = 1$, so the equation is $ax = 1$, giving $x = a^{-1} = a+1$.

Check: $a(a+1) = 1$, so $ax + 1 = 1 + 1 = 0$ ✓

**Example 6: Count irreducible polynomials.**

How many monic irreducible quadratics are there over $\mathbb{F}_2$?

**Solution.** Monic quadratics over $\mathbb{F}_2$: $x^2$, $x^2+1$, $x^2+x$, $x^2+x+1$. Four in total.

- $x^2 = x \cdot x$: reducible.
- $x^2+1 = (x+1)^2$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$, since $2x = 0$: reducible.
- $x^2+x = x(x+1)$: reducible.
- $x^2+x+1$: no root, so irreducible.

**Exactly one.** That's why $GF(4)$ has a canonical construction - there's no other choice of modulus.

For higher degrees there are several, and any of them gives an isomorphic field. Two monic irreducible cubics exist over $\mathbb{F}_2$, and both build $GF(8)$.

**Example 7: The identity $x^q = x$.**

Verify $x^4 = x$ for all $x \in GF(4)$.

**Solution.** For $x = 0$: $0 = 0$ ✓

For nonzero $x$, the multiplicative group has order 3, so $x^3 = 1$ and hence $x^4 = x$ ✓

Explicitly: $1^4 = 1$; $a^4 = a^3 \cdot a = 1 \cdot a = a$; $(a+1)^4 = (a+1)^3(a+1) = a$... let's check that directly. $(a+1)^2 = a$ from Example 3, so $(a+1)^4 = a^2 = a+1$ ✓

So every element of $GF(4)$ is a root of $x^4 - x$, which means $x^4 - x$ factors completely over $GF(4)$. In general $x^q - x$ is exactly the product of $(x - \alpha)$ over all $\alpha \in GF(q)$, which is one clean way to characterize the field.

## Real-World Applications

**AES.** The Advanced Encryption Standard operates on bytes as elements of $GF(2^8)$, constructed as $\mathbb{F}_2[x]/\langle x^8+x^4+x^3+x+1\rangle$. One byte is one field element, which is why the construction is so convenient in hardware. The S-box is essentially the map $x \mapsto x^{-1}$ in this field, chosen because inversion is highly nonlinear and so resists linear cryptanalysis.

**Reed–Solomon codes.** QR codes, CDs, DVDs, Blu-ray, and NASA deep-space links use Reed–Solomon over $GF(256)$. A QR code can lose up to 30% of its area and still decode, because the data are values of a polynomial over a finite field and enough surviving points determine it uniquely. The Voyager probes' images reached Earth through these codes.

**RAID storage.** RAID 6 tolerates two simultaneous disk failures by storing two parity blocks computed in $GF(2^8)$. Recovering a failed drive means solving a small linear system over that field.

**Elliptic-curve cryptography.** Curves are defined over $GF(p)$ or $GF(2^n)$. The security assumption is the difficulty of the discrete logarithm in the curve's group, which relies on the field being finite and the group being cyclic of large prime order.

**Random number generation.** Linear feedback shift registers are multiplication by a primitive element in $GF(2^n)$, and choosing a primitive polynomial gives maximal period $2^n - 1$. Used in GPS signal generation, Bluetooth frequency hopping, and hardware test patterns.

**Combinatorial designs.** Finite projective planes are built from finite fields, and the existence question for orders that are not prime powers is a hard open problem. The nonexistence of a projective plane of order 10 was settled in 1989 by thousands of hours of computer search.

:::note[You've Got This]
The classification is the thing to remember, and it's short: a finite field exists exactly for prime-power orders, and there's exactly one of each. The trap to avoid is assuming that $GF(p^n)$ is the same thing as $\mathbb{Z}_{p^n}$; GF(4) has characteristic 2 while ℤ₄ has characteristic 4 and isn't a field at all. Beyond that, the construction is mechanical: pick an irreducible polynomial of the right degree and quotient by it. Practise a few multiplications in GF(4) with a² = a + 1 until the reduction step feels automatic, because that's exactly the arithmetic running inside every AES encryption and every QR code you scan.
:::
