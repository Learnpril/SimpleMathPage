## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn what a subgroup is, the efficient test for checking one, why subgroups must share the parent's identity, and how the subgroups of a group fit together into a lattice.

## The Concept

### The definition

A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a **subgroup** of $G$, written $H \le G$, if $H$ is itself a group under the same operation.

The phrase "under the same operation" is doing real work. $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ is a subset of $\mathbb{Z}$ and a group under multiplication mod 5, but it is not a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, because the operations differ.

### The subgroup test

Checking all four axioms is wasteful, because two come free. Associativity is inherited: if it holds for every triple in $G$, it holds for every triple in $H$. And once $H$ is nonempty and closed under inverses, closure hands you the identity.

So there are only three things to check:

1. $H$ is **nonempty** (in practice, check $e \in H$).
2. **Closed under the operation**: $a, b \in H \implies ab \in H$.
3. **Closed under inverses**: $a \in H \implies a^{-1} \in H$.

There's an even tighter version. For a nonempty $H$:

$$
H \le G \iff ab^{-1} \in H \text{ for all } a, b \in H
$$

**One-step subgroup test.** It works because taking $a = b$ gives $aa^{-1} = e \in H$, then $a = e$ gives $eb^{-1} = b^{-1} \in H$, and then replacing $b$ by $b^{-1}$ gives $a(b^{-1})^{-1} = ab \in H$. All three conditions fall out of the one.

For finite $H$ it's even easier: **a nonempty finite subset closed under the operation is automatically a subgroup.** Repeatedly multiplying $a$ by itself in a finite set must eventually repeat, which forces a power of $a$ to be the identity, and the previous power is then $a^{-1}$.

### Every group has at least two

$\{e\}$ and $G$ itself are always subgroups. They're called the **trivial** and **improper** subgroups, and any other is a **proper nontrivial** subgroup. Some groups have none: $\mathbb{Z}_7$ has only $\{0\}$ and itself.

### The identity must match

A subgroup's identity is forced to be the parent's identity. Suppose $f$ is the identity of $H$. Then $f f = f$ in $G$, and cancelling one $f$ (legal, since $f$ has an inverse in $G$) gives $f = e$.

The consequence is practical: a subset that omits the parent's identity is not a subgroup, no matter how well behaved it otherwise looks. The positive integers are closed under addition and never contain $0$, so they are not a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$.

### Generated subgroups

Pick $a \in G$. The **subgroup generated by $a$** is everything you can build from it:

$$
\langle a \rangle = \{ \dots, a^{-2}, a^{-1}, e, a, a^2, \dots \}
$$

This is always a subgroup, and it's the smallest one containing $a$. In additive notation it's all integer multiples of $a$.

In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, $\langle 3 \rangle = \{\dots, -6, -3, 0, 3, 6, \dots\} = 3\mathbb{Z}$. In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, $\langle 3 \rangle = \{0, 3, 6, 9\}$, which closes up after four steps because the group is finite.

$\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ has exactly six subgroups, one for each divisor of 12. That is not an accident and it is not typical of groups in general; it's a special property of cyclic groups, proved next lesson.

Two things to notice in the diagram. Every subgroup's order divides 12, which is **Lagrange's theorem** arriving early. And the lattice mirrors the divisor lattice of 12 exactly, with $\langle a \rangle \subseteq \langle b \rangle$ precisely when $b$ divides $a$. Larger generators give smaller subgroups, which reads backwards at first and is worth pausing on.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Is the set of even integers a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$?**

**Solution.** Use the one-step test on $H = 2\mathbb{Z}$.

Nonempty: $0 = 2 \cdot 0 \in H$. ✓

For $a = 2m$ and $b = 2n$ in $H$, the additive version of $ab^{-1}$ is $a - b$:

$$
a - b = 2m - 2n = 2(m - n) \in H
$$

**Yes**, $2\mathbb{Z} \le \mathbb{Z}$.

The odd integers are not, since they don't contain $0$ - and closure fails anyway.

**Example 2: Is $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$?**

**Solution.** Closed under addition ✓. But $0 \notin \mathbb{N}$, and no element has an inverse in $\mathbb{N}$ since $-5$ is not a positive integer.

**No.** This is the standard reminder that closure alone is not enough. Note the contrast with the finite case: for a _finite_ subset closure would have sufficed. Infinitude is exactly what lets $\mathbb{N}$ be closed without ever cycling back to an identity.

**Example 3: Find all subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_8$.**

**Solution.** Compute $\langle a \rangle$ for each element.

$$
\langle 0 \rangle = \{0\}
$$

$$
\langle 1 \rangle = \{0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7\} = \mathbb{Z}_8
$$

$$
\langle 2 \rangle = \{0, 2, 4, 6\}
$$

$$
\langle 3 \rangle = \{0,3,6,1,4,7,2,5\} = \mathbb{Z}_8
$$

$$
\langle 4 \rangle = \{0, 4\}
$$

$\langle 5 \rangle$, $\langle 7 \rangle$ also give all of $\mathbb{Z}_8$, and $\langle 6 \rangle = \{0,6,4,2\} = \langle 2 \rangle$.

The distinct subgroups are $\{0\}$, $\{0,4\}$, $\{0,2,4,6\}$, and $\mathbb{Z}_8$: **four subgroups**, of orders 1, 2, 4, 8, which are exactly the divisors of 8.

**Example 4: A subset closed under the operation but not a subgroup.**

In $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$, consider matrices with integer entries and determinant $\pm 1$... actually that _is_ a subgroup. Instead take $H$ = matrices with integer entries and nonzero determinant.

**Solution.** Closure ✓: a product of integer matrices has integer entries, and determinants multiply so the product's determinant is nonzero.

Inverses fail. For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, $\det A = 2 \ne 0$, and

$$
A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

which has no integer entries, so $A^{-1} \notin H$.

**Not a subgroup.** Requiring $\det = \pm 1$ fixes it, because then the inverse formula divides by $\pm 1$ and stays integral. That group is $GL_2(\mathbb{Z})$ and it matters in number theory.

**Example 5: Prove the intersection of two subgroups is a subgroup.**

**Solution.** Let $H, K \le G$ and let $a, b \in H \cap K$.

$H \cap K$ is nonempty because $e$ belongs to both.

Since $a, b \in H$ and $H$ is a subgroup, $ab^{-1} \in H$. Since $a, b \in K$ likewise $ab^{-1} \in K$. So $ab^{-1} \in H \cap K$.

By the one-step test, $H \cap K \le G$. ∎

The **union** is a different story. In $\mathbb{Z}$, take $H = 2\mathbb{Z}$ and $K = 3\mathbb{Z}$. Then $2, 3 \in H \cup K$ but $2 + 3 = 5$ is in neither. Unions of subgroups are almost never subgroups.

**Example 6: The center of a group.**

Show that $Z(G) = \{z \in G : zg = gz \text{ for all } g \in G\}$ is a subgroup.

**Solution.** $e \in Z(G)$ since $eg = g = ge$. ✓

Take $y, z \in Z(G)$ and any $g \in G$. First, $z^{-1}$ commutes with $g$: from $zg = gz$, multiply both sides by $z^{-1}$ on the left and right to get $gz^{-1} = z^{-1}g$.

Then

$$
(yz^{-1})g = y(z^{-1}g) = y(gz^{-1}) = (yg)z^{-1} = (gy)z^{-1} = g(yz^{-1})
$$

So $yz^{-1} \in Z(G)$, and $Z(G) \le G$. ∎

The **center** measures how far a group is from abelian: $Z(G) = G$ exactly when $G$ is abelian, and a small center means most elements fail to commute. For $D_4$ the center has just two elements out of eight.

## Real-World Applications

**Symmetry breaking in physics.** A physical system often has a large symmetry group at high energy, and as it cools the symmetry drops to a subgroup. The Higgs mechanism is exactly this: an electroweak symmetry group breaks to a subgroup, and the pattern of breaking determines which particles acquire mass.

**Crystallography.** A crystal's point group is a subgroup of the full rotation group, and which subgroup it is constrains its physical properties. Only crystals whose symmetry group lacks a centre of inversion can be piezoelectric, which is why quartz works in watches and many other crystals don't.

**Error-correcting codes.** A linear code is literally a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}_2^n, +)$: the set of valid codewords is closed under bitwise XOR and contains the all-zeros word. Decoding works by finding which coset of that subgroup a received word lies in, which is the topic of a later lesson.

**Rubik's cube subgroups.** Restricting yourself to a set of moves generates a subgroup, and solving methods work by descending a chain of nested subgroups, each easier to handle than the last. Thistlethwaite's algorithm is explicitly built as a chain of four subgroups.

**Music.** Within $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, the whole-tone scale is $\langle 2 \rangle = \{0,2,4,6,8,10\}$, a subgroup of order 6. The diminished seventh chord is $\langle 3 \rangle$, of order 4. These sound "symmetric" and rootless precisely because they are subgroups, invariant under transposition by their generator.

:::note[You've Got This]
Two things to hold onto. First, use the short test: nonempty, closed under the operation, closed under inverses - or the single condition $ab^{-1} \in H$. Never re-check associativity; it's inherited. Second, when you're asked whether a subset is a subgroup and you suspect not, check for the identity first, because that's the fastest disqualifier and it catches the most common candidates. The lattice picture is worth revisiting later too: it's the closest thing to a portrait of a group's internal structure.
:::
