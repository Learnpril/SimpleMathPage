## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn what a coset is, why cosets partition a group into equal-sized blocks, and how that single observation proves Lagrange's theorem, the most-used counting result in group theory.

## The Concept

### Cosets

Let $H \le G$ and $a \in G$. The **left coset** of $H$ by $a$ is

$$
aH = \{ah : h \in H\}
$$

Think of it as $H$ translated by $a$. There's a matching notion of **right coset** $Ha = \{ha : h \in H\}$.

Cosets are almost never subgroups. $aH$ contains the identity only when $a \in H$, in which case $aH = H$ exactly. So among all the cosets of $H$, precisely one is a subgroup: $H$ itself.

### Cosets partition the group

Here's the key structural fact, and everything else in the lesson is a consequence.

<CosetPartition />

**The left cosets of $H$ partition $G$.** They cover everything and never overlap. Two things to prove.

**Every element is in some coset.** For $a \in G$, we have $a = ae \in aH$ since $e \in H$.

**Two cosets are either identical or disjoint.** Suppose $aH$ and $bH$ share an element $x$. Then $x = ah_1 = bh_2$ for some $h_1, h_2 \in H$, so $a = bh_2h_1^{-1}$. Since $h_2h_1^{-1} \in H$, every element $ah$ of $aH$ equals $b(h_2h_1^{-1}h) \in bH$, giving $aH \subseteq bH$. The same argument the other way gives $bH \subseteq aH$, so the two cosets are equal.

So overlapping cosets coincide entirely. There's no partial overlap.

### All cosets have the same size

**$|aH| = |H|$ for every $a$.** The map $h \mapsto ah$ from $H$ to $aH$ is onto by definition, and it's injective by cancellation: $ah_1 = ah_2$ forces $h_1 = h_2$.

So a coset is a perfect copy of $H$, just relocated. Combined with the partition property, this is a very rigid picture: $G$ is tiled by non-overlapping blocks all of size $|H|$.

### Lagrange's theorem

Count the elements two ways and you're done.

> **Lagrange's theorem.** If $G$ is finite and $H \le G$, then
>
> $$
> |G| = [G:H] \cdot |H|
> $$
>
> In particular, **$|H|$ divides $|G|$.**

Proof: the cosets partition $G$ into $[G:H]$ blocks, each of size $|H|$. ∎

That's it. A two-line proof that constrains every finite group in existence.

### What Lagrange gives you

The divisibility statement is the workhorse.

**The order of every element divides $|G|$.** Because $|a| = |\langle a \rangle|$, and $\langle a \rangle$ is a subgroup.

**$a^{|G|} = e$ for every $a$.** Write $|G| = |a| \cdot k$; then $a^{|G|} = (a^{|a|})^k = e^k = e$.

**Every group of prime order is cyclic.** Any $a \ne e$ generates a subgroup of order dividing $p$ and bigger than 1, hence order $p$, hence everything.

**Groups of order 4 have no element of order 3.** Since 3 doesn't divide 4. This kind of instant elimination is what makes classification arguments possible.

### The converse is false

Lagrange says every subgroup's order divides $|G|$. It does **not** say that every divisor gives a subgroup.

$A_4$ has order 12, and $4 \mid 12$... but $A_4$ has no subgroup of order 6. So a divisor can fail to be realized. Cyclic groups do have a subgroup for every divisor, which is a special property of theirs, not a general truth.

Partial converses exist and are important. **Cauchy's theorem** says if a prime $p$ divides $|G|$ then $G$ has an element of order $p$. The **Sylow theorems** guarantee subgroups of prime-power order. But the naive converse is simply false.

### Left versus right

In an abelian group $aH = Ha$ always. In a non-abelian group they can differ.

In $S_3$, take $H = \{e, s\}$ where $s$ is a transposition. Then $rH = \{r, rs\}$ while $Hr = \{r, sr\} = \{r, r^2s\}$. Different sets.

The number of left cosets always equals the number of right cosets, so the index is unambiguous. But whether $aH = Ha$ for every $a$ is a special condition, and it's exactly the definition of a **normal** subgroup — the subject of the next lesson.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Find the cosets of $H = \{0, 3, 6, 9\}$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$.**

**Solution.** $H = \langle 3 \rangle$, of order 4.

$$
0 + H = \{0,3,6,9\} = H
$$

$$
1 + H = \{1,4,7,10\}
$$

$$
2 + H = \{2,5,8,11\}
$$

Now $3 + H = \{3,6,9,0\} = H$ again, so we're done. **Three cosets.**

$$
[G:H] = 3, \qquad |G| = 3 \cdot 4 = 12 \ \checkmark
$$

Notice these are exactly the residue classes mod 3, which is not a coincidence: cosets of $\langle 3 \rangle$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ record the remainder on division by 3.

**Example 2: Cosets of $3\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Z}$.**

**Solution.** $H = 3\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots,-3,0,3,6,\dots\}$.

$$
0 + H = \{\dots, -3, 0, 3, \dots\}, \quad 1 + H = \{\dots, -2, 1, 4, \dots\}, \quad 2 + H = \{\dots, -1, 2, 5, \dots\}
$$

$3 + H = H$, so there are exactly three: $[\mathbb{Z} : 3\mathbb{Z}] = 3$.

Both $|G|$ and $|H|$ are infinite here, so Lagrange's equation doesn't apply numerically, but the index is still a perfectly good finite number. **These three cosets are the congruence classes mod 3**, which is where the notation $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ comes from.

**Example 3: Show left and right cosets can differ.**

In $S_3 = \{e, r, r^2, s, rs, r^2s\}$ with $H = \{e, s\}$.

**Solution.** Using $sr = r^2s$:

$$
rH = \{r \cdot e, r \cdot s\} = \{r, rs\}
$$

$$
Hr = \{e \cdot r, s \cdot r\} = \{r, r^2 s\}
$$

$rH \ne Hr$ since $rs \ne r^2s$.

So $H$ is **not normal** in $S_3$. By contrast the rotation subgroup $\{e, r, r^2\}$ _is_ normal, and having index 2 is the reason, as the next lesson explains.

**Example 4: Use Lagrange to rule things out.**

A group $G$ has order 15. What element orders are possible?

**Solution.** Orders must divide 15, so they lie in $\{1, 3, 5, 15\}$.

Order 1 belongs to the identity alone. So every non-identity element has order 3, 5, or 15. **No element of order 2**, and hence no element is its own inverse except $e$.

A stronger conclusion is available: every group of order 15 is in fact cyclic, so there's exactly one. That needs the Sylow theorems, but Lagrange alone already eliminated most possibilities.

**Example 5: Fermat's little theorem from Lagrange.**

Prove that if $p$ is prime and $\gcd(a,p) = 1$ then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$.

**Solution.** Work in $U(p)$, the nonzero residues mod $p$ under multiplication. Since $p$ is prime, $|U(p)| = p - 1$.

The condition $\gcd(a,p)=1$ says $a \in U(p)$. By the corollary of Lagrange that $x^{|G|} = e$ for all $x$:

$$
a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p
$$

∎

A cornerstone of number theory, and it's a two-line consequence of counting cosets. The generalization to composite modulus, **Euler's theorem** $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$, is the same argument in $U(n)$, and it is what makes RSA decryption work.

**Example 6: A group of order 12 with no subgroup of order 6.**

**Solution.** Take $A_4$, the even permutations of four objects, $|A_4| = 12$.

Suppose $H \le A_4$ with $|H| = 6$. Then $[A_4 : H] = 2$, and any index-2 subgroup is normal, with $A_4/H \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$. So $x^2 \in H$ for every $x \in A_4$.

But $A_4$ contains eight 3-cycles, and for a 3-cycle $x$ we have $x^2 = x^{-1}$, also a 3-cycle. As $x$ ranges over all eight 3-cycles, $x^2$ does too, so $H$ would have to contain all eight of them plus the identity: at least 9 elements. That contradicts $|H| = 6$.

**No subgroup of order 6 exists**, even though $6 \mid 12$. This is the standard counterexample to the converse of Lagrange.

## Real-World Applications

**Error-correcting codes.** A linear code $C$ is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}_2^n, +)$, and the cosets of $C$ are the possible "error patterns." Decoding a received word means identifying which coset it lies in and correcting by that coset's most likely error, its **coset leader**. Standard-array decoding is literally a table of cosets, and Lagrange tells you how many rows it has.

**RSA.** The key equation $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}$ and the decryption step both rest on Euler's theorem, which is Lagrange applied to $U(n)$. Security comes from $\varphi(n)$ being hard to compute without factoring $n$.

**Rubik's cube.** God's number, the 20-move upper bound for solving any scramble, was proved in 2010 by partitioning the cube group into cosets of a large subgroup and searching each coset separately. Roughly two billion cosets were analysed; the coset decomposition is what made the problem finite enough to attack.

**Crystallography.** A crystal is described by a lattice group, and the distinct atom positions correspond to cosets of the site's stabilizer. The count of symmetry-inequivalent positions is an index computation.

**Balanced tournament design.** Scheduling round-robins and balanced incomplete block designs often uses cosets of a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ to generate rounds, guaranteeing every pair meets exactly once.

:::note[You've Got This]
Lagrange's theorem does more work than any other result in this section, and the proof is just careful counting: equal blocks, no overlaps, so the sizes multiply. Keep the tiling picture in your head. The two things people trip over are worth naming: the converse is false, so a divisor need not give a subgroup, and left and right cosets can genuinely differ in a non-abelian group. That second point isn't a nuisance — it's the door into normal subgroups, which is the next lesson and the gateway to everything after it.
:::
