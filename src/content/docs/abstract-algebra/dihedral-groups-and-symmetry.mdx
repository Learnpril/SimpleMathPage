## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn what the dihedral group $D_n$ is, how to compute in it using generators and relations, why reflections don't commute with rotations, and how the same ideas describe symmetry in three dimensions.

## The Concept

### Symmetries of a shape

A **symmetry** of a figure is a motion that leaves it looking unchanged. Symmetries always form a group: doing nothing is the identity, any symmetry can be reversed, and one symmetry after another is again a symmetry.

For a regular $n$-gon there are exactly $2n$ of them, and they make up the **dihedral group** $D_n$:

- $n$ **rotations**, by multiples of $360°/n$.
- $n$ **reflections**, across lines through the centre.

$$
|D_n| = 2n
$$

A warning on notation: some books write $D_{2n}$ for this group, emphasizing its order rather than the polygon's side count. This site uses $D_n$ for the $n$-gon, so $|D_4| = 8$.

### The eight symmetries of a square

<DihedralSquare />

For a square, the reflection axes come in two flavours: two through opposite edge midpoints, and two through opposite corners. For odd $n$ every axis passes through one vertex and the opposite edge's midpoint, so all $n$ reflections look alike. That asymmetry between even and odd $n$ shows up repeatedly.

### Generators and relations

Every element of $D_n$ is built from two moves: one rotation $r$ and one reflection $s$. Every element can be written as

$$
r^k \quad \text{or} \quad r^k s, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1
$$

That's $n + n = 2n$ elements, as promised. Three relations govern all computation:

$$
r^n = e, \qquad s^2 = e, \qquad srs = r^{-1}
$$

The last one is the important one, usually written $sr = r^{-1}s$. It says **a reflection turns a rotation into its opposite**, which is why $D_n$ is non-abelian for $n \ge 3$.

Physically this is obvious once you try it. Flip a square, then rotate it clockwise; that's the same as rotating counter-clockwise first and then flipping. Reflecting reverses your sense of "clockwise."

Writing a group as generators plus relations is called a **presentation**:

$$
D_n = \langle r, s \mid r^n = s^2 = e,\ srs = r^{-1} \rangle
$$

This is a complete rulebook. Any product of $r$'s and $s$'s can be pushed into the standard form $r^k$ or $r^k s$ by repeatedly applying the relations, so you never need the multiplication table.

### Computing with the relations

The move that matters: whenever you see $sr$, rewrite it as $r^{-1}s$. That pushes every $s$ to the right end. For example in $D_5$:

$$
(r^2 s)(r^3 s) = r^2 (s r^3) s = r^2 (r^{-3} s) s = r^{-1} s^2 = r^{-1} = r^4
$$

The step $sr^3 = r^{-3}s$ follows from applying $sr = r^{-1}s$ three times.

Two useful facts fall out. **Every element of the form $r^k s$ has order 2**, because

$$
(r^k s)(r^k s) = r^k (s r^k) s = r^k r^{-k} s^2 = e
$$

So all $n$ reflections are self-inverse, which matches the intuition that flipping twice does nothing. And **the rotations form a cyclic subgroup** $\langle r \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_n$ of index 2.

### Symmetry in three dimensions

The same reasoning works on solids, where the groups get bigger and more interesting.

<RotationGroup3D />

For the cube, count the rotations by choosing which face points up (6 ways) and then how it is spun about the vertical axis (4 ways): $6 \times 4 = 24$ rotations. Remarkably this group is isomorphic to $S_4$, because each rotation permutes the cube's four long diagonals, and every permutation of those four diagonals is achievable.

Including reflections gives 48 symmetries, the **full** symmetry group of the cube. The distinction between rotations only (**proper** symmetries) and rotations plus reflections matters in chemistry, where a molecule and its mirror image can behave very differently.

All rotations of 3D space, not just those preserving a particular solid, form the infinite non-abelian group $SO(3)$. Every symmetry group of a solid is a finite subgroup of it, and there is a complete classification of those subgroups: the cyclic groups, the dihedral groups, and exactly three exceptional ones from the tetrahedron, cube/octahedron, and dodecahedron/icosahedron.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: List the elements of $D_3$.**

**Solution.** $|D_3| = 6$. Rotations: $e$, $r$ (120°), $r^2$ (240°). Reflections: $s$, $rs$, $r^2s$, one through each vertex.

$$
D_3 = \{e,\ r,\ r^2,\ s,\ rs,\ r^2s\}
$$

$D_3 \cong S_3$: the symmetries of a triangle are exactly the permutations of its three vertices, and both groups have order 6. This is the only $n$ for which $D_n$ and $S_n$ coincide, since $|D_n| = 2n$ and $|S_n| = n!$ agree only at $n = 3$.

**Example 2: Simplify $s r^2 s r$ in $D_4$.**

**Solution.** Push each $s$ rightwards using $sr = r^{-1}s$.

$$
s r^2 s r = (s r^2) s r = (r^{-2} s) s r = r^{-2} s^2 r = r^{-2} r = r^{-1} = r^3
$$

The answer is $r^3$, a 270° rotation. Sanity check: the expression contains two reflections, and two reflections always compose to a rotation, so a rotation is the right kind of answer.

**Example 3: Find the order of every element of $D_4$.**

**Solution.**

- $e$: order 1.
- $r$: order 4, since $r^4 = e$ and no smaller power is $e$.
- $r^2$: order 2, since $(r^2)^2 = r^4 = e$.
- $r^3$: order 4.
- $s$, $rs$, $r^2s$, $r^3s$: order 2 each, by the general fact that $(r^ks)^2 = e$.

So $D_4$ has one element of order 1, **five** of order 2, and two of order 4. Note every order divides 8, as Lagrange requires, but there is **no element of order 8**, so $D_4$ is not cyclic.

Compare $\mathbb{Z}_8$, which also has order 8 but has an element of order 8. Both have order 8; they are different groups. So do $\mathbb{Z}_4 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$, $\mathbb{Z}_2^3$, and the quaternion group $Q_8$ - five groups of order 8 in total.

**Example 4: Is $D_4$ abelian?**

**Solution.** No. Take $r$ and $s$:

$$
rs \quad \text{versus} \quad sr = r^{-1}s = r^3 s
$$

These differ because $r \ne r^3$ in $D_4$. **Non-abelian.**

$D_1$ has order 2 and $D_2$ has order 4, and both are abelian. From $n = 3$ upward, $r \ne r^{-1}$ and the group is non-abelian.

**Example 5: The center of $D_4$.**

**Solution.** The center is the elements commuting with everything.

$r$ fails, since $rs \ne sr$. Any reflection $r^ks$ fails, since it doesn't commute with $r$ by the same relation.

Test $r^2$: it must commute with $s$. Using the relation twice, $sr^2 = r^{-2}s = r^2s$ in $D_4$ because $r^{-2} = r^2$ when $r^4 = e$. So $r^2$ **does** commute with $s$, and it commutes with all rotations automatically.

$$
Z(D_4) = \{e, r^2\}
$$

The 180° rotation is central because it is its own inverse, so the reflection has nothing to reverse. For odd $n$, $Z(D_n) = \{e\}$, since there is no half-turn.

**Example 6: Subgroups of $D_4$.**

**Solution.** By Lagrange, orders can only be 1, 2, 4, 8.

- Order 1: $\{e\}$.
- Order 2: $\{e, r^2\}$, and $\{e, x\}$ for each of the four reflections. **Five** of them.
- Order 4: $\langle r \rangle = \{e,r,r^2,r^3\}$; $\{e, r^2, s, r^2s\}$; $\{e, r^2, rs, r^3s\}$. **Three**, and the last two are Klein four-groups.
- Order 8: $D_4$ itself.

Ten subgroups in all. Contrast $\mathbb{Z}_8$, which has exactly four. **Cyclic groups have one subgroup per divisor; other groups can have many more**, and that difference is a good measure of how much structure a group has.

## Real-World Applications

**Crystallography and materials.** A crystal's rotational symmetry must be compatible with a repeating lattice, and that constraint permits only 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-fold rotation axes. Five-fold symmetry is impossible in a periodic crystal, which is why the 1984 discovery of quasicrystals with 5-fold diffraction patterns was so startling and eventually earned a Nobel Prize.

**Chemistry.** Molecular point groups are dihedral or related groups, and they determine which vibrational modes absorb infrared light. Benzene has $D_{6h}$ symmetry, and its spectrum is a direct readout of that group's structure. Whether a molecule is **chiral**, meaning distinguishable from its mirror image, is exactly the question of whether its symmetry group contains any reflection, which has direct pharmacological consequences.

**Computer graphics and robotics.** Composing rotations is group multiplication in $SO(3)$, and because it's non-abelian, applying rotations in a different order gives a different result. Unit quaternions provide a double cover of $SO(3)$ and avoid the gimbal-lock degeneracies of Euler angles, which is why nearly every game engine and spacecraft attitude controller uses them.

**Architecture and design.** Rose windows, tiling patterns and logos are built from dihedral symmetry, and the group tells you exactly how many distinct motifs you need to draw: one, repeated by the group action. Islamic geometric art systematically explores wallpaper groups, of which there are exactly 17.

**Physics.** Conservation laws come from continuous symmetry groups by Noether's theorem, while discrete symmetries govern selection rules. The observed violation of parity symmetry in the weak interaction was a genuine shock precisely because reflection had been assumed to be a symmetry of nature.

:::note[You've Got This]
The one relation to memorize is $sr = r^{-1}s$, and the physical picture behind it is worth keeping: reflecting reverses your sense of rotation. With that single rewriting rule you can simplify any product in $D_n$ without ever consulting a table, which is the real payoff of a presentation. The lesson's other takeaway is that order 8 admits five different groups, so counting elements tells you very little. Structure is the thing, and the next few lessons build the tools for pinning it down.
:::
