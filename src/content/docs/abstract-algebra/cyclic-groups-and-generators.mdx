## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn what makes a group cyclic, how to find the order of an element, which elements generate a cyclic group, and the classification theorem that says there is essentially only one cyclic group of each size.

## The Concept

### The order of an element

The **order** of an element $a$, written $|a|$, is the smallest positive integer $n$ with

$$
a^n = e
$$

If no such $n$ exists, $a$ has **infinite order**.

In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, the element $3$ has order 4, because $3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12 \equiv 0$ and no smaller count reaches $0$. In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, every nonzero element has infinite order, since no number of copies of $5$ ever sums to $0$.

Note the collision flagged in an earlier lesson: $|G|$ is how many elements the group has, while $|a|$ is a property of one element. Same notation, different meanings, distinguished only by whether the thing inside is a group or an element.

### Cyclic groups

$G$ is **cyclic** if there is a single element $a$ with

$$
G = \langle a \rangle
$$

Such an $a$ is a **generator**. One element, applied over and over, produces the whole group.

Every cyclic group is abelian, and the proof is one line: $a^i a^j = a^{i+j} = a^j a^i$, since integer addition commutes. The converse is false — the Klein four-group is abelian and not cyclic — so "cyclic" is strictly stronger.

### Which elements generate?

<CyclicClock />

In $\mathbb{Z}_n$, the element $k$ generates the whole group exactly when

$$
\gcd(k, n) = 1
$$

More generally, $\langle k \rangle$ has order

$$
|k| = \frac{n}{\gcd(k, n)}
$$

Here's why. Repeatedly adding $k$ produces the multiples of $k$ mod $n$, and you first return to $0$ at the least common multiple of $k$ and $n$, which is $\frac{kn}{\gcd(k,n)}$. Dividing by $k$ gives the number of steps, $\frac{n}{\gcd(k,n)}$. When the gcd is 1 that count is $n$, so you visit everything.

So $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ has generators $1, 5, 7, 11$: four of them, which is $\varphi(12)$. In general **a cyclic group of order $n$ has exactly $\varphi(n)$ generators.**

A prime-order group is the extreme case: $\mathbb{Z}_7$ has $\varphi(7) = 6$ generators, meaning every element except the identity generates the whole thing.

### Subgroups of cyclic groups

Cyclic groups have the tidiest subgroup structure in all of group theory.

**Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.** And if $|G| = n$, then $G$ has **exactly one subgroup of order $d$ for each divisor $d$ of $n$**, and no others.

That's why the lattice of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ from the previous lesson had exactly six nodes, matching the six divisors of 12. The subgroup of order $d$ is generated by $n/d$.

This is a strong property, and most groups don't have it. $D_4$ has order 8 but five different subgroups of order 2.

### The classification of cyclic groups

Here is the first classification theorem in the section, and it is remarkably clean.

- Every **infinite** cyclic group is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$.
- Every **finite** cyclic group of order $n$ is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Z}_n, +)$.

So up to renaming, there is exactly **one** cyclic group of each size. When you understand $\mathbb{Z}_n$, you understand every cyclic group of order $n$ that anyone will ever show you.

The reason is easy to see: if $G = \langle a \rangle$ has order $n$, matching $a^k \leftrightarrow k$ turns $G$'s multiplication into addition mod $n$, and that dictionary is a perfect translation.

### A consequence worth remembering

**Every group of prime order is cyclic.** Take $|G| = p$ prime and any $a \ne e$. The subgroup $\langle a \rangle$ has order dividing $p$ (Lagrange, coming shortly), so its order is $1$ or $p$. It isn't 1 because $a \ne e$. So $\langle a \rangle = G$.

That settles the structure of infinitely many groups in three lines. There is exactly one group of order 7, one of order 11, one of order 101.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Find the order of each element of $\mathbb{Z}_{10}$.**

**Solution.** Use $|k| = \frac{10}{\gcd(k,10)}$.

| $k$ | $\gcd(k,10)$ | $\|k\|$ |
| :-: | :----------: | :-----: |
|  0  |      10      |    1    |
|  1  |      1       |   10    |
|  2  |      2       |    5    |
|  3  |      1       |   10    |
|  4  |      2       |    5    |
|  5  |      5       |    2    |
|  6  |      2       |    5    |
|  7  |      1       |   10    |
|  8  |      2       |    5    |
|  9  |      1       |   10    |

Generators are $1, 3, 7, 9$, and $\varphi(10) = 4$ confirms the count. Every order in the table divides 10.

**Example 2: Is $U(9)$ cyclic?**

**Solution.** $U(9) = \{1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8\}$, the residues coprime to 9, so $|U(9)| = \varphi(9) = 6$.

Try 2 as a generator, computing powers mod 9:

$$
2^1 = 2, \quad 2^2 = 4, \quad 2^3 = 8, \quad 2^4 = 16 \equiv 7, \quad 2^5 = 14 \equiv 5, \quad 2^6 = 10 \equiv 1
$$

All six elements appear before returning to 1. **Yes, cyclic**, with $U(9) = \langle 2 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_6$.

Contrast $U(8) = \{1,3,5,7\}$ from an earlier lesson, where every element squares to 1, so it is not cyclic. Whether $U(n)$ is cyclic is a genuinely delicate question: it is cyclic exactly when $n$ is $1, 2, 4$, a power of an odd prime, or twice such a power.

**Example 3: Find all generators of $\mathbb{Z}_{18}$.**

**Solution.** Need $\gcd(k, 18) = 1$ with $18 = 2 \cdot 3^2$, so exclude multiples of 2 and 3.

$$
\{1, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17\}
$$

Six generators, and $\varphi(18) = 18 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = 6$. ✓

**Example 4: Find the subgroup of order 6 in $\mathbb{Z}_{24}$.**

**Solution.** For a subgroup of order $d$ in $\mathbb{Z}_n$, the generator is $n/d$. Here $24/6 = 4$:

$$
\langle 4 \rangle = \{0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20\}
$$

Six elements ✓. And it is the _only_ subgroup of order 6, by the classification of cyclic subgroups.

**Example 5: The order of an element in a product.**

Find the order of $(2, 3)$ in $\mathbb{Z}_4 \times \mathbb{Z}_6$.

**Solution.** The order of a pair is the least common multiple of the component orders.

In $\mathbb{Z}_4$: $|2| = \frac{4}{\gcd(2,4)} = 2$. In $\mathbb{Z}_6$: $|3| = \frac{6}{\gcd(3,6)} = 2$.

$$
|(2,3)| = \operatorname{lcm}(2, 2) = 2
$$

Check directly: $(2,3) + (2,3) = (4 \bmod 4, 6 \bmod 6) = (0,0)$. ✓

Since $|\mathbb{Z}_4 \times \mathbb{Z}_6| = 24$ and the largest possible element order is $\operatorname{lcm}(4,6) = 12 < 24$, **no element generates the group**, so it is not cyclic.

**Example 6: Prove every group of order 5 is cyclic.**

**Solution.** Let $|G| = 5$ and pick $a \ne e$. Consider $H = \langle a \rangle$.

$H$ is a subgroup, so by Lagrange's theorem $|H|$ divides 5. The divisors of 5 are 1 and 5.

$|H| = 1$ would mean $H = \{e\}$, contradicting $a \in H$ with $a \ne e$.

So $|H| = 5 = |G|$, giving $H = G$. Therefore $G$ is cyclic and $G \cong \mathbb{Z}_5$. ∎

The same argument works for any prime, so there is exactly one group of each prime order.

## Real-World Applications

**Diffie-Hellman key exchange** picks a large prime $p$ and a generator $g$ of the cyclic group $U(p)$. Alice sends $g^a$, Bob sends $g^b$, and each computes $g^{ab}$. An eavesdropper sees $g^a$ and $g^b$ and must solve the **discrete logarithm problem** to recover $a$. Generating the whole group matters: if $g$ generated only a small subgroup, the search space would collapse and the scheme would break. Choosing a bad generator is a real class of implementation bug.

**Pseudorandom number generators.** A linear congruential generator computes $x_{n+1} = (ax_n + c) \bmod m$. Its period is maximal exactly when certain coprimality conditions hold, which is the same gcd condition that decides generators in $\mathbb{Z}_m$. Get it wrong and your "random" sequence cycles after a few hundred values.

**The circle of fifths.** Pitch classes form $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, and moving up a fifth is adding 7. Since $\gcd(7,12) = 1$, repeated fifths visit all twelve keys before returning, which is why the circle of fifths closes exactly once around. Stacking major thirds instead means adding 4, and $\gcd(4,12) = 4$, so you get a 3-cycle: an augmented triad.

**Gear ratios and cicadas.** Two meshed gears with $m$ and $n$ teeth return to their starting alignment after $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$ teeth, so coprime tooth counts spread wear evenly. The same arithmetic explains why periodical cicadas emerge on 13- and 17-year cycles: prime periods maximize the time before re-synchronizing with a predator's cycle.

**Hash tables.** Open addressing with a probe step $k$ modulo table size $m$ visits every slot exactly when $\gcd(k, m) = 1$. Implementations often pick a prime table size for precisely this reason: with $m$ prime, any nonzero step works.

:::note[You've Got This]
Cyclic groups are the friendliest objects in the section, and it's worth enjoying that while it lasts. Almost everything reduces to one formula, $|k| = n/\gcd(k,n)$, and one classification: there's only one cyclic group per size. Keep the clock picture in mind — a generator is a step size that eventually lands on every hour, and gcd is what decides whether it does. If you can compute element orders in ℤₙ quickly, you'll find Lagrange's theorem in two lessons feels almost obvious.
:::
