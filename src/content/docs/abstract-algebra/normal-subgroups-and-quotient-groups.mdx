## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn what makes a subgroup normal, why normality is exactly what's needed to multiply cosets, how to build the quotient group $G/N$, and what quotients are for. This is the traditional hard spot in a first course, so it's worth going slowly.

## The Concept

### The problem cosets create

We want to make the cosets of $H$ into a group. The obvious rule is

$$
(aH)(bH) = abH
$$

But there's a catch. A coset has many names: if $a' \in aH$ then $a'H = aH$. For the rule to define anything at all, the answer has to come out the same whichever representative you pick. **The operation must be well-defined**, and for a general subgroup it isn't.

Concretely, in $S_3$ with $H = \{e, s\}$: we have $rH = \{r, rs\}$, so $r$ and $rs$ name the same coset. Multiply on the left by $sH$:

- Using $r$: $(sH)(rH) = srH = r^2sH = \{r^2s, r^2\}$
- Using $rs$: $(sH)(rsH) = s(rs)H = (r^2s)sH = r^2 H = \{r^2, r^2s\}$

Those happen to agree. Try instead $(rH)(sH)$ two ways: with $r$, get $rsH = \{rs, r\}$; with $rs$, get $(rs)(s)H = rH = \{r, rs\}$. Also agree. But now compute $(rH)(rH)$: using $r$ twice gives $r^2H = \{r^2, r^2s\}$; using $r$ and $rs$ gives $r(rs)H = r^2sH = \{r^2s, r^2\}$. Still fine — but the general pattern breaks for other choices, and the theorem below says exactly when.

### Normal subgroups

$N \le G$ is **normal**, written $N \trianglelefteq G$, if any of these equivalent conditions holds:

1. $gN = Ng$ for all $g \in G$ — left and right cosets coincide.
2. $gNg^{-1} = N$ for all $g \in G$ — conjugation preserves $N$.
3. $gng^{-1} \in N$ for all $g \in G$, $n \in N$ — the practical test.

Condition 3 is what you actually check. Note it does **not** say $gng^{-1} = n$; the conjugate may be a different element of $N$, it just has to stay inside.

**Conjugation** $n \mapsto gng^{-1}$ means "do $g$, then $n$, then undo $g$." It's the same operation viewed from a relabelled position. So normality says: **the subgroup looks the same from everywhere in the group.**

Some automatic cases:

- In an **abelian** group, every subgroup is normal, since $gng^{-1} = ngg^{-1} = n$.
- Any subgroup of **index 2** is normal. With only two cosets, $N$ and everything else, there's no room for left and right to differ.
- $\{e\}$ and $G$ are always normal.
- The **center** $Z(G)$ is always normal, since its elements commute with everything.

### Quotient groups

> If $N \trianglelefteq G$, the set of cosets $G/N$ with $(aN)(bN) = abN$ **is a group**, of order $[G:N] = |G|/|N|$.

Normality is exactly what makes the operation well-defined. Here's the check: suppose $aN = a'N$ and $bN = b'N$, so $a' = an_1$ and $b' = bn_2$. Then

$$
a'b' = an_1bn_2 = ab(b^{-1}n_1b)n_2
$$

Normality makes $b^{-1}n_1b \in N$, so $a'b' \in abN$, and the answer is the same coset. Without normality that middle term escapes and the rule collapses.

The quotient's identity is $N$ itself, and $(aN)^{-1} = a^{-1}N$.

### What a quotient does

Forming $G/N$ **deliberately forgets information**: it declares everything in $N$ to be equivalent to the identity. What survives is whatever $N$ couldn't see.

The clearest example is $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}_n$. Quotienting the integers by the multiples of $n$ throws away everything except the remainder. Clock arithmetic is a quotient group, and you've been using one since childhood.

That's the standard use: a quotient keeps the feature you care about and discards the rest.

### Simple groups

A group with no normal subgroups other than $\{e\}$ and itself is **simple**. Simple groups can't be broken down by quotients, which makes them the atoms of finite group theory.

$\mathbb{Z}_p$ for prime $p$ is simple. $A_5$, of order 60, is the smallest non-abelian simple group. The classification of all finite simple groups took fifty years and ten thousand pages, and $A_5$'s simplicity is precisely why the quintic has no solution formula.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Show every subgroup of an abelian group is normal.**

**Solution.** Let $G$ be abelian, $H \le G$, $g \in G$, $h \in H$.

$$
ghg^{-1} = hgg^{-1} = he = h \in H
$$

The first step uses commutativity. So condition 3 holds and $H \trianglelefteq G$. ∎

This is why normality never comes up in $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}_n$: it's automatic.

**Example 2: Show $\{e, s\}$ is not normal in $S_3$.**

**Solution.** Test condition 3 with $g = r$, $n = s$, using $sr = r^2s$ so $sr^{-1} = sr^2 = rs$:

$$
rsr^{-1} = r(sr^{-1}) = r(rs) = r^2s
$$

Is $r^2s \in \{e, s\}$? No.

**Not normal.** Geometrically: conjugating a reflection by a rotation gives a _different_ reflection, because rotating the whole picture moves the mirror line.

**Example 3: Show $A_n$ is normal in $S_n$.**

**Solution.** $A_n$ has index 2 in $S_n$, so it is normal immediately.

For the direct argument, take $\sigma \in A_n$ (even) and any $\tau \in S_n$. Signs multiply, so

$$
\operatorname{sign}(\tau\sigma\tau^{-1}) = \operatorname{sign}(\tau)\operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\operatorname{sign}(\tau)^{-1} = \operatorname{sign}(\sigma) = +1
$$

The conjugate is even, hence in $A_n$. ∎

**Example 4: Build $\mathbb{Z}_{12}/\langle 4 \rangle$.**

**Solution.** $N = \langle 4 \rangle = \{0,4,8\}$, $|N| = 3$, so the quotient has order $12/3 = 4$.

Cosets: $\{0,4,8\}$, $\{1,5,9\}$, $\{2,6,10\}$, $\{3,7,11\}$, which we name $[0], [1], [2], [3]$.

Addition: $[1] + [1] = [2]$, $[2]+[2] = [4] = [0]$, $[1]+[3] = [4] = [0]$.

So $[1]$ has order 4 and generates:

$$
\mathbb{Z}_{12}/\langle 4 \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_4
$$

Pattern worth remembering: $\mathbb{Z}_n / \langle d \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_d$ when $d \mid n$.

**Example 5: A quotient that loses non-abelianness.**

Compute $D_4/\{e, r^2\}$.

**Solution.** $N = \{e, r^2\}$ is the center of $D_4$, so it's normal. $|N| = 2$ and $|D_4| = 8$, so the quotient has order 4.

Cosets: $\{e,r^2\}$, $\{r, r^3\}$, $\{s, r^2s\}$, $\{rs, r^3s\}$.

In the quotient, $(rN)^2 = r^2N = N$, the identity. Likewise $(sN)^2 = N$ and $(rsN)^2 = N$. Every non-identity element squares to the identity, so

$$
D_4/Z(D_4) \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2
$$

the Klein four-group. **A non-abelian group has an abelian quotient.** That's typical: quotienting by the center forgets exactly the information that made things fail to commute.

**Example 6: Why $A_4$ has no subgroup of order 6, revisited.**

**Solution.** Suppose $H \le A_4$ with $|H| = 6$. Index 2 makes $H$ normal, so $A_4/H \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$.

In a group of order 2 every element satisfies $x^2 = e$, so for every $g \in A_4$ we get $(gH)^2 = H$, meaning $g^2 \in H$.

Now take any 3-cycle $g$. Then $g^2 = g^{-1}$ is also a 3-cycle, and as $g$ runs over the eight 3-cycles of $A_4$ so does $g^2$. So $H$ contains all eight 3-cycles plus $e$: at least 9 elements.

That contradicts $|H| = 6$. **No such subgroup.** ∎

The quotient did the work: assuming the subgroup existed forced a constraint on every element, and counting broke it.

## Real-World Applications

**Modular arithmetic.** Every time you compute a day of the week, a clock time, or a checksum, you're working in the quotient $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. The quotient construction is what makes "it's the same as 3 o'clock" a legitimate equation rather than sloppiness.

**Error-correcting codes.** For a linear code $C \le \mathbb{Z}_2^n$, the quotient $\mathbb{Z}_2^n / C$ is the **syndrome** space. A decoder computes which coset a received word falls in, and the syndrome is the name of that coset. Syndrome decoding is the quotient group used as an algorithm.

**Homology in topology.** Homology groups are quotients: cycles modulo boundaries. The quotient is precisely what detects holes, because it declares "boundaries don't count" and sees what's left. This is the mechanism behind topological data analysis.

**Physics and gauge theory.** Physical states are often defined up to a symmetry, meaning the real state space is a quotient. Gauge invariance says two mathematically different field configurations describe the same physics, and the quotient by the gauge group is the physically meaningful object.

**Cryptography.** Elliptic curve cryptography works in a quotient of a group of points, and RSA works in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. Quotients are what make these groups finite and computable.

**Music theory.** Pitch classes are a quotient: the group of all pitches modulo octave equivalence gives $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$. Saying "C is C regardless of octave" is forming a quotient group.

:::note[You've Got This]
This is the lesson people find hardest, so if it felt steep, that's the material and not you. Two anchors will get you through. First, normality is the condition $gng^{-1} \in N$, and its meaning is "the subgroup looks the same from every vantage point in the group." Second, a quotient is deliberate forgetting: ℤ/nℤ throws away everything but the remainder, and you've trusted that construction your whole life without calling it a quotient group. When the abstraction gets slippery, compute a small example in ℤ₁₂ and the fog lifts fast.
:::
