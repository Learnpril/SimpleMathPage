## What You'll Learn

This review covers everything from Calculus 3: vectors in 3D space, vector functions, partial derivatives, multiple integrals, vector fields, line and surface integrals, and the three big theorems (Green's, Stokes', Divergence). Use this as your final checkpoint.

## Key Concepts Summary

### 1. Vectors and Geometry in 3D

The dot product $\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = \|\mathbf{A}\|\|\mathbf{B}\|\cos\theta$ measures how aligned two vectors are. The cross product $\mathbf{A} \times \mathbf{B}$ gives a vector perpendicular to both, with magnitude equal to the area of the parallelogram they form. Lines in 3D are parametrized as $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{v}$, and planes are described by $\mathbf{n} \cdot (\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0) = 0$.

### 2. Vector Functions and Space Curves

A space curve is parametrized by $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle$. The tangent vector is $\mathbf{r}'(t)$, the unit tangent is $\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{r}'/\|\mathbf{r}'\|$, and arc length is $L = \int_a^b \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| \, dt$. Curvature $\kappa$ measures how fast the curve turns.

### 3. Partial Derivatives and the Gradient

For $f(x,y,z)$, partial derivatives $f_x, f_y, f_z$ measure the rate of change in each direction independently. The gradient $\nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y, f_z \rangle$ points in the direction of steepest ascent. The directional derivative in direction $\mathbf{u}$ is $D_\mathbf{u}f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$. Tangent planes and linear approximations extend the idea of tangent lines to surfaces.

### 4. Multiple Integrals

Double integrals compute area, volume, and accumulated quantities over 2D regions. Triple integrals extend this to 3D. The key is choosing the right coordinate system:

- Rectangular: $dA = dx\,dy$, $dV = dx\,dy\,dz$
- Polar: $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$
- Cylindrical: $dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$
- Spherical: $dV = \rho^2 \sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta$

The extra factors (r, $\rho^2\sin\varphi$) are Jacobians that account for how the coordinate grid stretches.

### 5. Vector Fields, Line Integrals, and Surface Integrals

A vector field assigns a vector to each point: $\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle P, Q, R \rangle$. The line integral $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ computes work done by the field along a path. The surface integral $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ computes flux through a surface. Conservative fields have a potential function $f$ where $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$, and their line integrals are path-independent.

### 6. The Three Big Theorems

These theorems connect integrals across different dimensions. They all say the same thing: what happens on the boundary determines what's happening inside.

Green's Theorem (2D): line integral around a closed curve = double integral over the enclosed region

$$
\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_D \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA
$$

Stokes' Theorem (3D surfaces): surface integral of curl = line integral around the boundary

$$
\iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}
$$

Divergence Theorem (3D volumes): flux through a closed surface = triple integral of divergence inside

$$
\oiint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_E (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F})\,dV
$$

Curl measures rotation. Divergence measures sources and sinks. If curl F = 0 (and the domain is simply connected), F is conservative. If div F = 0, the field is incompressible.

## Worked Review Examples

**Example 1: Divergence Theorem**

Compute the outward flux of $\mathbf{F} = \langle x, y, z \rangle$ through the unit sphere.

<Sphere3D />

Instead of parametrizing the sphere, use the Divergence Theorem. The divergence is:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3
$$

Integrate over the unit ball:

$$
\oiint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_E 3\,dV = 3 \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi = 4\pi
$$

**Example 2: Stokes' Theorem**

Evaluate the circulation of $\mathbf{F} = \langle -y, x, 0 \rangle$ around the unit circle in the xy-plane.

import GreensTheoremDiagram from "../../../components/GreensTheoremDiagram.astro";

<GreensTheoremDiagram />

Compute the curl:

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \langle 0, 0, 2 \rangle
$$

The flat unit disk has upward normal $\mathbf{n} = \langle 0,0,1 \rangle$, so:

$$
\iint_D (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iint_D 2\,dA = 2\pi
$$

**Example 3: Volume in Spherical Coordinates**

Find the volume of the unit ball $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \leq 1$.

In spherical coordinates, $\rho$ (rho) is the distance from the origin, $\varphi$ (phi) is the angle from the positive z-axis, and $\theta$ (theta) is the angle in the xy-plane. The volume element is $dV = \rho^2 \sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta$.

$$
V = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi} \int_0^{1} \rho^2 \sin\varphi \, d\rho \, d\varphi \, d\theta
$$

The innermost integral:

$$
\int_0^1 \rho^2 \, d\rho = \frac{1}{3}
$$

The middle integral:

$$
\int_0^{\pi} \sin\varphi \, d\varphi = 2
$$

The outer integral:

$$
\int_0^{2\pi} d\theta = 2\pi
$$

Multiply them together: $V = \frac{1}{3} \cdot 2 \cdot 2\pi = \frac{4\pi}{3}$.

**Example 4: Line Integral (Work)**

Compute the work done by $\mathbf{F} = \langle -y, x \rangle$ along the unit circle from (1,0) back to (1,0), counterclockwise.

import RotationalField2D from "../../../components/RotationalField2D.astro";

<RotationalField2D />

Parametrize: $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t \rangle$ for $t$ from 0 to $2\pi$.

$$
\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -\sin t, \cos t \rangle
$$

$$
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle -\sin t, \cos t \rangle
$$

$$
\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{r}' = (-\sin t)(-\sin t) + (\cos t)(\cos t) = \sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1
$$

$$
W = \int_0^{2\pi} 1\,dt = 2\pi
$$

**Example 5: Green's Theorem**

Evaluate $\oint_C (x^2 + y)\,dx + (3x - y)\,dy$ where C is the unit circle.

Using Green's Theorem with $P = x^2 + y$ and $Q = 3x - y$:

$$
\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 3 - 1 = 2
$$

$$
\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_D 2\,dA = 2 \cdot \pi(1)^2 = 2\pi
$$

**Example 6: Curl of a Rotational Field**

For $\mathbf{F} = \langle -y, x, 0 \rangle$, compute the curl.

import CurlExample2D from "../../../components/CurlExample2D.astro";

<CurlExample2D />

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \left\langle \frac{\partial 0}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial z},\; \frac{\partial(-y)}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial 0}{\partial x},\; \frac{\partial x}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial(-y)}{\partial y} \right\rangle = \langle 0, 0, 2 \rangle
$$

The curl is a constant vector pointing straight up, confirming uniform counterclockwise rotation in the xy-plane with strength 2.

## Real-World and Game Development Applications

- Gauss's Law: the Divergence Theorem applied to electric fields. Total electric flux through a closed surface equals the enclosed charge.
- Faraday's Law: Stokes' Theorem applied to the electric field. The induced voltage around a loop equals the rate of change of magnetic flux.
- Fluid dynamics: divergence tells you where fluid is being created or destroyed. Curl tells you where it's spinning.
- Game engines: wind simulation uses vector fields with curl for vortices and divergence for sources/sinks. Particle emitters use divergence to control outflow. Lighting on curved surfaces uses surface integrals. Flow fields guide AI pathfinding.

:::note[You've Got This]
Calculus 3 adds a third dimension and ties together a lot of new ideas. The payoff is the three big theorems: Green's, Stokes', and Divergence. They all say the same thing in different dimensions: what happens on the boundary tells you what's happening inside. Master the connections between these theorems, get comfortable switching coordinate systems, and you'll have a powerful toolkit for physics, engineering, graphics, and game development. You made it through Calc 3.
:::
