## What You'll Learn

A system of linear equations is a set of equations you want to solve simultaneously. In this lesson you'll learn how to solve them using substitution, elimination, and matrix form, and understand what their solutions look like geometrically.

## The Concept

A system of two equations in two unknowns:

$$
\begin{cases} 2x + 3y = 8 \\ 4x - y = 7 \end{cases}
$$

We want to find values of $x$ and $y$ that satisfy all equations at the same time.

### Three Main Methods

1. Substitution: Solve one equation for one variable and substitute into the other.
2. Elimination: Add or subtract equations to eliminate one variable.
3. Matrix Method: Write the system as $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ and solve using matrices (covered more in later lessons).

### Types of Solutions

Geometrically, each equation in 2D is a line. Two lines can:
1. Intersect at exactly one point (unique solution)
2. Be parallel and never meet (no solution, inconsistent)
3. Be the same line (infinitely many solutions, dependent)

### The Augmented Matrix

We often write the system as an augmented matrix, combining $A$ and $\mathbf{b}$:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 2 & 3 & 8 \\ 4 & -1 & 7 \end{array}\right]
$$

This compact form is what we'll use for row reduction in the next lesson.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Substitution Method**

$$
\begin{cases} x + 2y = 5 \\ 3x - y = 8 \end{cases}
$$

From the first equation: $x = 5 - 2y$

Plug into the second: $3(5 - 2y) - y = 8$

$15 - 6y - y = 8$

$15 - 7y = 8$

$y = 1$

Then $x = 5 - 2(1) = 3$. Solution: $(3, 1)$.

**Example 2: Elimination Method**

$$
\begin{cases} 2x + 3y = 12 \\ 4x - 3y = 6 \end{cases}
$$

Add the equations: $6x = 18$, so $x = 3$.

Plug back in: $2(3) + 3y = 12$, so $3y = 6$, $y = 2$.

Solution: $(3, 2)$.

**Example 3: No Solution (Inconsistent)**

$$
\begin{cases} x + y = 5 \\ x + y = 7 \end{cases}
$$

Both equations say $x + y$ equals something, but 5 and 7 are different. The lines are parallel. No solution exists.

## Real-World Application

Systems of equations show up everywhere:
- Game physics: solving for collision points between objects
- Pathfinding: balancing constraints across routes
- Circuit analysis: finding currents through components
- Economics: supply and demand equilibrium

Example: In a 3D game, you might solve a system to find where a projectile will hit a moving target, or to determine the exact position where two objects collide. The engine does this many times per frame.

:::note[You've Got This]
If you've solved two equations with two unknowns before, you already know the basics. The elimination method is often the most efficient by hand. Writing it in matrix form is just a cleaner way to organize the same information. The geometric picture (lines crossing, parallel, or overlapping) gives you great intuition for what's happening. Next up: a systematic method (Gaussian elimination) for solving any system, no matter how big.
:::
