## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn the cross product (a key 3D operation) and vector projection, which tells you how much of one vector lies in the direction of another.

## The Concept

### 1. Cross Product

The cross product $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ of two vectors in 3D produces a new vector that is perpendicular to both $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.

Formula (using the determinant):

$$
\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \langle u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2,\; u_3 v_1 - u_1 v_3,\; u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1 \rangle
$$

Key properties:
- The result is perpendicular to both input vectors (right-hand rule)
- Magnitude: $\lVert \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} \rVert = \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \sin \theta$ (equals the area of the parallelogram formed by $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$)
- Anti-commutative: $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = -(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{u})$

### 2. Vector Projection

The projection of vector $\mathbf{u}$ onto vector $\mathbf{v}$ tells you "how much of $\mathbf{u}$ points in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$."

$$
\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} = \left( \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2} \right) \mathbf{v}
$$

Think of it as the "shadow" of $\mathbf{u}$ cast onto $\mathbf{v}$. The dashed line drops straight down (perpendicular) from the tip of $\mathbf{u}$ to the line of $\mathbf{v}$:

In the diagram: $\mathbf{u} = \langle 3, 4 \rangle$ (blue) is projected onto the x-axis direction $\mathbf{v} = \langle 5, 0 \rangle$. The y-component is stripped away, leaving only the part of $\mathbf{u}$ that lies along $\mathbf{v}$.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Cross Product**

$\mathbf{u} = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle$, $\mathbf{v} = \langle 4, 5, 6 \rangle$

$$
\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \langle (2)(6) - (3)(5),\; (3)(4) - (1)(6),\; (1)(5) - (2)(4) \rangle = \langle -3, 6, -3 \rangle
$$

Step by step:
- First component: $2 \times 6 - 3 \times 5 = 12 - 15 = -3$
- Second component: $3 \times 4 - 1 \times 6 = 12 - 6 = 6$
- Third component: $1 \times 5 - 2 \times 4 = 5 - 8 = -3$

**Example 2: Magnitude of Cross Product**

Using the same vectors:

$\lVert \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 6^2 + (-3)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 36 + 9} = \sqrt{54} = 3\sqrt{6}$

This equals $\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \sin \theta$ and represents the area of the parallelogram formed by $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.

**Example 3: Vector Projection**

Project $\mathbf{u} = \langle 3, 4 \rangle$ onto $\mathbf{v} = \langle 5, 0 \rangle$ (along the x-axis).

Step 1: $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (3)(5) + (4)(0) = 15$

Step 2: $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 = 5^2 + 0^2 = 25$

Step 3: $\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{u} = \frac{15}{25} \langle 5, 0 \rangle = \frac{3}{5} \langle 5, 0 \rangle = \langle 3, 0 \rangle$

The y-component is removed. The projection lies entirely on the x-axis.

## Real-World Application

- Cross Product: Used to calculate surface normals (for lighting), torque in physics, and angular momentum
- Projection is used in games for:
  - Finding how much force applies in a certain direction
  - Calculating the closest point on a line or plane
  - Implementing "sliding" collision response
  - AI sight cones and threat detection

Example: When a character runs into a wall at an angle, projection helps calculate the sliding direction along the wall instead of stopping completely.

:::note[You've Got This]
The cross product gives you a perpendicular vector, which is very powerful in 3D. The right-hand rule takes a bit of practice, but it's worth it. Vector projection is one of the most practical tools in linear algebra. It lets you "strip away" the part of a vector that's not useful and keep only the useful component. These operations are used constantly in game engines. Keep practicing the calculations and visualizing them.
:::
