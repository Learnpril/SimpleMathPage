## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll meet the preimage definition of continuity, check that it agrees with epsilon-delta, learn what a homeomorphism is and why the inverse must also be continuous, and see how to prove two spaces are not homeomorphic.

## The Concept

### Continuity, restated

> $f:X\to Y$ is **continuous** if $f^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$ for every open $V\subseteq Y$.

That is the whole definition. No epsilons, no distances, no sequences — and it works in any topological space.

<PreimageContinuity />

The figure pulls an open band back through two functions. For $x^2$, the band $(1,4)$ pulls back to $(-2,-1)\cup(1,2)$, an open set. For the step function, the band $(0.5,1.5)$ pulls back to a set containing its own left boundary point, which is not open. The figure decides openness by checking whether the function value at a run boundary lands strictly inside the band, so the verdict is computed rather than asserted.

Notice the direction. Continuity is about **preimages**, not images. A continuous function can easily map an open set to a non-open one: $f(x)=x^2$ sends the open set $(-1,1)$ to $[0,1)$, which is not open. Maps that do send open sets to open sets are called **open maps**, and that is a separate property.

### Why this matches epsilon-delta

For metric spaces the two definitions agree, and seeing why explains where the epsilons came from.

Suppose $f^{-1}(V)$ is always open, and fix $x_0$ and $\varepsilon>0$. The ball $B(f(x_0),\varepsilon)$ is open, so $f^{-1}(B(f(x_0),\varepsilon))$ is open and contains $x_0$. Being open, it contains a ball $B(x_0,\delta)$. That $\delta$ is exactly the one epsilon-delta asks for.

Running the argument backwards gives the converse. **The epsilon-delta definition is the preimage definition with the balls written out.**

### Equivalent formulations

All of these are the same condition, and different ones are convenient in different proofs:

- $f^{-1}(V)$ is open for every open $V$
- $f^{-1}(C)$ is closed for every closed $C$
- $f^{-1}(B)$ is open for every $B$ in a **basis** of $Y$ — usually the cheapest to check
- $f(\bar A)\subseteq\overline{f(A)}$ for every $A\subseteq X$
- For every $x$ and every neighbourhood $V$ of $f(x)$, there is a neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ with $f(U)\subseteq V$

The basis version is worth remembering: to show a map into $\mathbb{R}$ is continuous you need only check preimages of open intervals.

### Standard facts

- **Compositions** of continuous maps are continuous, immediately from $(g\circ f)^{-1}(V) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(V))$.
- **Constant maps** are continuous: the preimage is $\varnothing$ or $X$.
- **The identity** is continuous.
- **Restrictions** of continuous maps to subspaces are continuous.
- **Any map from a discrete space** is continuous, since every preimage is open.
- **Any map into a trivial space** is continuous, since the only open sets to pull back are $\varnothing$ and $Y$.

Those last two are worth pausing on. They say that continuity depends on the topologies as much as on the function — the same function can be continuous or not depending on which topologies you place on the two sets.

### Homeomorphism

> $f:X\to Y$ is a **homeomorphism** if $f$ is a bijection, $f$ is continuous, and $f^{-1}$ is continuous.

Equivalently, $f$ is a continuous bijection that is also an **open map**. Spaces related by a homeomorphism are **homeomorphic**, written $X\cong Y$, and they are indistinguishable by any topological property.

**The inverse condition is not redundant.** Consider

$$
f:[0,2\pi)\to S^1, \qquad f(t) = (\cos t,\sin t)
$$

This is a continuous bijection. Its inverse is not continuous: points on either side of $(1,0)$ on the circle are close to each other, but their preimages, near 0 and near $2\pi$, are far apart. The map glues the two ends of the interval together, and the inverse has to tear them.

So $[0,2\pi)$ and $S^1$ are **not** homeomorphic, which we can confirm independently: removing one point disconnects the circle never, and disconnects the interval sometimes.

There is one important case where the condition comes free:

> A continuous bijection from a **compact** space to a **Hausdorff** space is automatically a homeomorphism.

That saves a great deal of work later, and it is why so many theorems specify compact and Hausdorff.

### Proving two spaces are not homeomorphic

You cannot do it by failing to find a map. You exhibit a topological property one has and the other lacks:

| Invariant                            | Distinguishes                 |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Cardinality                          | trivially different sizes     |
| Number of components                 | $(0,1)$ from $(0,1)\cup(2,3)$ |
| Compactness                          | $[0,1]$ from $(0,1)$          |
| Connectedness after removing a point | $[0,1]$ from $S^1$            |
| Number of non-cut points             | $[0,1]$ from $[0,1)$          |
| Fundamental group                    | $S^2$ from the torus          |

The cut-point technique deserves highlighting because it is so cheap: if $X\cong Y$ then removing corresponding points must leave homeomorphic spaces, so any difference in what removal does is a proof.

### Embeddings

> An **embedding** is a map that is a homeomorphism onto its image.

This is the right notion when you care how a space sits inside another. A knotted circle and an unknotted circle in $\mathbb{R}^3$ are homeomorphic as spaces but are not equivalent embeddings, and knot theory studies exactly that difference.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Show $f(x)=x^2$ is continuous using preimages.**

**Solution.** By the basis criterion it suffices to check preimages of open intervals $(c,d)$.

If $d\le0$ the preimage is empty. If $c<0<d$ it is $(-\sqrt d,\sqrt d)$. If $0\le c<d$ it is

$$
(-\sqrt d,-\sqrt c)\cup(\sqrt c,\sqrt d)
$$

Every case is open, so $f$ is continuous ✓

For $(1,4)$ specifically this gives $(-2,-1)\cup(1,2)$, matching the figure. ∎

**Example 2: Show a continuous map need not send open sets to open sets.**

**Solution.** With $f(x)=x^2$, the image of the open set $(-1,1)$ is $[0,1)$, which is not open, since 0 has no interval around it inside the image.

**Continuity constrains preimages only.** ∎

**Example 3: Show the step function is not continuous by a preimage.**

**Solution.** Let $g(x)=0$ for $x<0$ and $g(x)=1$ for $x\ge0$. Then

$$
g^{-1}\big((0.5,1.5)\big) = [0,\infty)
$$

which is not open, since no interval around 0 stays inside it.

$$
\text{not continuous}
$$

∎

**Example 4: Show any map from a discrete space is continuous.**

**Solution.** Let $X$ carry the discrete topology and let $f:X\to Y$ be arbitrary. For any open $V\subseteq Y$, the set $f^{-1}(V)$ is a subset of $X$, and every subset of a discrete space is open.

$$
\text{continuous}
$$

∎

**Continuity is a statement about the topologies**, not only the formula. The same function becomes discontinuous if $X$ is given a coarser topology.

**Example 5: Exhibit a continuous bijection that is not a homeomorphism.**

**Solution.** $f:[0,2\pi)\to S^1$ with $f(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)$ is a continuous bijection.

The inverse fails continuity at $(1,0)$. Take the open set $[0,1)\subseteq[0,2\pi)$, which is open in the subspace. Its image is an arc containing the point $(1,0)$ but no full neighbourhood of it in $S^1$, so the image is not open — meaning $f$ is not an open map and $f^{-1}$ is not continuous.

$$
\text{not a homeomorphism}
$$

∎

**Example 6: Show $(0,1)\cong\mathbb{R}$.**

**Solution.** The map

$$
f(x) = \tan\left(\pi x - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)
$$

is a continuous bijection from $(0,1)$ to $\mathbb{R}$, and its inverse $f^{-1}(y) = \frac{1}{\pi}\arctan y+\frac12$ is continuous too.

$$
(0,1)\cong\mathbb{R}
$$

∎

**Boundedness is not topological.** A bounded space and an unbounded one can be homeomorphic, which is exactly why compactness rather than boundedness is the topological notion.

**Example 7: Show $[0,1]\not\cong[0,1)$ by cut points.**

**Solution.** Call $p$ a non-cut point if $X\setminus\{p\}$ is still connected. In $[0,1]$ the non-cut points are 0 and 1, so there are **two**. In $[0,1)$ only 0 qualifies, so there is **one**.

A homeomorphism would carry non-cut points to non-cut points, so the counts would have to agree.

$$
[0,1]\not\cong[0,1)
$$

∎

## Real-World Applications

**Coordinate charts.** A manifold is defined by homeomorphisms from patches of it onto open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Map projections are exactly such charts for the Earth, and the fact that no single chart covers the sphere is a topological statement.

**Data embedding.** Dimensionality reduction methods aim to embed data homeomorphically into a low-dimensional space. When the method distorts the topology — merging separate clusters, or tearing a loop — the failure is a failure of homeomorphism.

**Mesh parameterization.** Flattening a 3D surface patch for texturing requires a homeomorphism onto a planar region, and it is impossible without cutting when the patch has the wrong topology. Graphics tools detect this and introduce seams.

**Robot workspace equivalence.** Two mechanisms with homeomorphic configuration spaces admit the same motion-planning strategies, even if the machines look nothing alike. Recognizing the equivalence saves reimplementation.

**Circuit layout.** Whether a circuit can be drawn on a plane without crossings is a topological question about the graph, and it determines whether a single-layer board suffices.

**Protein structure.** Comparing folds uses topological equivalence rather than exact geometry, because the same functional shape appears with quite different coordinates.

:::note[You've Got This]
The preimage definition is shorter than epsilon-delta and stronger, in the sense that it works everywhere, so it is worth making it your default. Two things to keep straight: continuity restricts preimages and says nothing about images, and a homeomorphism needs the inverse to be continuous too — the interval wrapping onto the circle is the example that shows why. When you want to prove two spaces differ, reach for an invariant, and the cut-point trick is usually the cheapest one available.
:::
