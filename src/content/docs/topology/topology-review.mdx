## Metric and Topological Spaces

A **metric** satisfies non-negativity, identity of indiscernibles, symmetry, and the triangle inequality. Open balls give open sets, and **different metrics can produce the same topology** - $d_1$, $d_2$ and $d_\infty$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ all do, which is the motivation for dropping the metric entirely.

A **topology** is a family of subsets containing $\varnothing$ and $X$, closed under **arbitrary unions** and **finite intersections**. The asymmetry is forced: $\bigcap(-\frac1n,\frac1n) = \{0\}$ is not open.

Closed sets dualize: arbitrary intersections and finite unions. The dual counterexample is $\bigcup[\frac1n,1] = (0,1]$.

**Open and closed are not opposites.** All four combinations occur in $\mathbb{R}$: $(0,1)$, $[0,1]$, $\varnothing$, $[0,1)$.

### The standard topologies

| Topology                 | Open sets                                          |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Discrete                 | all subsets; finest                                |
| Trivial                  | only $\varnothing$ and $X$; coarsest               |
| Standard on $\mathbb{R}$ | unions of open intervals                           |
| Cofinite                 | $\varnothing$ plus sets with finite complement     |
| Lower limit              | generated by $[a,b)$; strictly finer than standard |

Counts of distinct topologies: 1, 4, 29, 355, 6942 on 1 to 5 points. No formula is known.

### Bases and subspaces

A **basis** is a family whose unions give every open set. To generate a topology it must cover $X$ and satisfy: for $x\in B_1\cap B_2$ there is $B_3$ with $x\in B_3\subseteq B_1\cap B_2$.

The **subspace topology** on $A$ is $\{U\cap A\}$. So **openness is always relative**: $[0,\frac12)$ is open in $[0,1]$ and not in $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ is discrete.

Hereditary: Hausdorff, metrizable, second countable. **Not** hereditary: compactness, connectedness. Closed subspaces are the well-behaved case.

## Continuity

$$
f \text{ continuous} \iff f^{-1}(V) \text{ open for every open } V
$$

Equivalent forms: preimages of closed sets are closed; preimages of basis elements are open (cheapest); $f(\bar A)\subseteq\overline{f(A)}$.

**Continuity constrains preimages only.** $x^2$ sends the open $(-1,1)$ to $[0,1)$.

A **homeomorphism** is a continuous bijection with continuous inverse. The inverse condition is not redundant: $[0,2\pi)\to S^1$ is a continuous bijection that is not a homeomorphism. It comes free when the domain is **compact** and the target **Hausdorff**.

To prove $X\not\cong Y$, exhibit a differing invariant. **Matching invariants prove nothing.**

## Interior, Closure, Boundary

Every point is interior, boundary, or exterior relative to $A$.

Always: $\overline{A\cup B}=\bar A\cup\bar B$ and $\operatorname{int}(A\cap B)=\operatorname{int}A\cap\operatorname{int}B$.

Containment only: $\overline{A\cap B}\subseteq\bar A\cap\bar B$ and $\operatorname{int}(A\cup B)\supseteq\operatorname{int}A\cup\operatorname{int}B$.

**Dense** means $\bar A=X$; **nowhere dense** means $\operatorname{int}\bar A=\varnothing$. $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$ with $\partial\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{R}$; $\mathbb{Z}$ and the Cantor set are nowhere dense.

## Connectedness

**Connected** means no separation into two nonempty disjoint open sets. Equivalently only $\varnothing$ and $X$ are clopen, or every continuous map to a discrete space is constant.

> The connected subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ are exactly the **intervals**.

Continuous images of connected sets are connected, which gives the **intermediate value theorem** in one line. $\mathbb{Q}$ is totally disconnected.

**Path-connected** implies connected, not conversely. The **topologist's sine curve** has 1 component and 2 path components. The two notions agree when the space is **locally path-connected**, which covers open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and all manifolds.

$$
\text{convex}\implies\text{star-shaped}\implies\text{path-connected}\implies\text{connected}
$$

all strict.

## Compactness

**Compact** means every open cover has a finite subcover. It is the topological stand-in for finiteness.

> **Heine–Borel.** In $\mathbb{R}^n$ only: compact $\iff$ closed and bounded.

In a general metric space the correct statement is **complete + totally bounded**, and $\{e_n\}\subseteq\ell^2$ is bounded without being totally bounded.

Consequences: continuous images are compact; the **extreme value theorem**; closed subspaces of compact spaces are compact; compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces are closed; continuous functions on compact metric spaces are **uniformly continuous**; products of compact spaces are compact (**Tychonoff**).

Compactness is **not hereditary**: $(0,1)$ inside $[0,1]$.

In metric spaces, compact = sequentially compact = complete and totally bounded = limit point compact. None of these equivalences holds in general.

## Constructions

**Product topology.** Basis of boxes $U\times V$; the coarsest making the projections continuous. A map into a product is continuous iff its components are.

**Quotient topology.** $V$ open iff $q^{-1}(V)$ is open; the finest making $q$ continuous. A map out is continuous iff its composite with $q$ is. Quotients can destroy Hausdorff.

Gluing a square:

| Gluing             | Result          | Orientable | Boundary circles |
| ------------------ | --------------- | ---------- | ---------------- |
| one pair, straight | cylinder        | yes        | 2                |
| one pair, flipped  | Möbius strip    | no         | 1                |
| both straight      | torus           | yes        | 0                |
| one flipped        | Klein bottle    | no         | 0                |
| both flipped       | $\mathbb{RP}^2$ | no         | 0                |

## Separation Axioms

$$
T_4\implies T_3\implies T_2\implies T_1\implies T_0
$$

$T_0$: distinguishable. $T_1$: singletons closed. $T_2$ **Hausdorff**: disjoint neighbourhoods, hence unique limits. $T_3$: point from closed set. $T_4$ **normal**: two closed sets, which is what **Urysohn's lemma** needs.

Every metric space is $T_4$, so **failing any axiom rules out metrizability**. The cofinite topology on an infinite set is $T_1$ and not $T_2$. **Urysohn metrization:** regular Hausdorff plus a countable basis implies metrizable. Compact + Hausdorff implies normal.

## Surfaces

A **closed surface** is a compact 2-manifold without boundary.

> **Classification.** Every closed connected surface is the sphere, a connected sum of $g$ tori, or a connected sum of $k$ projective planes - determined by **orientability and genus**.

$$
\chi = 2-2g \ \text{(orientable)}, \qquad \chi = 2-k \ \text{(non-orientable)}
$$

Connected sum: $\chi(S_1\# S_2)=\chi(S_1)+\chi(S_2)-2$, the sphere is the identity, $\mathbb{RP}^2\#\mathbb{RP}^2$ is the Klein bottle, and once non-orientable, adding a torus equals adding two crosscaps.

**$\chi$ alone is not enough:** the torus and Klein bottle both have $\chi=0$.

Edge words: sphere $aa^{-1}$, torus $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$, $\mathbb{RP}^2$ $aa$, Klein bottle $abab^{-1}$.

## The Euler Characteristic

$\chi=V-E+F$, invariant because refining adds a vertex and an edge, or an edge and a face. It is a **homotopy** invariant, so a disc and a point share $\chi=1$.

Applications: exactly **five Platonic solids** from $\frac1p+\frac1q>\frac12$; planar graphs satisfy $E\le3V-6$, ruling out $K_5$; **Poincaré–Hopf** gives the **hairy ball theorem** since $\chi(S^2)=2\ne0$.

## Homotopy and the Fundamental Group

A **homotopy** is a continuous $H:X\times[0,1]\to Y$ joining two maps. **Homotopy equivalence** needs only the composites to be homotopic to the identities, making it coarser than homeomorphism: it forgets dimension, compactness and orientability.

**Deformation retracts** are the practical tool: $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$, the annulus, the solid torus and the Möbius strip all retract to a circle.

$\pi_1(X,x_0)$ is the group of path-homotopy classes of loops under concatenation, with associativity holding only up to homotopy.

| Space                                                      | $\pi_1$                           |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| $\mathbb{R}^n$, discs, cones                               | trivial                           |
| $S^1$, annulus, punctured plane, solid torus, Möbius strip | $\mathbb{Z}$                      |
| $S^n$, $n\ge2$                                             | trivial                           |
| Torus                                                      | $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$      |
| Figure eight                                               | free on 2 generators, non-abelian |
| $\mathbb{RP}^2$                                            | $\mathbb{Z}/2$                    |

**Simply connected** means path-connected with trivial $\pi_1$ - the hypothesis in Cauchy's theorem. $S^2$ is simply connected and **not** contractible.

$\pi_1$ is functorial, multiplicative on products, and computable by van Kampen. It separates the torus from the Klein bottle where $\chi$ cannot, and it proves no retraction $D^2\to S^1$ exists.

## Fixed Point Theorems

> **Brouwer.** Every continuous self-map of a closed ball has a fixed point.

$n=1$ is the intermediate value theorem applied to $f(x)-x$. $n=2$ follows from the non-existence of a retraction $D^2\to S^1$, hence from $\pi_1(S^1)=\mathbb{Z}$. Fails on the annulus (a hole), on the open disc (not compact), and for discontinuous maps.

> **Banach.** A contraction on a complete metric space has a **unique** fixed point, reachable by iteration.

Brouwer is topological, existence only, non-constructive. Banach is metric, unique, and gives an algorithm.

Consequences: **hairy ball**, **Borsuk–Ulam**, **ham sandwich**, **Nash equilibria**, **Perron–Frobenius**.

## The Standard Counterexamples

| Object                            | Shows                                                     |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| $\bigcap(-\frac1n,\frac1n)=\{0\}$ | intersections must be finite                              |
| $\bigcup[\frac1n,1]=(0,1]$        | unions of closed sets need not be closed                  |
| $[0,1)$                           | neither open nor closed                                   |
| $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$      | $\partial A$ can be the whole space; totally disconnected |
| Cofinite on an infinite set       | $T_1$ without $T_2$; not metrizable                       |
| Sierpiński space                  | $T_0$ without $T_1$                                       |
| Trivial topology                  | limits not unique; compact sets not closed                |
| $[0,2\pi)\to S^1$                 | continuous bijection, not a homeomorphism                 |
| $(0,1)\cong\mathbb{R}$            | boundedness is not topological                            |
| $(0,1)$ in $[0,1]$                | compactness is not hereditary                             |
| $\{e_n\}\subseteq\ell^2$          | bounded but not totally bounded                           |
| Topologist's sine curve           | connected, not path-connected                             |
| Cantor set                        | compact, uncountable, totally disconnected, nowhere dense |
| Möbius strip vs cylinder          | one reversed gluing changes orientability                 |
| Torus vs Klein bottle             | same $\chi$, different spaces                             |
| $S^2$                             | simply connected, not contractible                        |
| Annulus rotation                  | Brouwer needs the right shape                             |

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking open and closed are opposites.** $[0,1)$ is neither; $\varnothing$ and $X$ are both.
- **Forgetting "open in what?"** $[0,\frac12)$ is open in $[0,1]$ and not in $\mathbb{R}$.
- **Requiring arbitrary intersections** to be open, or arbitrary unions of closed sets to be closed.
- **Defining continuity with images** instead of preimages.
- **Omitting continuity of the inverse** in a homeomorphism.
- **Treating closed and bounded as compact** outside $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- **Assuming connected implies path-connected**, without local path-connectedness.
- **Assuming compactness or connectedness is hereditary.**
- **Concluding two spaces are homeomorphic because invariants agree.**
- **Using $\chi$ alone to classify a surface**, ignoring orientability.
- **Confusing simply connected with contractible.** $S^2$ is the first and not the second.
- **Expecting Brouwer to give uniqueness**, or Banach to work without a contraction.
- **Confusing the solid torus with the torus surface**; their $\pi_1$ are $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$.

:::note[You've Got This]
Look at how little this section assumed: a family of subsets closed under unions and finite intersections. Out of that came continuity without epsilons, the intermediate value theorem in one line, the extreme value theorem in another, a complete classification of surfaces, and a group that can tell a torus from a Klein bottle. The habit worth keeping is the one from the first lesson - to show two things differ, find an invariant, and to show they agree, build the map. And if the axioms felt thin at the start and useful by the end, that is exactly the arc the subject is designed to have.
:::
