## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll learn to define a topology by a basis instead of listing every open set, check the two conditions a basis must satisfy, and work with the subspace topology, where the ambient space is always part of the question.

## The Concept

### Why a basis

Listing every open set of $\mathbb{R}$ is hopeless: there are uncountably many. But every one of them is a union of open intervals, so the intervals determine the whole topology. That is the idea.

> A **basis** for a topology $\tau$ on $X$ is a collection $\mathcal{B}\subseteq\tau$ such that every open set is a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$.

Equivalently, and more usefully in practice: for every open $U$ and every $x\in U$, there is a basis element $B$ with $x\in B\subseteq U$.

<BasisSubspace />

The left panel builds a two-piece open set as a union of overlapping intervals, with the covering computed rather than drawn. Every open subset of $\mathbb{R}$ can be produced this way, which is what makes the intervals a basis.

### Going the other way

Usually you have a candidate family and want a topology from it.

> A collection $\mathcal{B}$ of subsets of $X$ is a basis for **some** topology if
>
> 1. $\mathcal{B}$ covers $X$: every point lies in some basis element.
> 2. For $B_1,B_2\in\mathcal{B}$ and $x\in B_1\cap B_2$, there is $B_3\in\mathcal{B}$ with $x\in B_3\subseteq B_1\cap B_2$.

Then the topology generated is all unions of basis elements. Condition 2 is weaker than requiring $B_1\cap B_2$ itself to be in $\mathcal{B}$, and that flexibility is what makes bases convenient: open intervals satisfy it because the intersection of two intervals is an interval, while open discs in the plane satisfy it even though the intersection of two discs is not a disc.

### The standard bases

| Space                              | Basis                             |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| $\mathbb{R}$, standard             | open intervals $(a,b)$            |
| $\mathbb{R}$, standard (countable) | intervals with rational endpoints |
| $\mathbb{R}^n$                     | open balls, or open boxes         |
| Metric space                       | open balls $B(x,r)$               |
| Discrete                           | singletons $\{x\}$                |
| $\mathbb{R}$, lower limit          | half-open intervals $[a,b)$       |
| Product $X\times Y$                | products $U\times V$ of open sets |

The rational-endpoint basis is worth noticing: it is **countable**, which makes $\mathbb{R}$ second countable and is what lets many analysis arguments enumerate cases.

A related notion: a **local basis** at $x$ is a family of neighbourhoods of $x$ such that every neighbourhood of $x$ contains one of them. In a metric space the balls $B(x,1/n)$ form a countable local basis, and that is precisely why sequences work so well in metric spaces.

### Comparing topologies via bases

A convenient test: $\tau_1\subseteq\tau_2$ if and only if for every $x$ and every basis element $B_1\in\mathcal{B}_1$ containing $x$, there is $B_2\in\mathcal{B}_2$ with $x\in B_2\subseteq B_1$.

This is how the lower limit topology is shown to be strictly finer than the standard one: inside any $(a,b)$ containing $x$ you can fit $[x, b)$, but inside $[0,1)$ there is no open interval containing 0.

### The subspace topology

> If $A\subseteq X$, the **subspace topology** on $A$ is
>
> $$
> \tau_A = \{\,U\cap A \ :\ U\in\tau_X\,\}
> $$

So the open sets of $A$ are exactly the traces of the open sets of $X$. This is the only sensible choice, and it is why a subset of a space is automatically a space.

The right panel of the figure shows the consequence that surprises people: intersecting the ambient open interval with $[0,1]$ produces a half-open set, which is **open in $[0,1]$** and not open in $\mathbb{R}$.

**"Open" is never a property of a set by itself.** It always means open in a particular space, and forgetting to say which is the most common source of confusion in this material.

### What behaves well and what does not

Some properties pass to subspaces automatically, and some do not.

| Property          | Inherited by every subspace?  |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Hausdorff         | yes                           |
| Metrizable        | yes                           |
| Second countable  | yes                           |
| Compact           | no — only by closed subspaces |
| Connected         | no                            |
| Complete (metric) | no — only by closed subspaces |

A property inherited by all subspaces is called **hereditary**. Compactness and connectedness are not hereditary, which is why $[0,1]$ is compact and connected while its subspace $\{0\}\cup(\frac12,1)$ is neither.

Closed subspaces are the well-behaved case, and that pattern recurs: a closed subspace of a compact space is compact, and a closed subspace of a complete metric space is complete.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Verify that open intervals form a basis for $\mathbb{R}$.**

**Solution.** Condition 1: every real number lies in some interval, for instance $(x-1,x+1)$ ✓

Condition 2: the intersection of two open intervals is either empty or an open interval, so it is itself a basis element and serves as $B_3$ ✓

$$
\text{a basis}
$$

∎

**Example 2: Verify that open discs form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$.**

**Solution.** Condition 1 is clear. For condition 2, let $x\in D_1\cap D_2$. Each disc contains a small disc about $x$, say of radii $r_1$ and $r_2$, so the disc about $x$ of radius $\min(r_1,r_2)$ lies inside both.

$$
\text{a basis}
$$

∎

**Note the intersection of two discs is not a disc**, which is exactly why condition 2 is stated with a containment rather than membership.

**Example 3: Show the intervals with rational endpoints form a basis for $\mathbb{R}$.**

**Solution.** Given open $U$ and $x\in U$, there is $(a,b)\subseteq U$ containing $x$. By density of $\mathbb{Q}$, choose rationals $p\in(a,x)$ and $q\in(x,b)$. Then

$$
x\in(p,q)\subseteq(a,b)\subseteq U
$$

$$
\text{a basis, and a countable one}
$$

∎

**A countable basis for an uncountable space.** This is what second countability means, and it is used constantly in analysis.

**Example 4: Find the subspace topology on $\{0\}\cup(1,2)$ inside $\mathbb{R}$.**

**Solution.** Open sets are intersections with ambient open sets. Taking $V=(-\frac12,\frac12)$ gives $\{0\}$, so the singleton $\{0\}$ is **open** in this subspace.

The subspace is therefore disconnected, splitting into the clopen pieces $\{0\}$ and $(1,2)$. ∎

**A singleton can be open in a subspace** without being open in $\mathbb{R}$, which is worth internalizing.

**Example 5: Is $[0,1)$ open in $[0,2]$?**

**Solution.** Yes: $[0,1) = (-1,1)\cap[0,2]$, an ambient open set intersected with the subspace.

Is it closed in $[0,2]$? Its complement in $[0,2]$ is $[1,2]$, which equals $[1,3]\cap[0,2]$ and is the intersection with a closed set, hence closed. So $[0,1)$ is open and not closed in $[0,2]$.

$$
\text{open in } [0,2]
$$

∎

**Example 6: Show the subspace topology on $\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ is discrete.**

**Solution.** For any integer $n$,

$$
\left(n-\tfrac12,\ n+\tfrac12\right)\cap\mathbb{Z} = \{n\}
$$

so every singleton is open in the subspace, and therefore every subset is.

$$
\text{discrete}
$$

∎

**A discrete subspace of a non-discrete space.** The subspace topology can look nothing like the ambient one.

**Example 7: Show compactness is not hereditary.**

**Solution.** $[0,1]$ is compact, as we prove in lesson 10. Its subspace $(0,1)$ is not: the cover by $\left(\frac1n,1\right)$ for $n\ge2$ has no finite subcover, since any finite collection has a smallest $\frac1n$ and misses everything below it.

$$
\text{compact space, non-compact subspace}
$$

∎

**Closed subspaces are the exception**: a closed subspace of a compact space is always compact, and $(0,1)$ is not closed in $[0,1]$.

## Real-World Applications

**Constraint surfaces in robotics.** A robot arm's reachable set is a subspace of its full configuration space, and it inherits its topology. Path planning happens in that subspace, where openness and connectedness may differ from the ambient space.

**Manifolds and data.** The manifold hypothesis in machine learning says high-dimensional data lies near a low-dimensional subspace, and the subspace topology is what makes local charts and dimensionality reduction meaningful.

**Level sets in simulation.** A constraint surface such as an isosurface of temperature is studied with its subspace topology, and whether it is connected determines whether a physical region is isolated.

**Sampling and second countability.** A countable basis is what justifies approximating a continuous space by a countable grid, which every numerical method relies on. Without it, discretization has no guarantee of capturing the topology.

**Digital signal spaces.** The set of realizable filter coefficients is a subspace of a parameter space, and the design problem is constrained to it. Boundary behaviour differs between the two spaces, which is where marginal stability lives.

**Geographic information systems.** A region's boundary is treated differently depending on whether you work in the plane or in the region as its own space, and inconsistent choices produce the classic sliver-polygon errors.

:::note[You've Got This]
Two practical takeaways. A basis lets you specify a topology with a small, understandable family instead of an unmanageable list, and the two conditions to check are short. And the subspace topology is where the phrase "open in what?" earns its keep: $[0,\frac12)$ is open in $[0,1]$, $\{0\}$ can be open in a subspace, and $\mathbb{Z}$ sitting in $\mathbb{R}$ is discrete. If you always name the ambient space, most of the confusion in this material disappears.
:::
