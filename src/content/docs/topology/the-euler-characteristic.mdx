## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll compute $V-E+F$, see why it is independent of the subdivision, use it to prove there are exactly five Platonic solids, and meet its consequences from graph planarity to the hairy ball theorem.

## The Concept

### The definition

> For a surface cut into vertices, edges and faces,
>
> $$
> \chi = V - E + F
> $$

The remarkable fact is that the answer depends only on the **surface**, not on how you cut it up.

<EulerCharacteristic />

The figure computes $\chi$ for four polyhedra and two triangulations of the torus. The four polyhedra have wildly different counts - the tetrahedron has 4 vertices, the icosahedron has 12 - and every one gives 2. The two torus triangulations likewise give 0 with different counts.

**That invariance is the entire content.** The individual numbers are accidents of the subdivision; the alternating sum is a property of the space.

### Why it is invariant

Any two subdivisions of a surface have a common refinement, so it suffices to check that refining does not change $\chi$. There are two elementary moves:

- **Split an edge** by adding a vertex in the middle. Then $V$ increases by 1 and $E$ increases by 1, so $V-E+F$ is unchanged.
- **Split a face** by adding an edge across it. Then $E$ increases by 1 and $F$ increases by 1, so again nothing changes.

Every refinement is a sequence of these, so $\chi$ survives all of them. ∎

More generally $\chi$ is a **homotopy invariant**, which is much stronger than being a homeomorphism invariant: spaces that can be continuously deformed into each other share it.

### The values

| Space                        | $\chi$     |
| ---------------------------- | ---------- |
| Point                        | 1          |
| Interval, disc               | 1          |
| Circle                       | 0          |
| Sphere $S^2$                 | 2          |
| Torus                        | 0          |
| Genus $g$ orientable surface | $2-2g$     |
| Klein bottle                 | 0          |
| $\mathbb{RP}^2$              | 1          |
| $S^n$                        | $1+(-1)^n$ |

Note that the disc and the point share $\chi=1$: they are homotopy equivalent, since a disc contracts to its centre. So $\chi$ cannot tell them apart, and that is a feature of it being a homotopy invariant rather than a defect.

### The five Platonic solids

Here is the classical application, and it is a genuine impossibility proof.

Suppose a convex polyhedron has all faces regular $p$-gons with $q$ meeting at each vertex. Counting each edge twice, once from each of its two faces, and each edge twice again from its two endpoints:

$$
pF = 2E, \qquad qV = 2E
$$

Substituting into $V-E+F=2$:

$$
\frac{2E}{q}-E+\frac{2E}{p} = 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac1p+\frac1q = \frac12+\frac1E
$$

Since $E>0$ we need $\frac1p+\frac1q>\frac12$, and both $p,q\ge3$. Enumerating:

| $(p,q)$ | Solid        |
| ------- | ------------ |
| $(3,3)$ | tetrahedron  |
| $(4,3)$ | cube         |
| $(3,4)$ | octahedron   |
| $(5,3)$ | dodecahedron |
| $(3,5)$ | icosahedron  |

Any other pair fails the inequality: $(4,4)$ gives exactly $\frac12$, and anything larger gives less. **Exactly five, and Euler's formula is why.** Nothing about angles or lengths was used.

### Graph planarity

For a connected planar graph drawn without crossings, $V-E+F=2$ where the outer region counts as a face. Two corollaries:

- A simple planar graph satisfies $E\le3V-6$.
- $K_5$, the complete graph on 5 vertices, has $V=5$ and $E=10>3(5)-6=9$, so it is **not planar**.
- $K_{3,3}$ is not planar either, by a similar count using that its shortest cycle has length 4.

Those two graphs are exactly the obstructions, by **Kuratowski's theorem**: a graph is planar iff it contains neither as a subdivision.

This is where the Königsberg bridges lead: circuit board layout, and whether a network can be drawn on a single layer, is decided by these counts.

### The hairy ball theorem

> **Poincaré–Hopf theorem.** For a smooth vector field on a closed surface with isolated zeros, the sum of the indices of the zeros equals $\chi$.

Since $\chi(S^2)=2\ne0$, a vector field on the sphere **must** have a zero:

> **Hairy ball theorem.** There is no nowhere-zero continuous tangent vector field on $S^2$.

You cannot comb a sphere flat. On the torus, where $\chi=0$, you can - comb it around the hole. The reason one is possible and the other is not is a single integer.

The meteorological consequence is real: at any moment there is a point on Earth with zero horizontal wind, which is why there is always a cyclone or an eye somewhere.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Verify $\chi=2$ for the cube and the icosahedron.**

**Solution.** Cube: $V=8$, $E=12$, $F=6$, so $8-12+6=2$ ✓

Icosahedron: $V=12$, $E=30$, $F=20$, so $12-30+20=2$ ✓

**Completely different counts, same answer**, because both are subdivisions of a sphere. ∎

**Example 2: Compute $\chi$ for a triangulated torus and confirm it is 0.**

**Solution.** Take a $3\times3$ grid on the square with opposite edges identified. The 9 grid squares each split into 2 triangles, giving $F=18$.

After identification, the grid has $V=9$ vertices. Each vertex has degree 6 in the triangulated grid, and counting each edge from both ends gives $E = \frac{9\cdot6}{2}=27$.

$$
\chi = 9-27+18 = 0
$$

matching the figure's first torus row ✓ ∎

**Example 3: Find the genus of a surface with $\chi=-6$.**

**Solution.** For an orientable closed surface, $\chi=2-2g$:

$$
-6 = 2-2g \implies g=4
$$

A four-holed torus. ∎

**Example 4: Prove there are at most five Platonic solids.**

**Solution.** As derived above, regularity forces

$$
\frac1p+\frac1q > \frac12, \qquad p,q\ge3
$$

If $p=3$ then $\frac1q>\frac16$, so $q\in\{3,4,5\}$. If $p=4$ then $\frac1q>\frac14$, so $q=3$. If $p=5$ then $\frac1q>\frac{3}{10}$, so $q=3$. If $p\ge6$ then $\frac1p\le\frac16$ and $\frac1q\le\frac13$, giving a sum of at most $\frac12$ - not enough.

**Answer:** five possibilities, and all five are realized. ∎

**Example 5: Show $K_5$ is not planar.**

**Solution.** $K_5$ has $V=5$ and $E=\binom52=10$.

A simple planar graph must satisfy $E\le3V-6$, which here gives $E\le9$.

$$
10>9, \text{ so } K_5 \text{ is not planar}
$$

∎

**A counting argument settles a drawing question**, with no attempt to try layouts.

**Example 6: Explain the hairy ball theorem via $\chi$.**

**Solution.** By Poincaré–Hopf, the indices of the zeros of any smooth tangent vector field on a closed surface sum to $\chi$.

On $S^2$ that sum must be 2. A nowhere-zero field has no zeros, so its index sum is 0.

$$
0\ne2, \text{ so no such field exists}
$$

∎

On the torus $\chi=0$, so a nowhere-zero field is not obstructed, and indeed the field pointing around the hole works.

**Example 7: Compute $\chi$ of a disc with three holes.**

**Solution.** Start with a disc, $\chi=1$. Removing an open disc from a surface lowers $\chi$ by 1, since it deletes one face.

$$
\chi = 1-3 = -2
$$

This is a "pair of pants" with an extra leg, and it has 4 boundary circles. ∎

## Real-World Applications

**Circuit board design.** Planarity decides whether a circuit fits on one layer, and the $E\le3V-6$ bound gives a quick impossibility test before any layout is attempted.

**Mesh validation.** Graphics and CAD software computes $\chi$ from a mesh's vertex, edge and face counts to detect holes, cracks and duplicated geometry. An unexpected value means the mesh is broken.

**Molecular topology.** Counting atoms, bonds and rings gives an Euler-type relation, and it is used to determine the number of independent rings in a molecular structure.

**Meteorology.** The hairy ball theorem guarantees a point of zero horizontal wind on Earth at all times, so there is always at least one cyclonic centre.

**Antenna and sensor arrays.** Vector field constraints from Poincaré–Hopf limit how field patterns can be arranged over a closed surface, which constrains conformal antenna designs.

**Robotics and control.** Obstructions to globally continuous control laws on a configuration space are often Euler characteristic obstructions, which is why some systems provably require switching between controllers.

:::note[You've Got This]
One integer, and it does an astonishing amount of work: it identifies which surface you are on, proves there are exactly five Platonic solids, decides graph planarity, and shows you cannot comb a sphere. The invariance argument is worth understanding rather than memorizing, because it is short - refining a subdivision adds a vertex and an edge, or an edge and a face, and either way the alternating sum is untouched. Everything else in the lesson follows from that stability.
:::
