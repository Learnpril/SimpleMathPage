## What You'll Learn

In this lesson you'll define homotopy of maps, learn homotopy equivalence as a coarser relation than homeomorphism, identify contractible spaces, and see which invariants survive homotopy.

## The Concept

### Homotopy of maps

> Two continuous maps $f,g:X\to Y$ are **homotopic**, written $f\simeq g$, if there is a continuous
>
> $$
> H:X\times[0,1]\to Y, \qquad H(x,0)=f(x), \quad H(x,1)=g(x)
> $$

Think of $s\in[0,1]$ as time and $H$ as a continuous movie deforming $f$ into $g$. Continuity is required in $x$ and $s$ **jointly**, which is what stops the deformation from jumping.

Homotopy is an equivalence relation on maps: constant deformation gives reflexivity, reversing $s$ gives symmetry, and concatenating in $s$ gives transitivity.

**The hole is the obstruction**, and measuring it precisely is the next lesson's job.

### Homotopy equivalence

> $X$ and $Y$ are **homotopy equivalent** if there are maps $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to X$ with
>
> $$
> g\circ f\simeq \operatorname{id}_X, \qquad f\circ g\simeq \operatorname{id}_Y
> $$

Note the compositions need only be homotopic to the identities, not equal to them. That relaxation is what makes this strictly coarser than homeomorphism:

$$
\text{homeomorphic} \implies \text{homotopy equivalent}
$$

and the converse fails badly. A disc is homotopy equivalent to a point but not homeomorphic to it — one has uncountably many points. A solid torus is homotopy equivalent to a circle. An annulus is too.

**Homotopy equivalence forgets dimension.** That is its purpose: it keeps track only of holes and connectivity, and discards everything else.

### Contractible spaces

> $X$ is **contractible** if it is homotopy equivalent to a point, equivalently if the identity map on $X$ is homotopic to a constant map.

Contractible examples: any convex set, any star-shaped set, $\mathbb{R}^n$, a disc, a ball, a cone on anything, and a tree as a graph.

Not contractible: the circle, the sphere, the torus, the annulus, the punctured plane. In each case something cannot be shrunk away.

A useful subtlety: contractible is **not** the same as "has no holes" in any naive sense. There are contractible spaces that are hard to picture and non-contractible spaces that look simple. The circle is the cleanest non-contractible example and the standard starting point.

### Deformation retraction

The most common way to prove a homotopy equivalence in practice:

> $A\subseteq X$ is a **deformation retract** if the identity on $X$ can be continuously deformed, keeping $A$ fixed throughout, into a map whose image is $A$.

Then $X$ and $A$ are homotopy equivalent, and you have exhibited the equivalence rather than merely asserting it.

Standard instances:

- $\mathbb{R}^n$ deformation retracts to a point.
- $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ deformation retracts to the unit circle by $x\mapsto x/|x|$.
- A solid torus retracts to its core circle.
- A cylinder retracts to a central circle.
- The Möbius strip retracts to its core circle, which is why it is homotopy equivalent to a circle despite being one-sided.

That last one is instructive: **homotopy equivalence does not see orientability.** The Möbius strip and the cylinder are homotopy equivalent to each other, and telling them apart requires a finer invariant.

### What is preserved

| Invariant                  | Homeomorphism | Homotopy equivalence |
| -------------------------- | ------------- | -------------------- |
| Number of components       | yes           | yes                  |
| Fundamental group          | yes           | yes                  |
| Euler characteristic       | yes           | yes                  |
| Homology groups            | yes           | yes                  |
| Compactness                | yes           | **no**               |
| Dimension                  | yes           | **no**               |
| Orientability of a surface | yes           | **no**               |

The two "no" rows are exactly what makes homotopy equivalence useful: a disc is compact and a point is compact, but $\mathbb{R}^n$ is not compact and is still equivalent to a point. Homotopy-invariant tools are therefore easier to compute with, at the cost of resolving less.

### Homotopy of paths

Specializing to paths gives the tool for the next lesson.

> Two paths with the **same endpoints** are **path-homotopic** if one deforms into the other while both endpoints stay fixed throughout.

The endpoint condition is essential. Without it every path in a path-connected space would be homotopic to every other, and the notion would carry no information. With it, the path-homotopy classes of loops at a point form a group — the fundamental group.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Show any two maps into a convex set are homotopic.**

**Solution.** Let $f,g:X\to C$ with $C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ convex, and define

$$
H(x,s) = (1-s)f(x)+sg(x)
$$

Convexity keeps the value in $C$, and $H$ is continuous jointly since $f$ and $g$ are.

$$
f\simeq g
$$

∎

**The straight-line homotopy is the default construction**, and convexity is precisely what licenses it.

**Example 2: Show $\mathbb{R}^n$ is contractible.**

**Solution.** Take $H(x,s) = (1-s)x$. At $s=0$ this is the identity and at $s=1$ it is the constant map to the origin.

$$
\text{contractible}
$$

∎

**Example 3: Show $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ is homotopy equivalent to $S^1$.**

**Solution.** Let $r(x) = x/|x|$, which lands in $S^1$, and let $i:S^1\to\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ be the inclusion.

Then $r\circ i = \operatorname{id}_{S^1}$ exactly. For the other composite,

$$
H(x,s) = (1-s)x+s\frac{x}{|x|}
$$

never passes through 0, since it is a positive multiple of $x$ throughout, and it deforms the identity into $i\circ r$.

$$
\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}\simeq S^1
$$

∎

**Example 4: Show the annulus and the Möbius strip are homotopy equivalent.**

**Solution.** Each deformation retracts to its core circle: the annulus to its central circle, the Möbius strip to the circle traced by the midpoints of its cross-sections.

Both are therefore homotopy equivalent to $S^1$, and hence to each other. ∎

**But they are not homeomorphic**, since one has two boundary circles and one has one. Homotopy equivalence is genuinely coarser.

**Example 5: Show $S^1$ is not contractible.**

**Solution.** Suppose it were. Then its fundamental group would be trivial, since a point has trivial fundamental group and the group is a homotopy invariant.

But $\pi_1(S^1)=\mathbb{Z}$, as shown in the next lesson, so the circle is not contractible.

$$
\text{not contractible}
$$

∎

Note that this requires an invariant. **"I cannot see how to shrink it" is not a proof**, and the whole point of the next lesson is to supply the argument.

**Example 6: Show a cone is contractible.**

**Solution.** In $CX = (X\times[0,1])/(X\times\{1\})$ define

$$
H([x,t],s) = [x,\ t+s(1-t)]
$$

At $s=0$ this is the identity and at $s=1$ everything lands at the collapsed tip.

$$
\text{contractible}
$$

∎

**Every cone is contractible regardless of $X$**, which is why cones are used to kill topology deliberately.

**Example 7: Compare a solid torus with a hollow one.**

**Solution.** A **solid** torus deformation retracts to its core circle, so it is homotopy equivalent to $S^1$.

A **hollow** torus — the surface $T^2$ — is homotopy equivalent to $S^1\times S^1$, which is itself and not to a single circle. Its fundamental group is $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$ rather than $\mathbb{Z}$.

$$
\text{solid torus} \simeq S^1, \qquad T^2\not\simeq S^1
$$

∎

**Solid and hollow are very different homotopically**, which is worth being careful about since both are called a torus.

## Real-World Applications

**Path planning with obstacles.** Two routes are interchangeable if they are homotopic in the free space, so a planner only needs one representative per homotopy class. Enumerating those classes is how qualitatively different routes are found.

**Cable and wire routing.** Whether a cable can be rerouted without disconnecting equipment is a homotopy question, and homotopy classes count the genuinely distinct routings around obstacles.

**Animation and morphing.** A morph between two shapes is a homotopy, and it fails when the shapes are not homotopy equivalent — which is why morphing a sphere into a torus always shows an artefact.

**Robot arm reconfiguration.** Moving a manipulator between configurations without collision is a path in configuration space, and the homotopy class determines whether the motion wraps around an obstacle.

**Persistent homology.** Topological data analysis computes homotopy-invariant features across scales, and their invariance under deformation is exactly what makes them robust to noise in the data.

**Sensor network coverage.** Whether a region is fully covered reduces to whether the union of sensor ranges is homotopy equivalent to the region, which can be decided from connectivity data alone.

:::note[You've Got This]
Homotopy equivalence is a deliberately blunter instrument than homeomorphism, and that bluntness is the point: it forgets dimension, compactness and orientability, keeping only holes and connectivity. That makes it computable. The figure is the whole idea in one picture — the same straight-line deformation succeeds in the plane and is blocked by a single missing point. The next lesson turns that obstruction into a group, which is where the subject gets its sharpest tools.
:::
